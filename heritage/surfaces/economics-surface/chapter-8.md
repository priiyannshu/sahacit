# 8 — Why do prices move, and what can be done about it?

Everyone has felt it before they understood it. The rice your family bought last year costs more this year; the cooking oil, the bus fare, the school shoes. The experience of inflation arrives before any explanation does, which is probably why the political conversation about prices is so heavily emotional. Governments fall on inflation. Central bankers become famous or infamous depending on what happens to the price of onions. But ask *why* prices move — as a general matter, not just this week's vegetable — and the answer turns out to be more contested than the noise suggests. I think that contestedness is worth sitting with, because the disagreement over causes is not merely academic: it determines what instrument you reach for when you want to do something about it.

Let me start with a definition that matters. *Inflation* is not a single price rising. If the price of wheat doubles and the price of everything else falls proportionately, nothing inflationary has happened — that is a *relative price change*, the market adjusting the allocation of purchasing power between goods. Inflation is when the *general price level* moves: when the same basket of goods costs more rupees this year than it did last year, in a persistent and sustained way. The money has not changed; it is what the money buys that has diminished. This is why inflation is, at its root, a story about the relationship between the supply of money and the supply of goods. Too much money chasing too few goods — prices are bid up. Too little money relative to output — prices fall, which sounds desirable but brings its own problems (falling prices lead consumers to defer purchases, which reduces demand, which reduces production, which is the logic of deflation and depression).

Why, then, do prices move? Two schools, two different answers, and both are right under different conditions.

The first answer — *demand-pull* — is that inflation arises when total demand in the economy exceeds total supply. Either households and firms have more money to spend (wage rises, credit expansion, deficit financing), or supply has fallen for reasons unrelated to money (a drought destroying crops). The result is the same: the same amount of money is trying to buy fewer goods, and the price level rises. The Keynesian tradition saw this as primarily a story about aggregate demand management — fiscal policy, government spending, deficits. The monetarist school, after Milton Friedman, reduced it further: inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon, meaning that sustained general price rises require a sustained expansion of the money supply. You cannot have a wage-price spiral without someone financing the higher wages; you cannot have a demand-pull episode without someone creating the extra purchasing power.

The second answer — *cost-push* — is that inflation can be driven from the supply side, by shocks that raise the costs of production independently of what is happening to money or demand. An oil price spike makes fuel more expensive; truckers pass the cost to distributors; distributors pass it to retailers; retailers pass it to consumers. A failed monsoon reduces the harvest; food prices spike. A supply chain disruption — a port lockdown, a war disrupting commodity flows — raises input prices across industries. None of this required the RBI to print a single extra rupee. The price level rose because supply fell or input costs rose, not because households had more money to spend.

India's inflation is, by historical pattern, more cost-push than demand-pull. The two dominant cost-push drivers are food and fuel: food because the agricultural supply chain is fragmented and poorly buffered — inadequate cold storage, thin distributor networks, high perishability of the produce — and fuel because India imports close to 85 per cent of its crude oil, making domestic fuel prices hostage to whatever is happening in global commodity markets. When global crude spiked in 2021-22 following the Ukraine war, Indian petrol and diesel prices followed, and the pass-through to transport costs, fertiliser prices, and manufactured goods was swift. When the 2009 monsoon failed, onion prices spiked by over a hundred per cent in weeks — not because the RBI had been too loose, but because the crop had failed and the infrastructure to store surplus from good years did not exist.

This is the central tension I want you to feel before we go further. The primary tool the country has for managing inflation is monetary policy — the repo rate, the toolkit Chapter 3 described. Raising the repo rate makes borrowing costlier, slows credit growth, cools spending, reduces demand. Against demand-pull inflation, this works. Against a failed monsoon, it does not. Raising rates does not bring more onions to the market. This is not a failure of the central bank; it is the natural limit of what a monetary instrument can do when the problem is agronomic or geopolitical.

Before you can manage inflation, you need to measure it. India uses two indices, and the choice of which one is the *headline* measure — the number that the policy framework officially targets — has changed once within living memory, and the change reflects a developmental choice about whose price experience matters.

The **Wholesale Price Index** — the **WPI** — was India's primary inflation measure through the planning era. It tracks prices at the wholesale level: manufactured goods, raw materials, primary commodities, fuel. Its origin is colonial: the British commercial administration needed to know what was happening to the prices of traded commodities, which mattered for import-export calculations and industrial input costs. Households, their food prices, their actual cost of living — these were not what colonial measurement was designed to see. The WPI inherited this bias into independence and kept it. Through the 1990s and 2000s, policymakers watched the WPI as the headline inflation number, even as CPI inflation for urban workers was running substantially higher.

The shift came in 2014-15, formalised as part of the same *Urjit Patel Committee* process that recommended inflation targeting. The committee's case was pointed: policy should respond to the prices that *households actually pay*, not the prices producers pay for industrial inputs. A new combined **Consumer Price Index** — **CPI-C**, combining rural and urban sub-indices — became the headline measure. The basket composition tells you immediately what drives India's headline inflation: roughly 46 per cent is food and beverages, around 6 per cent fuel and light, the rest split between housing and miscellaneous. Nearly half the index is what is on the plate. A monsoon failure does not move the WPI by much; it moves the CPI-C violently. Using CPI as the policy target was a decision about *which economy* the policy was designed to protect.

Within the CPI, a further distinction operates. *Headline inflation* is the number as reported. *Core inflation* strips out food and fuel — the two most volatile components, both driven primarily by supply shocks outside monetary control — and measures the underlying price trend in the rest of the basket. A monetary policymaker looking at headline inflation during a food price spike is seeing a signal that their instrument cannot address. Core inflation is what tells them whether the monetary conditions themselves are generating inflationary pressure. The **Monetary Policy Committee** watches both, but it is core inflation — and the persistence of headline above target — that most shapes the rate decision.

Chapter 3 walked through the MPC's creation and the toolkit it operates. I want to come at the same framework here from the direction of what it is aimed at and what happens when the aim is harder than the architecture implies. The 2015 agreement between the Government of India and the RBI, formalised by a 2016 amendment to the RBI Act, committed the central bank to a single target: 4 per cent **CPI** inflation, with a tolerance band of ±2 per cent. Sustained breach — above 6 per cent or below 2 per cent for three consecutive quarters — requires a formal written explanation to the government. The choice of 4 per cent is not derived from any general theory of optimal inflation. Developed-world central banks — the Bank of England, the European Central Bank, the Federal Reserve — target 2 per cent, calibrated for mature low-inflation economies. India's *structural inflation* — the baseline rate driven by food price drift, logistics costs, and the gradual formalisation of an informal economy — had been running well above that for most of the 2000s. The Patel committee chose 4 per cent as ambitious relative to India's recent history but achievable without a growth-destroying tightening. The tolerance band acknowledges what I described above: that food and fuel shocks will push the number around regardless of what the committee decides, and the policy should not overreact to transient supply-side noise.

The MPC meets every two months. It sets the repo rate. It publishes a statement, its forecasts, and the individual votes of each member. New Zealand pioneered inflation targeting in 1989; the Bank of England adopted it in 1997. India formalised it in 2015-16, arriving late but borrowing the architecture deliberately. The committee design — three RBI insiders, three external appointees, decisions by majority vote, minutes and dissents published — was intended to address a specific risk: that a single decision-maker, however competent, was too easily captured or too easily blamed, and that accountability required named choices. A committee that publishes its minutes cannot quietly miss the target while attributing the miss to circumstances.

What the committee cannot escape is the *transmission lag*. A repo rate decision made in August shapes consumer prices in the following February or March — roughly six to twelve months later. The cut made to support growth today will be working through lending rates and borrowing decisions and household spending by the time next monsoon is already known. The committee is, unavoidably, forecasting. It sets today's rate based on where it thinks inflation will be in twelve months. The forecast is constructed from models, surveys, market data. It is better than no forecast. It is also systematically wrong when supply shocks arrive unexpectedly — which is the condition that most frequently produces above-target inflation in India.

The business cycle connects to all of this because monetary policy is the primary counter-cyclical instrument. Every market economy passes through recognisable phases: expansion, peak, contraction, trough, recovery. Why the cycle exists at all has three main answers in the literature. Keynes attributed it to swings in investment — business confidence is volatile, fixed investment follows it, and the multiplier effects amplify both the expansion and the contraction. Schumpeter attributed cycles to waves of *creative destruction* — innovation renders old capital obsolete, the transition produces unemployment and disruption, the new equilibrium eventually emerges more productive than the old. The Austrian school traced it to credit cycles: low interest rates encourage borrowing for projects that only make sense at low rates; when rates rise, the malinvestment is exposed; the correction is a recession. Each account captures something. None is complete.

India's business cycle record is unusual. A *technical recession* — two consecutive quarters of negative **GDP** growth — had not occurred from 1979 until the pandemic struck in 2020. Forty years without a formal contraction is striking, and the reasons are instructive. Agriculture, which still employs close to half the workforce, does not cycle with the credit system: a bad monsoon reduces agricultural output, but a good monsoon restores it, and neither event is correlated with global financial conditions or interest rate cycles. The agricultural sector's relative inertia smoothed the aggregate number during industrial and services contractions. Capital controls — India's restrictions on short-term portfolio flows — limited the financial contagion from the 1997 Asian crisis and the 2008 global financial crisis, both of which produced deep recessions in more financially open emerging markets. State-led demand provided a floor: governments in India have historically been willing to run larger deficits during downturns, maintaining public expenditure even at fiscal cost. And one should note, honestly, a measurement element: the informal economy — still roughly half of GDP — is estimated through periodic surveys rather than directly counted, and informal activity may have been contracting in episodes when the headline number showed otherwise.

COVID-19 ended the streak comprehensively. India's GDP contracted by over 6 per cent in 2020-21, the sharpest contraction in the records. The MPC cut rates to historic lows and held them there through 2021. The recovery was *K-shaped*: the formal sector — tech services, organised manufacturing, large retail — bounced back quickly, aided by fiscal transfers and accommodative monetary conditions; the informal sector — street vendors, migrant construction workers, small manufacturers — absorbed the first lockdown shock and recovered slowly, many permanently displaced. Then, in 2022, global supply chain disruption and the Ukraine war drove commodity prices sharply higher. Indian **CPI** breached 6 per cent — the upper limit of the tolerance band — and stayed above it for several months. The MPC raised rates by 250 basis points in under a year, the fastest tightening in the targeting era. Both the easing and the tightening were responses to supply-side shocks — a pandemic collapsing supply, a war raising commodity prices — and in both cases monetary policy was doing the only thing it could: responding to the symptom in the absence of any tool for the underlying cause.

I want to end on that observation, because it is the honest limit of everything this chapter has described. *Inflation targeting* is the most accountable, transparent framework for monetary management the world has developed since the 1990s. The MPC is a real improvement over a Governor deciding alone. The 4 per cent target is calibrated for India's specific structural conditions. The publication of minutes and votes has made monetary policy, for the first time in India's history, genuinely legible to the public. None of this can change what the framework was designed to manage and what it was not. Monetary policy can cool an overheating economy. It cannot build cold-chain infrastructure so that onion surpluses survive to the lean season. It cannot diversify the energy mix away from imported crude. It cannot make monsoons more regular or supply chains more resilient. Those are agricultural, infrastructural, and fiscal problems. The tendency to demand that the MPC solve them — to blame the central bank when food prices spike, to demand rate cuts when the issue is supply not demand — is widespread and understandable and mistaken. The instrument is precise in its setting and partial in its reach. Carrying that distinction into the chapters that follow is the most useful thing this one can leave you with.
