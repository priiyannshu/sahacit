# 10 — Why did the factory not arrive when the door was opened?

Begin with a number that should startle you. India's manufacturing sector contributes roughly seventeen per cent of GDP in 2026. It contributed somewhere between fifteen and sixteen per cent in 1991. In between, an economy liberalised, an industrial licensing regime was dismantled, foreign capital was invited in, and services boomed. The factory, somehow, did not follow. Every country that has broken out of poverty into sustained prosperity — Britain, Japan, South Korea, China — has done it through a sustained period in which manufacturing's share of the economy grew, absorbing agricultural labour at scale and creating a platform of exports, wages, and industrial linkages that then supports everything else. India liberalised at roughly the right moment, has the largest young population on earth, and still did not build the factory economy. The **National Manufacturing Policy of 2011** set a target of twenty-five per cent of GDP by 2022. The target was not met. That is the puzzle this chapter is about.

I think the place to begin is before independence, and even before the British, because the puzzle makes no sense without the substrate. India was, in the eighteenth century, not an agricultural backwater. It was one of the manufacturing centres of the world. Its *weavers* — the muslin workers of Dacca, the cotton artisans of Surat, the silk weavers of Bengal — supplied luxury and everyday textiles across the Indian Ocean world and into European markets. Mughal India's share of world industrial output before the East India Company's consolidation has been estimated at close to a quarter. It was, by some reckonings, the second-largest economy on earth.

The colonial period did not simply fail to develop this. It *actively dismantled* it. Machine-made cotton cloth from Lancashire, backed by tariff policies that kept Indian textile production unprotected in its own market, priced Dacca's weavers out of their own country by the mid-nineteenth century. No factory economy was built in their place. The railways of this period — often cited as a colonial gift — followed extraction logic, not integration logic: they ran from the interior to the port, designed to move raw materials toward British ships and finished goods back in. By 1947, as I traced in Chapter Two, the colonial inheritance was a *deliberately thinned* industrial base, not a blank slate. The founders were not starting from nothing. They were starting from a country that had once made things and had been, over two centuries, systematically prevented from making them again.

The response they chose — and I want to be clear that it was a choice made against real alternatives — was the Nehru-Mahalanobis model: state-led, capital goods first. What I want to linger on here, because Chapter Two moved quickly through it, is the specific industrial architecture this produced.

The **Industrial Policy Resolution of 1948** began the sorting. It divided industries by ownership rights in a mixed-economy frame. But it was the **Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956** that set the industrial character of the economy for the next three and a half decades. Schedule A — seventeen industrial areas — was reserved for the state alone: defence production, atomic energy, iron and steel, heavy machinery, aircraft, railways, coal, petroleum, electricity generation. No private firm could enter. The Central Public Sector Undertakings that grew out of Schedule A numbered 254 by 1991, and included the steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela, and Durgapur — Nehru's "temples of modern India" — along with the engineering research institution, the atomic energy programme, and the public-sector behemoths in banking and insurance.

The *license-permit-quota raj* was the administrative form this vision took in practice. No new factory without an industrial license. No expansion of capacity without a separate license. No import of capital goods without a permit. No foreign technical collaboration without government approval. These were not, in origin, arbitrary obstacles. They were a rationing instrument, built on wartime British machinery and elaborated into a peacetime tool for directing scarce capital toward planning priorities rather than toward whatever generated private profit. The logic was defensible in 1956. By 1975 the machinery had been captured by incumbents, who used licensing not to build industry but to block entry.

The **MRTP Act of 1969** — enacted by Indira Gandhi to restrict the concentration of industrial power in large private houses — added another layer. Firms above the MRTP limit required prior approval for expansion even in the theoretically open sectors. This, too, was intended to prevent monopoly. It succeeded, mostly, in preventing scale. The inheritance of MRTP is worth holding precisely: it was a 1969 inheritance from Indira Gandhi's political economy, finally dismantled in 2002 when the **Competition Act** replaced it with a market-oriented framework. Thirty-three years of a law that nobody who understood markets found defensible, still on the books, still shaping firm behaviour.

What the planning era did not build was competitive manufacturing at scale. Firms that emerged from the license raj had no incentive to grow beyond licensed capacity, no reason to adopt newer technologies when imports were restricted, and no experience of competing. When the door opened in 1991, they were not ready for it.

The 1991 liberalisation was, on its face, the most thoroughgoing dismantling of industrial regulation that any developing country had attempted in a single budget cycle. Industrial licensing was abolished for almost all sectors — the narrow exceptions retained being alcohol, cigarettes, hazardous chemicals, industrial explosives, electronics, aerospace, and pharmaceuticals. Public-sector reservation was narrowed to three sectors only: defence equipment, atomic energy, and railways. Foreign investment caps were loosened across most industries and fell further over the following decade. Import tariffs on capital goods were slashed from peaks of 130 per cent.

The industrial response was slow. Capacity utilisation rose as import competition forced inefficient producers to either improve or contract. Automobiles and pharmaceuticals took off through the 1990s — sectors where India had an existing technical base and the liberalisation created genuine competition. But manufacturing's share of GDP barely moved. The infrastructure gaps that the license raj era had not resolved — chronic power shortages, congested ports, poor road connectivity to manufacturing clusters — did not dissolve overnight. Labour laws remained rigid: the Industrial Disputes Act required government permission to lay off workers in factories employing more than 100 people, creating a powerful disincentive to hire at scale. Land acquisition was slow and contested. And then — this is the part that was genuinely unplanned — something entirely different arrived instead.

The services boom of the late 1990s and 2000s was not the product of an industrial policy. It was the product of an accident of history, compounded by accumulated human capital. The planning era had, through the IITs and Regional Engineering Colleges, built an engineering and scientific graduate base that the stunted manufacturing sector had never fully absorbed. When cheap international bandwidth arrived in the early 1990s and the internet followed, a labour-market arbitrage opened that nobody had designed: English-speaking, technically trained graduates in Bangalore and Hyderabad could provide software services to American corporations at a fraction of the American cost. TCS, Infosys, Wipro — companies that had existed in various smaller forms through the 1980s — became globally significant in less than a decade. The **IT and IT-enabled services** sector grew from negligible to eight per cent of GDP by the mid-2010s. The telecom revolution compounded this: Reliance's entry into mobile telephony in the early 2000s compressed decades of infrastructure development into five years, putting a phone in the pocket of three to four hundred million people and creating the platform on which internet-era services — Flipkart, Ola, Paytm, and eventually Jio's second Reliance disruption — were built.

Services are now roughly fifty-five per cent of GDP. I want to say something honest about what that means and what it does not. The productive, high-wage, export-oriented services that drive the headline number — IT, finance, telecommunications — employ perhaps five to eight per cent of the workforce. The vast remainder of services employment is domestic-facing, mostly informal, and not high-productivity: street vendors, domestic workers, construction labour, small traders. *Services-led growth does not automatically pull agricultural labour into productive employment the way manufacturing does.* The structural transformation — rural workers moving to factory jobs, acquiring skills, building the demand base that then deepens an industrial economy — has not happened in India at scale. Bangladesh moved perhaps forty million workers into garment factories in two decades; the structural consequence was a fall in the female poverty rate, a rise in the female literacy rate, a demographic transition. India's services boom did not produce anything comparable in scale. Hold the constraint: language and technical skill created an *elite* services advantage that the whole workforce cannot access. The factory might have employed anyone. The IT park employs the graduates.

Between the large organised manufacturers and the informal economy lies a sector India has never quite figured out: its micro, small, and medium enterprises. **MSMEs** employ roughly 110 million people — the second-largest employment source after agriculture — and contribute perhaps thirty per cent of GDP. They are the backbone of Indian manufacturing and a significant slice of services.

And they are, structurally, in trouble. Chronically credit-constrained, because banks find them costly to evaluate and too small to make the paperwork worthwhile. Technologically weak, because regulatory thresholds tied to firm *size* penalise growth — the jump from micro to small, or from small to medium, triggers the loss of tax exemptions, credit guarantees, and other concessions, giving firms a positive incentive to stay small even when they could expand. This is the *dwarf* problem: not stunted ambition, but rational restraint imposed by a regulatory ecosystem that treats size as suspicious. **MUDRA** — the Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency, launched in 2015 — addressed the credit side: a refinance institution that channels funds to microenterprises through banks and microfinance institutions. The MSME definition was revised upward in 2020 to raise the turnover thresholds, allowing more firms to access MSME-designated support without the old ceiling. The dwarf problem is structural, though, not definitional. Raising the label's ceiling does not, by itself, change the incentive to stay beneath it.

For roughly thirty years after 1991, "industrial policy" was a phrase the mainstream consensus associated with the license raj's failures. The prescription of the 1990s was to get the state out of the way and let comparative advantage determine what India made. India's comparative advantage, it turned out, was in services. Manufacturing was left to fend for itself in a world where China offered infrastructure, disciplined labour, and production scale that India could not match.

The mood shifted through the 2010s and turned sharply after 2020. **Make in India** (2014) was the branding moment — a government campaign aimed at attracting manufacturing investment across twenty-five sectors, with streamlined clearances and improved ease-of-business rankings as instruments. The results were uneven. Headline rankings improved. Manufacturing's GDP share did not move dramatically.

The **Production-Linked Incentive scheme** (**PLI**), launched in 2020 and extended to fourteen sectors, is a different instrument. Rather than subsidising inputs or protecting domestic markets, it pays manufacturers a percentage of *incremental* production above a base year — an attempt to replicate the East Asian developmental-state playbook (South Korea's chaebols, Taiwan's semiconductor push, Japan's MITI-era coordination) in a WTO-compliant form. The sectors chosen — mobile electronics, pharmaceuticals, medical devices, automobiles, specialty steel, textiles, food processing, telecom equipment, white goods, batteries, solar panels, and semiconductors — reflect strategic concern as much as industrial logic. The semiconductor mission (2021 onwards) represents the sharpest expression of this: fabrication plant investments at a scale India has never attempted, aimed at securing a place in the global semiconductor supply chain that the US-China decoupling has suddenly made strategically valuable.

The bet is partly geopolitical. American and European corporations trying to reduce dependence on Chinese supply chains — the *China+1* strategy — have created a window of opportunity. Whether India fills it or Vietnam, Indonesia, and Mexico do is the open industrial question of this decade. The structural constraints remain: labour laws that make large-scale factory employment risky for employers, infrastructure still weaker than East Asian competitors a generation into their manufacturing push, a skill mismatch between the education system's output and what a modern factory floor needs. The window is real. It is also narrow.

Here is the stance I want you to carry forward, into the external sector chapter that follows. Industrial policy in India is back, after thirty years of disrepute. It has returned not because the 1991 consensus was refuted, but because geopolitics has changed what comparative advantage means — and because the services boom, however impressive, did not solve the employment problem that only scale manufacturing can solve. The PLI scheme and the semiconductor mission are India's bet that this time, unlike 1956 and unlike 2014, the industrial ambition will find its infrastructure. Whether the inheritance will absorb it — the thinned colonial base, the licence-raj distortions, the services-led growth path that absorbed a generation of talent the factory might have taken — is not decided. It will be decided in factories that either get built or don't, in the years this surface cannot yet see.
