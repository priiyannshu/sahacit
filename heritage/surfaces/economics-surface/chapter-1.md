# 1 — What are we doing when we measure an economy?

Begin, perhaps, with a number you have stopped seeing. The GDP of India this quarter grew by some figure or other — six and a half per cent, let us say, or seven, or four. The newspapers will report it on a Tuesday morning; the television will set the figure in a coloured box; an analyst whose name you will not remember will explain why it was higher or lower than expected. By Wednesday afternoon the figure will be gone from your attention, replaced by the next one. You will have absorbed, without quite noticing, an idea that would have struck any ancestor of yours as very strange — that the productive activity of one and a half billion people, undertaken for one quarter of one trip of the Earth around the Sun, is meaningfully captured in a single number, and that knowing the number tells you something important about how the country is doing.

I want to slow down, in this opening chapter, on the strangeness of that idea. Not because the number is wrong — it is, in a sense, the best number we have — but because the number was *built*, by particular people, in a particular decade, for a particular purpose, and almost everything you will read in the chapters that follow will be the output of that built thing. If we do not understand the lens, every later chapter will be a story about a thing we cannot see properly.

How did people know how their country was doing, before there were national income figures? They didn't, not really. They had pieces. They had the harvest — was it good this year, or bad. They had the price of grain in the markets — high or low. They had the volume of trade across the ports, recorded by customs officials. They had the receipts of taxes, recorded by treasuries. Each of these told a story about a fragment of economic life, and the wise sovereign was the one who could hold several fragments in mind at once and form a *judgment*. There was no number that summed them, and there was no profession of summing.

This was the condition of every government, everywhere, until the nineteen-thirties. It was the condition of the British government over India, of the Mughals before them, of every kingdom and republic of which we have records. The Depression made it intolerable. Roosevelt's government, faced with a collapse it could not measure, commissioned a young economist named Simon Kuznets to invent a measure. In 1934 Kuznets handed Congress the first national income estimate. The American economy, he reported, had shrunk by half in four years.

Kuznets did not invent the *idea* that an economy had a single size. He invented the practice of *computing* that size — a method that, given the right data, would yield a number. To do that he had to make a hundred decisions about what to include. Should illegal income count? Should unpaid housework count? Should the services of a government — which has no market price, since no one pays for them in the ordinary sense — count, and if so, at what value? Each decision was contested at the time, and in some form remains contested today. Kuznets himself, in the report, warned Congress that the number should not be confused with welfare. *Goals for more growth,* he wrote, *should specify of what and for what.* The warning was ignored almost immediately.

I think this — the quiet ignoring of Kuznets's warning — is the central event in the history of how countries see themselves. The number, once invented, became the thing. Within fifteen years every major government computed it. The Second World War institutionalised the apparatus: allied governments needed to know how much output they could divert to fighting without collapsing the home economy, and Kuznets's methods were the only methods available. By 1947 the United Nations had adopted a *System of National Accounts*, a global rulebook telling countries how to compute their numbers in a comparable way. The SNA has been revised four times since — 1953, 1968, 1993, 2008 — and each revision is, beneath its technical surface, a quiet renegotiation of what counts as economic activity.

India inherited the apparatus rather than invented it. The Central Statistical Organisation was set up in 1951, the same year the First Five-Year Plan began, and was tasked with producing national income estimates by the SNA standard. Its early methodology was shaped by P. C. Mahalanobis, the same statistician whose model would later define the Second Plan's industrial strategy. This timing is not accidental, and I want you to feel it. Indian economic measurement was, from its first day, a tool of *planning* — built not to describe the economy but to allocate it. That origin still shapes what India counts well, which is the formal sector and large industry and government spending, and what it counts badly, which is the informal sector and household labour and anything that does not generate a tax receipt.

Once the lens existed, it had to be sliced. The single number "national income" turned out to be too crude for any real question, and so the apparatus produces today not one number but a family of them, each one a different cut of the same data.

Begin with **Gross Domestic Product**. GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced *within the borders* of India in a year. Two phrases in that definition do most of the work. *Within the borders* — so an Indian company's profits earned in Dubai are not GDP, but a Japanese company's profits earned in Gurgaon are. *Final* — so the wheat sold to a flour mill is not counted, only the bread sold to the consumer; otherwise the wheat would be counted twice, once as wheat and again as bread. **Gross National Product** is GDP plus what Indians earned abroad, minus what foreigners earned in India. Both are *gross*, meaning they have not subtracted the wear-and-tear on the country's machines and roads and buildings during the year. That wear-and-tear is *depreciation*, and subtracting it gives **Net Domestic Product** and **Net National Product**. NNP becomes **National Income** after removing indirect taxes (which inflate market prices without representing income to anyone) and adding subsidies. National Income then splits into **Personal Income** (what households actually receive) and **Disposable Personal Income** (what they keep after direct taxes).

The cascade is worth holding in your head, and not only because the examiner will test it. The *direction* of the cascade tells you what economists believe matters. We start at the production boundary and end at the household wallet. Every step in between — gross to net, market price to factor cost, national to personal, personal to disposable — is the country deciding which leakages are worth subtracting before the number is allowed to mean *welfare*.

Notice that all of these numbers are still measured in rupees, and rupees themselves change value. A GDP of two hundred lakh crore in 2025 is not directly comparable to fifty lakh crore in 2005, because much of the difference is just inflation — the same goods costing more rupees, not more goods being produced. To strip out inflation, statisticians compute GDP at *constant prices* — choosing a base year and pricing every later year's output as if prices had not changed. This is **real GDP**, as against the **nominal GDP** measured in current prices, and the ratio between the two is the *GDP deflator*, an inflation index drawn from the entire economy.

The choice of base year matters more than it should. India revised its base from 2004-05 to 2011-12 in 2015, and the new methodology produced higher growth numbers than the old one — which the new government welcomed and the old government's economists disputed. This, I think, is the inheritance pattern at its purest. A methodological choice, made for technical reasons by statisticians, becomes a political object the moment the numbers it produces matter to whoever is in power. Every base-year revision is a small renegotiation of what the country is allowed to claim about itself.

There is one more curiosity in the apparatus that you should know. Total production should equal total income should equal total expenditure — if the country produced one hundred rupees of stuff, then someone earned one hundred rupees making it (wages, profit, rent, interest), and someone spent one hundred rupees buying it (consumption, investment, government, net exports). The same GDP can therefore be computed three ways: by the *production method*, the *income method*, and the *expenditure method*. In an honest accounting they should agree. In India they don't quite agree, and the gap between the production and expenditure estimates is reported as a separate line item called the *statistical discrepancy*. The fact that the methods diverge is not a flaw of the system — it is the system's only honest way of saying that the economy has changed faster than the measurement can keep up. The big divergences in our recent history — demonetisation in 2016, the pandemic in 2020 — are visible precisely as moments when the three methods disagreed sharply, and economists argued about which to trust.

Now I want to turn to what the lens does not see, because this is where the political stakes of measurement become unmistakable. National income accounting was designed in 1934 to measure the formal, monetised, taxable economy of the United States. India in 2026 is not the United States in 1934. Roughly half of India's workforce is informal — street vendors, domestic workers, construction labourers, marginal farmers. A great deal of household labour, mostly women's, produces real value but is never paid. Subsistence agriculture grows food eaten before it is sold. Forest communities harvest produce that supports them without entering any market. None of this fits neatly into the SNA's categories, and the official statisticians estimate it through periodic surveys and add the estimates to GDP — but the surveys lag the reality by years.

The other things the lens cannot see I will simply name. *Environmental degradation:* a factory that produces a hundred rupees of goods while polluting a river is recorded as adding a hundred rupees to GDP, and the pollution is invisible to the accounts. *Distribution:* two countries can have identical per-capita GDP, one with a thin elite and a vast poor, the other with a broad middle, and the number is the same. *Quality of public services:* a government school is recorded at the cost of running it, not at the value of what it teaches. These are not implementation bugs that better statisticians could fix. They are consequences of the lens itself. The moment you start counting things that are not bought and sold in markets, you have to put a price on them, and the price you choose determines the answer. The lens is honest about its boundary. The dishonesty enters when politicians treat numbers from inside the boundary as if they spoke for the whole country.

The most successful attempt at a different lens came from a quarrel inside the World Bank in the late 1980s. A Pakistani economist named Mahbub ul-Haq, frustrated that international development debates kept reducing every country to its per-capita GDP, started arguing for a measure that captured what GDP missed — whether people lived long, whether they were educated, whether they had the income to choose the lives they wanted to lead. He found a partner in Amartya Sen, who had spent a decade developing what he called the *capability approach* — the idea that development should be judged not by what people own but by what they are able to *do* and to *be*.

Sen's framing came from a quiet disagreement with the entire utilitarian tradition. A starving man and a fasting saint may both be hungry, but one has chosen and one has not, and any measure that treats them identically is missing the point. What matters is the *real freedom* people have to live differently if they wish. From this premise emerged, in 1990, the first United Nations Human Development Report, with the **Human Development Index** at its centre — a composite of life expectancy at birth, mean and expected years of schooling, and gross national income per capita adjusted for purchasing power, combined into a single number between zero and one. Sen was uneasy about the index from the start. He worried that it would do to development what GDP had done to economics — flatten a contested question into a single ranking that the contest could now hide behind. His worry was vindicated. India ranks in the lower half of HDI, well below where its GDP rank alone would place it, and that gap is itself a political object: every government wants the GDP rank to count and the HDI rank to not. But the lens still does work. Where GDP would show India's last twenty years as a triumph of growth, HDI shows the same period as a more uneven story.

I should say briefly, before closing, that the chapters that follow will say very little about microeconomics — the study of how individual consumers, firms and markets behave. This is not because micro is unimportant. It is because the examiner, in a decade of question patterns, has almost never asked a direct microeconomics question. The board treats micro theory as the school subject it is and asks instead about Indian institutions, schemes and outcomes, which is macro and political economy. Two micro concepts will return in later chapters — *price elasticity* (how much demand falls when a price rises) and *market structure* (perfect competition, monopoly, oligopoly). Beyond those, micro is filed away as background.

Here, then, is the move I am asking you to hold in mind, because everything that follows depends on it. The numbers you will read in the chapters ahead — industrial production, fiscal deficit, inflation, current account, bank credit growth — are not facts in the way that the height of a mountain is a fact. They are summaries, computed by methods inherited from a 1934 invention, refined through eight decades of revision, and contested every time the answer is politically inconvenient. The numbers are still useful — they are the best lens the country has. But they were *built*, and like any built thing they bear the marks of who built them and what they were built to see.

The reader who treats GDP growth as a verdict will be misled by every quarter's release. The reader who treats it as one chosen view of a country that has many views available will read the same release more carefully, and come away closer to the truth of what changed. That readerly stance — *which lens am I being shown, who chose it, what does it leave out* — is the orientation this surface is trying to build. The chapters that follow will not pause to remind you of it. Carry it with you.
