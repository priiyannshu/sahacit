# 11 — What does it mean to be in an open economy?

Begin, if you are willing, with a plane carrying gold.

In July 1991 the Government of India arranged to airlift 47 tonnes of gold from the Reserve Bank's vaults — first to the Bank of England, then to the Union Bank of Switzerland — as collateral for emergency loans. The country had, at that moment, foreign exchange reserves sufficient for roughly two weeks of imports. The government had already stopped rolling over short-term commercial debt. Credit lines from foreign banks were drying up. The rupee was under siege. A balance of payments crisis, which sounds like an accounting problem, had become the kind of crisis where the mathematics of the external sector simply ran out and the physical object — gold, on a plane — had to move instead.

I want to start there, rather than with the definitions, because the crisis inverts the usual order. You might expect this chapter to begin with the theory — here is how an open economy works, here is the accounting — and then use 1991 as an example near the end. But the crisis is not an example of openness failing. It is the moment that *made* India's openness: what happened after the gold flew was devaluation, import liberalisation, the permitting of foreign investment, the drastic cutting of tariffs. The country did not choose to open in 1991. It was opened by its inability to stay closed any longer. Understanding the accounting is understanding what made the crisis possible and what has shaped every external-sector decision since.

A closed economy, in the theoretical extreme, interacts only with itself — it produces what it needs, borrows from its own savings, never trades across a border. No country is actually this, but the *degree* of openness is a real choice, and India's chosen degree has shifted dramatically. In the 1950s and 1960s, the licensing raj applied to the external sector with the same rigour it applied to domestic industry: every import required a permit, every foreign investment was screened, capital flows were tightly controlled. This was not irrational. Import substitution was the dominant development model of the era, endorsed by UN economists, practised from Brazil to Egypt, grounded in a genuine suspicion that world markets were designed to keep developing countries exporters of raw materials and importers of manufactures. The colonial experience — which Dadabhai Naoroji had called the drain of wealth and which this surface addressed in Chapter 2 — was recent enough to be felt, not merely referenced.

The first partial loosening came in the 1980s. Rajiv Gandhi's government deregulated selected industries and permitted limited external commercial borrowing, enough to accelerate growth without genuinely opening the economy. What it also did was build up the fiscal deficit past 8 per cent of GDP and accumulate foreign debt to fragile levels. When the First Gulf War of 1991 spiked oil prices and halted the remittances of Gulf workers simultaneously, the structure broke. The proximate trigger was external; the underlying fragility was built over a decade of managed semi-closure.

Open trade flows, it turns out, are far easier to liberalise than to reverse without crisis. The reforms of 1991 — devaluation, import liberalisation, foreign investment permission, tariff reduction from peak rates of 130 per cent to under 30 per cent — created a structure the economy has since grown into. Reversing them would mean re-imposing controls on an economy that has adapted to openness, at the cost of disrupting the capital flows, supply chains, and export markets that have formed around the open structure. This is what the inheritance frame means in the external sector: each shift constrained the shifts that came after. The 1991 opening was not a declaration of principle. It was a constraint that became a structure.

The accounting system that makes all of this trackable is the **Balance of Payments**. Every economic transaction between a resident of India and anyone in the rest of the world — buying crude oil, exporting software, receiving a wire transfer from a relative in Dubai, issuing equity to a Singaporean fund — is recorded in the BoP. The double-entry logic means the accounts must balance by definition: what flows out must be matched by what flows in, with any gap absorbed by changes in foreign exchange reserves.

Two accounts do the work. The **Current Account** records flows that do not change the country's assets or liabilities — trade in goods, trade in services, and transfer payments. Goods trade is the most visible: India's merchandise exports (petroleum products, gems and jewellery, pharmaceuticals, engineering goods) against its merchandise imports (crude oil, which dominates, then gold, electronics, and capital machinery). India consistently imports more goods than it exports — the merchandise trade deficit is structural, driven by an import bill in crude oil and gold that no policy has fully been able to compress. Services trade is where the country's personality shows differently: IT exports and business process services have made India a net exporter of services since the late 1990s, partially offsetting the goods deficit. The third leg is transfers: remittances flow in, investment income flows out. India has been the world's largest receiver of remittances for years, with annual inflows from the Gulf, the United States, and the United Kingdom running well above $80 billion — more than many countries' entire export earnings. Add the three together and you get the current account balance. India's is typically negative — a **Current Account Deficit** — meaning the country spends more with the world than the world pays it directly.

The **Capital and Financial Account** records the financing. Every purchase or sale of assets between India and the rest of the world — equity investment in an Indian company by a foreign entity, a loan raised by an Indian corporation from a foreign bank, NRI deposits, changes in foreign exchange reserves — sits here. A current account deficit is sustainable as long as the capital account generates a matching surplus: the world is, in effect, lending India money to cover the gap, against the expectation of future returns. A current account deficit that cannot attract capital account financing is a BoP crisis in the making. In 1991 the capital account dried up. The financing stopped. That is when the gold moved.

India's normal BoP picture, in the years between crises, is: persistent merchandise deficit, services surplus, large remittances, net current account deficit of 1–2 per cent of GDP in ordinary years, capital account surplus from a mix of **FDI**, **FPI**, and external commercial borrowings. Reserves accumulate in good years, draw down in stressed ones. The 2013 taper tantrum — when the US Federal Reserve signalled it would begin withdrawing its post-2008 stimulus — showed how fast the normal picture can tilt: FPI outflows accelerated, the rupee fell nearly 15 per cent against the dollar in a few months, and the current account deficit briefly widened toward 5 per cent. The buffer is always thinner than it appears until it is tested.

The exchange rate is where this pressure becomes visible. Under **Bretton Woods** — the dollar-gold standard designed at the 1944 conference in New Hampshire, where 44 allied nations set up the post-war monetary architecture — the rupee was pegged to sterling. The peg held through periodic devaluations, including a painful 36 per cent devaluation in 1966 when sterling was weak and the Indian foreign exchange position was already stressed. When the United States suspended gold convertibility in 1971, the Bretton Woods system collapsed, and India shifted to pegging against a basket of currencies, with managed adjustments. The 1991 reform ended even this: devaluation of 22 per cent in two steps in July 1991 marked the transition to a market-determined rate, formalised as a managed float in 1993. Under a managed float, the rupee finds its level through market supply and demand, but the Reserve Bank intervenes — buying dollars to prevent excessive appreciation, selling to slow depreciation — without targeting any particular level. The rupee's trajectory traces the pressure: roughly $1 = Rs 4.79 at independence, Rs 7.5 after the 1966 devaluation, Rs 17 before 1991's forced adjustment, past Rs 45 by 2000, past Rs 65 by 2016, past Rs 80 by the mid-2020s.

The question of why the RBI maintains a managed float rather than a clean float has a concrete answer. Indian corporations borrow heavily in foreign currency — external commercial borrowings are a significant component of the capital account. When the rupee depreciates sharply, the rupee cost of servicing a dollar loan rises by exactly the same proportion, and firms that had borrowed at one exchange rate find themselves servicing at a much worse one. Sudden uncushioned depreciation can push otherwise-solvent companies into distress. The RBI smooths volatility not to prevent market adjustment but to give the real economy time to adjust without a cascade of corporate defaults. This is constraint operating on choice: the degree of openness in the capital account — the corporate sector's accumulation of foreign-currency liabilities — constrains the exchange rate regime available to the central bank.

Foreign capital comes in two forms, and the distinction is central to how India has managed the external sector since 1991. **Foreign Direct Investment** is long-term equity in real assets — a manufacturing plant, a data centre, a distribution network. It brings capital, often technology, sometimes management capacity, and it stays: you cannot dismantle a factory and move it to Vietnam at the click of a button. **Foreign Portfolio Investment** is purchases of listed shares and bonds by foreign entities — financial flows that can, and do, reverse at speed. When global risk appetite falls — a rate hike by the US Federal Reserve, a banking shock in the developed world, a geopolitical escalation — FPI leaves first, pressuring the currency and drawing down reserves. The post-1991 design welcomed both, but differentiated them. FDI enters through either an automatic route (most sectors, no government approval) or a government route (sensitive sectors — defence, retail, insurance, certain media — where the state retains approval rights). Sector caps limit foreign equity in some industries. FPI operates through registered foreign portfolio investors, with concentration limits on individual company holdings. The logic is legible: FDI builds capacity, FPI adds market depth and also market volatility. The country wanted the depth more than it feared the volatility, but it designed guardrails.

The international institutional architecture India operates within was designed before India was independent. The Bretton Woods Conference of 1944 produced two institutions: the **IMF**, tasked with providing emergency BoP financing to member countries experiencing external crises — with conditions attached to restore balance — and the **World Bank**, structured to provide longer-term development lending. India was a founding member of both. The relationship with each was defined in 1991: India approached both institutions that year, accepted the IMF's conditionalities, and received the financing. The country that had been a founding shareholder in 1944 arrived at the window as a client forty-seven years later.

The **WTO** is a different inheritance. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade dated from 1947, and the WTO replaced it in 1995 after the Uruguay Round — a set of new rules extending beyond goods to cover services (GATS), intellectual property (TRIPS), and agriculture (Agreement on Agriculture). India was a founding WTO member, which means it operates inside a rule-set it helped design, though from a position of much less negotiating power than the United States or the European Union who were the Uruguay Round's dominant voices. The agriculture rules — limiting domestic support and export subsidies — pressed hardest on developing countries with food security programmes built on price support. India's response has been consistent: push back from inside the framework rather than exit it. At the 2013 Bali Ministerial Conference, India blocked the Trade Facilitation Agreement until developed countries accepted that procurement programmes at Minimum Support Price would not be counted as trade-distorting subsidies beyond agreed limits. The block was controversial; the domestic logic was clear; the resolution was an interim peace clause that remains unfinished. This is what it looks like to shape rules rather than only follow them — you hold leverage at a specific moment and use it for a specific exemption, then continue operating inside the system.

The 2010s brought new institutions where India was a founder rather than an inheritor. The **New Development Bank** — the BRICS Bank — was established in 2014 by Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa. The **Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank** was initiated by China, with India as one of its largest non-Chinese founding shareholders, becoming operational in 2016. Neither institution approaches the IMF or World Bank in capitalisation — their scale is a fraction of the Bretton Woods institutions'. What they represent is a preference for having a second set of options: development finance whose conditionalities India participates in designing, infrastructure lending whose governance structures include India's voice from the founding moment. The direction of travel — from client at the IMF window in 1991 to co-founder of the NDB in 2014 — is real. It is also partial, contested at every multilateral negotiation, and far from the story of a country that has arrived at the table of rule-setters.

I should flag the current-affairs territory this chapter leaves at the door. Recent FTA negotiations — with the United Kingdom, the European Union, and Australia — involve specific sectoral trade-offs that belong to the current-affairs layer. The latest BoP numbers, the current rupee and reserve dynamics, and the outcomes of recent WTO ministerials are moving targets that the canonical sources here cannot track. This chapter has tried to set the structural ground on which those numbers land.

The stance I want you to carry forward is this: openness is not a value, and it is not a policy. It is a constellation of choices about which flows to permit, at what speed, with what safeguards, accumulated over decades of crises and adjustments. India's external sector today is the residue of every BoP-stressed year it has survived: the drain of wealth that colonialism extracted, the import substitution that the planning era chose, the forced opening of 1991, the FPI volatility of 2013. Read any trade data release, any exchange rate bulletin, any FDI figure — and the question worth asking is not "is this good or bad" but "which constraint is this the response to, and what does it preclude next." The reader who carries that question into the final chapter will be better placed to think about what the external sector is ultimately for.
