# 4 — What does it mean to have a banking system?

Begin with something that looks simple and is not. You deposit a thousand rupees in a bank. You can withdraw them tomorrow if you want to, or never, or in twenty years — the bank has promised, and you trust the promise. Across town, a small manufacturer borrows from the same bank to buy machinery he will take five years to pay off. The bank has lent the same money for five years that it promised you on demand. This is not a trick of accounting. It is the trick that banking *is*.

Economists call it *maturity transformation*, and its operating mechanism is *fractional reserve banking*. The bank holds a fraction of deposits in reserve and lends the rest. It can do this because it bets — correctly, most of the time — that not all depositors will want their money on the same day. The bet is well-grounded: depositors' withdrawal decisions are largely uncorrelated, and a large, diversified pool makes the math work comfortably. But the bet is never certain. If depositors collectively decide they all want their money at once — triggered by rumour, crisis, or genuine insolvency — the bank fails regardless of whether its underlying loans are sound. This is a *bank run*, and it is why banking has always required an external guarantee. In modern systems that guarantee comes from the central bank as *lender of last resort* — lending to banks facing short-term runs on long-term sound assets — and from explicit deposit insurance, which removes the individual depositor's incentive to run at the first sign of trouble.

I want you to sit with this for a moment, because the entire regulatory architecture of banking — every capital adequacy norm, every liquidity ratio, every annual stress test — is an answer to this one underlying problem. The bank's business model is useful precisely because it is fragile. Society gets the transformation of short-term savings into long-term investment; the price of that transformation is a system that can collapse in an afternoon if trust breaks. The regulator's job is to make collapse unlikely enough that the transformation is worth offering.

The Indian banking system that inherited this logic is not a single entity. It is several overlapping systems, built at different historical moments, regulated with different degrees of coherence, and still not quite stitched into a deliberate whole.

The largest layer is the *scheduled commercial banks* — institutions listed in the Second Schedule to the Reserve Bank of India Act, entitling them to central bank facilities and obligating them to RBI oversight. Within this layer, the most important distinction is public versus private. Public sector banks — those in which the government holds a majority stake — still account for roughly sixty per cent of both deposits and credit in India, even after three decades of licensed private banks. Behind that number sits a specific inheritance: the nationalisations of 1969 and 1980, which I will come to in a moment.

Below the scheduled commercial banks lies a second layer: the *cooperative banks*. Urban cooperative banks and rural cooperative credit institutions — Primary Agricultural Credit Societies, District Central Cooperative Banks, State Cooperative Banks — serve millions of depositors and borrowers, especially in Maharashtra and Gujarat. But they operate under a peculiar dual regulation. The RBI regulates their banking functions; state Registrars of Cooperatives regulate their management and governance. This split has produced, for decades, a system where neither regulator has complete authority. The governance failures it enables — political lending, weak auditing, occasional fraud — led finally to the Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020, which gave the RBI more direct oversight. Even so, cooperative banking remains the Raj-era inheritance the country has most incompletely resolved, and the RBI's ongoing concerns about governance in this segment belong to the current-affairs layer rather than to a structural chapter.

The third layer is the *Regional Rural Banks*, established on 2 October 1975 — five initially, growing to 196 by 1987 before amalgamation reduced them. RRBs were a deliberate compromise: government-mandated institutions, jointly capitalised by the central government (50 per cent), the sponsoring nationalised bank (35 per cent), and the relevant state (15 per cent), tasked with reaching credit to the rural poor at rates a purely commercial bank would never offer. By the early 1980s, most were losing money. The Bhandari and Basu Committees of the mid-1990s forced commercialisation — concessional rates abolished, lending targets widened — which stabilised their finances but narrowed the original purpose. There are today 56 RRBs functioning across India after multiple rounds of amalgamation.

Since 2015, two newer categories have added to the structure: *payments banks*, which can accept deposits and offer remittances but cannot lend, and *small finance banks*, which are licensed to lend but focused on small borrowers and the underserved. The distinction matters. Payments banks — Airtel, Jio, India Post — came from mobile and logistics operators; they brought reach and technology rather than credit expertise. Small finance banks came largely from microfinance institutions converting under a 2015 RBI licensing round. The result is a tiered structure that was not designed as a whole — each tier was built for a different problem, at a different moment, and the architecture is additive rather than coherent.

The structure is, at its core, a 1969 inheritance. To understand why public sector banks still dominate Indian banking more than fifty years later, you have to understand the choice that was made then and what it was trying to solve.

Before 1969, Indian banking was substantially private — dominated by families and industrial groups that lent heavily to their own interests. Agriculture was poorly served. Small businesses had little formal credit. The rural poor depended on moneylenders at rates that often exceeded 30 per cent. The political case against this system had been building since independence. In July 1969, *Indira Gandhi* used the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance to nationalise fourteen private banks with deposits above fifty crore rupees. Six more followed in April 1980. The stated reasons were that banks were serving commerce and industry at the expense of agriculture and the weaker sections, that credit was geographically concentrated, that private ownership of large banks was inconsistent with a socialist republic's developmental priorities.

The choice between nationalisation and stricter regulation is worth staying on, because the alternative existed. Indira Gandhi could have imposed directed lending mandates, capital adequacy requirements, and branch-opening obligations without transferring ownership — she could have regulated the private banks into the shape she wanted. She chose not to. The reasons were part ideological, part political. The 1969 nationalisation came at a moment of Congress split and sharp political polarisation; the move had enormous popular support and gave Gandhi a defining advantage in the rupture with the Congress Old Guard. Nationalisation was also faster and more visible than complex regulatory reform. Taking ownership gave the government direct control over lending priorities — *priority sector lending* targets, rural branch expansion, agricultural credit at directed rates — without the ongoing negotiation that would have been required if the banks remained private.

What the nationalisation solved, I think, was the access problem. In the three decades after 1969, bank branches spread into rural India in a way they never had before. Agricultural credit grew. Financial inclusion, however incomplete, reached populations that private banks would never have served on commercial terms. What it created was a governance problem. Bank boards became appointments in a political ecosystem. Lending decisions were influenced by proximity to power. The distinction between bad luck and bad governance became politically inconvenient to draw. The 1969 inheritance is not a simple story of government failure. It is a genuine trade-off, and six decades later both sides of it remain visible in every balance sheet of every public sector bank.

A loan becomes a **non-performing asset** once it has not been serviced — interest or principal — for ninety days. India adopted this 90-day norm in 2004, aligning with international practice. The adoption itself was diagnostic: before 2004, banks could reclassify bad loans and defer recognition. The resulting distress accumulated quietly through the 2000s and erupted around 2012-15, when the RBI's Asset Quality Review forced banks to stop *evergreening* — rolling over bad loans to avoid recognising loss — and acknowledge NPA levels that had been papered over for years.

The sectors responsible were specific. After the 2008-09 global financial crisis, the government pushed public sector banks to lend heavily to infrastructure projects — power plants, roads, telecom, steel — that required land acquisition and environmental clearances that did not come on schedule. By 2014, the Economic Survey estimated that five sub-sectors — infrastructure, iron and steel, textiles, mining and coal, aviation — held 54 per cent of all stressed advances of public sector banks. These were not consumer loans or small agricultural credits. They were large corporate bets made at the top of a growth cycle that turned sour, in sectors that the political economy had made it difficult to walk away from.

The resolution machinery was inadequate for the scale of the problem. The **SARFAESI Act, 2002** — Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest — gave banks the power to seize and sell collateral without going to court. This was significant: before it, a borrower could obtain a civil court stay order and frustrate recovery for years. SARFAESI broke that logic for secured creditors. But it worked only where there was identifiable, seizable collateral — not for large corporate borrowers with complex capital structures and assets tied up in ongoing projects. *Asset Reconstruction Companies* (ARCs), established under SARFAESI to acquire bad loans at a discount and attempt recovery, were undercapitalised and too small to absorb the volume of distress the post-2012 cycle generated. A proposed National Asset Reconstruction Company (NARCL) to consolidate this function at scale belongs to the current-affairs layer rather than to this structural account. *Debt Recovery Tribunals*, set up in 1993, were clogged and slow. The *Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction* (BIFR) — inherited from the Sick Industrial Companies Act, 1985 — had become a shelter for promoters: BIFR registration froze all creditor action, and promoters used it as a shield rather than a genuine restructuring tool.

The structural solution was the **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016**. I think the IBC is the single most significant creditor-rights reform in India's legal history, and the reason is not procedural. It reversed a principle that had been embedded in Indian commercial law for seventy years. Before the IBC, the system was substantially *debtor-friendly*: the promoter of a failing company retained control, creditors waited in a queue, and resolution could stretch over a decade. The IBC made *creditors-in-charge*: once a firm is admitted for insolvency, a resolution professional takes over management, the National Company Law Tribunal oversees a time-bound 180-day process (extendable to 270 days), and if no resolution plan emerges, liquidation follows. The promoter who caused the failure cannot bid to buy the company back — a provision that closed the most obvious loophole. Resolution rates, haircuts, and case backlogs at the NCLT remain live debates, and they belong to the current-affairs layer. But the expectation shift is the structural achievement: promoters who knew they could frustrate creditors for a decade now know they can lose control in months. That change in the default outcome — not the case statistics — is what the IBC was for.

The NPA crisis produced a second response alongside legal reform: consolidation of the public sector banks themselves. In 2017, the five associate banks of the State Bank of India were merged into SBI. In 2019, a larger wave of mergers reduced public sector banks from 27 to 12 — combining Oriental Bank of Commerce and United Bank into Punjab National Bank, Syndicate Bank into Canara Bank, Andhra Bank and Corporation Bank into Union Bank of India, and several other pairings. The stated rationale was that larger banks would have broader capital bases, stronger governance, and greater capacity to absorb shocks. The Banks Board Bureau, set up in 2016 to professionalise board appointments, was reconstituted as the Financial Services Institutions Bureau (FSIB) in 2022.

The persistent question, posed by every reform committee since the Narasimham Committee II in 1998 and answered by every government the same way — not yet — is whether the public sector should continue to dominate Indian banking at all. Privatisation of even one large public sector bank would be politically costly; recapitalisation through budget transfers when their capital erodes is fiscally costly; and the status quo, as usual, wins by default.

I want to close this chapter honestly about what the banking system can and cannot do, because the limits matter for everything that follows. Banks can do a great deal: they are India's primary channel for converting household deposits into loans, for reaching agricultural credit, for funding small business. What they cannot easily do is supply *long-term risk capital* — the equity and long-tenor debt that large infrastructure and manufacturing projects need, that cannot prudently be funded by deposits repayable on demand. Trying to do so — which the post-2008 infrastructure lending wave was essentially an attempt at — produces exactly the asset-liability mismatch that shows up, a few years later, as NPAs. A banking system is the right tool for intermediating short and medium-term credit. It is the wrong tool for bearing the long-tenor, high-uncertainty risk that capital markets exist to price. The country's banking system has been asked, repeatedly, to be both things at once — and the NPA cycle is what that mistake looks like in the accounts. Which is why Chapter 5, the capital markets chapter, is not a separate subject. It is the other half of an unresolved question.
