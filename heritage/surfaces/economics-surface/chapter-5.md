# 5 — What is a capital market for?

I want to begin with a limit, because the chapter is really about where one institution ends and another begins. The bank, which we looked at in the last chapter, does a specific thing: it takes money from depositors who may want it back tomorrow, and lends it to borrowers who promise to repay it, with interest, on a schedule. That basic transaction — borrow short, lend long — works for a great many purposes. A trader who needs three months of working capital. A family buying a house over twenty years. A company expanding its factory over five. The bank can absorb these because it has many depositors whose withdrawal demands don't all arrive at once, and it can price the risk into the interest rate it charges. This is a genuinely powerful system and it runs a large share of the world's economic activity.

But now think about an infrastructure project: a road, or a port, or a power plant that will take fifteen years to build and then generate returns for forty years after that. Or think about a startup — a company with an idea, no collateral, no track record, and a chance, perhaps, of becoming something significant, or perhaps of becoming nothing. Or think about a hospital that needs thirty-year financing to build capacity it will fill slowly. In each of these cases, the logic of the bank breaks down. Depositors' money cannot be committed for forty years. A fixed interest obligation cannot be sustained through a decade of losses. The risk is not the kind you can price into a spread and move on. These are fundamentally different financing problems, and a different mechanism evolved to solve them.

That mechanism is the capital market, and I think the clearest way to understand it is that capital markets exist *where banking ends.* Instead of a bank standing between the saver and the borrower — managing the risk, guaranteeing the deposit — capital markets let the saver and the risk-taker find each other directly. The investor holds the risk. The investor earns the return. There is no guarantee of getting the money back, because the investor has not lent money — they have, in many cases, *bought a share in the venture*.

This distinction between *debt* and *equity* is the first thing the chapter is about, and it matters more than it looks. If you borrow money to build something, you owe a specific amount by a specific date, and if you cannot pay, the lender has a claim on your assets. That is *debt:* impatient capital, with an obligation attached. If you sell ownership stakes in your company, you have no obligation to repay anything. The investors share your future profits, but they cannot demand repayment if the company underperforms, and they cannot foreclose if the industry turns. That is *equity:* patient capital, willing to wait for the venture to deliver. Each carries a different risk-return profile, a different governance structure — equity holders vote, debt holders don't — and a different claim on the company's future income. A company's choice between debt and equity is, in the end, a choice about how much risk it wants to share and how much control it is willing to relinquish.

Hold this in mind, because everything about the Indian capital market can be read as a long argument about how to balance these two modes of financing, and who should have access to which.

India inherited its capital markets, like most of its financial architecture, in layers — the oldest from colonial trade, the modern from a crisis, the recent from imports. The **Bombay Stock Exchange**, today the oldest stock exchange in Asia, began in 1875 in the most informal way imaginable: a group of brokers, four initially and then more, meeting under a banyan tree on what is now Dalal Street. They were trading primarily in shares of companies connected to the cotton trade, which had boomed briefly during the American Civil War when the Union blockade made Indian cotton suddenly precious to British mills. The cotton boom collapsed, but the practice of trading company shares did not. The Native Share and Stock Brokers' Association, as it was formally constituted, grew in the decades that followed, handling shares of the large British managing agencies that dominated Indian commerce. When independence arrived and the new government set about building public-sector enterprises and licensing private industry, the BSE already existed — and it became the primary market through which the new industrialists of independent India raised and traded equity. The inheritance here is direct: the BSE was not built to serve a planned economy, but a planned economy used it anyway, partly because there was nothing else.

For decades, the BSE was the market. Regional exchanges existed in Calcutta, Madras, Ahmedabad, and elsewhere, but they were thin and provincial. The BSE was where prices were set. And the BSE in that era was a club — a cartel of brokers who operated by open-outcry trading, kept their methods opaque, resisted outside scrutiny, and profited from the gap between what buyers paid and sellers received. This was not a minor inefficiency. It was the structure of the market, inherited from the colonial trading-house system and perpetuated by the license raj's restriction of capital movement. The firms that wanted equity financing had few options; the investors who wanted market access had few options; and the brokers in the middle held the information advantage.

That structure was broken by a crisis, not by reform. In 1992, a stockbroker named **Harshad Mehta** orchestrated what is still the most significant financial fraud in Indian market history. He exploited a gap in the settlement system — the fact that share transactions were settled with physical paper certificates, creating a lag between trade and delivery — to funnel money from bank accounts into the stock market, pushing up prices in specific scrips and profiting from the artificial rises. When the fraud was exposed, the market collapsed. Investors lost enormously. The settlement system's paper-based vulnerability was exposed. And the regulatory body that was supposed to oversee the market, the **Securities and Exchange Board of India**, had existed since 1988 in a purely advisory capacity, with no statutory power to compel, investigate, or punish.

The government's response was shaped by the crisis rather than by ideology — and this is exactly the kind of forced inheritance that recurs through India's financial architecture. Within months, SEBI was given statutory teeth through the SEBI Act of 1992. A new exchange — the **National Stock Exchange** — was launched in 1992, deliberately electronic and deliberately transparent, promoted by financial institutions including IDBI, as a competitor to the BSE's opacity. Electronic trading stripped the information advantage from the broker community; a national satellite link meant that a trader in Ahmedabad could access the same prices as one in Bombay. The BSE, facing competition, was forced to modernise. Both exchanges now run electronic order-matching systems; the physical trading floor is a museum exhibit.

The paper certificate problem required its own solution. Two depositories were created to dematerialise shares — to convert physical certificates into electronic records held centrally. **NSDL**, the National Securities Depository Limited, opened in 1996. **CDSL**, the Central Depository Securities Limited, followed in 1999. The two compete; together they hold the electronic record of virtually every share held by every investor in India. The word "dematerialisation" sounds technical, but the practical effect was profound: once shares existed as electronic records rather than paper, settlement became faster, fraud became harder, and small investors could hold fractional positions without a filing cabinet full of documents. The entire modern retail equity market — every SIP, every app-based brokerage, every mutual fund — runs on the infrastructure that the Harshad Mehta crisis forced into existence.

I want to say something clearly about what SEBI does, because it appears in enough questions to deserve precision. SEBI's mandate under the 1992 Act is threefold: to protect the interests of investors in securities, to promote the development of the securities market, and to regulate the market. In practice this means SEBI regulates what companies must disclose before raising public capital through an IPO; it regulates the intermediaries — brokers, depositories, merchant bankers, mutual funds, portfolio managers; and it surveils the market for insider trading, price manipulation, and fraud. It does not regulate banks (that is RBI), it does not regulate insurance (that is IRDAI), and it does not regulate commodity derivatives (that moved from the Forward Markets Commission to SEBI in 2015, bringing all derivatives under one regulator). The deliberate separation of SEBI from RBI was a constraint-and-choice decision worth noticing: the regulator of bank credit should not also regulate the equity market, which competes with bank deposits for the same household savings. The conflict of interest was considered too direct.

Now to the instruments themselves, because the market is not only about equity shares, and the examiner tests the vocabulary. *Equity shares* are the standard ownership claim — holders vote, holders receive dividends, holders are the last in line when a company winds up. *Preference shares* are a hybrid: holders receive a fixed dividend ahead of equity shareholders, but typically cannot vote. *Debentures and bonds* are debt instruments — the company borrows from the instrument holder at a fixed coupon, with repayment at maturity. *Commercial paper* is short-term debt, typically used by large companies for working capital, sold in tranches to institutional investors. All of these have existed in the Indian market for decades.

The more recent inventions are worth naming specifically because they represent designed inheritances from foreign models. **REITs** — Real Estate Investment Trusts — were imported from US and Singapore structures and formally enabled in India by SEBI in 2014, with the first REIT listed in 2019. They allow retail investors to hold fractional ownership of commercial real estate portfolios — office buildings, malls, warehouses — without having to buy an entire property. **InvITs** — Infrastructure Investment Trusts — are the equivalent for infrastructure assets: toll roads, power transmission lines, pipelines. The logic is the same: pool large, illiquid assets into a listed vehicle so smaller investors can access the income stream. Both were borrowed instruments, adapted to Indian regulatory context, addressing a specific gap: infrastructure projects need patient capital, but the people with patient capital are mostly small households, who individually cannot invest in a highway. **ETFs** — exchange-traded funds — allow investors to buy a basket of securities tracking an index, with low fees, traded on the exchange like a share. **AIFs** — Alternative Investment Funds — are a catch-all category for hedge funds, private equity, and venture capital, regulated by SEBI but designed for sophisticated investors.

The instrument that has most transformed the market in the last decade is not on this list, because it is not an instrument — it is a *mechanism*. The Systematic Investment Plan, or **SIP**, allows a mutual fund investor to automate a fixed monthly contribution — five hundred rupees, a thousand rupees, whatever — into a fund. The fund aggregates contributions from millions of such investors and deploys the capital into the market. SIPs were not invented in 2015; they existed before. But three things together created the post-2015 retail boom: digital onboarding enabled by Aadhaar-based KYC, which made opening a mutual fund account a matter of minutes rather than days; the AMFI advertising campaign whose tagline — *mutual funds sahi hai* — is now genuinely embedded in the popular vocabulary; and the zero-commission direct plan structure that the regulator mandated in 2013, which made funds cheaper for long-term investors. Monthly SIP contributions, which were under ten thousand crore rupees in 2016, crossed twenty-five thousand crore by the early 2020s. This is a large-scale structural shift in how Indian households park their savings.

*Derivatives* deserve a separate mention because they sit in the middle of every current regulatory debate. A derivative is an instrument whose value is *derived from* an underlying asset — a share, an index, a commodity, a foreign currency. A futures contract commits two parties to trade the asset at a specified price on a specified future date. An options contract gives one party the right, but not the obligation, to trade at the specified price. Derivatives were introduced on Indian exchanges in 2000, starting with index futures on SEBI's guidance. They were intended to serve two legitimate purposes: *hedging* (a company that fears a rupee depreciation can lock in today's exchange rate through a currency future), and *price discovery* (futures prices aggregate trader expectations about future prices and carry real information). Both purposes are genuine. The persistent problem is that the mechanism also attracts retail speculation — traders who have no underlying exposure to hedge, who are simply betting on short-term price movements, with leverage, and losing. Recent studies by SEBI itself showed that a very large share of retail F&O traders lose money, most of them repeatedly. The regulator tightened F&O entry conditions and contract structures in 2024. The trade-off between price discovery plus hedging benefits on one side and retail-loss concerns on the other has not been resolved; the tightening is the most recent position in an ongoing negotiation.

Why have both NSE and BSE rather than a single national exchange? The answer is the same logic that gave us two depositories: competition forces innovation. NSE's arrival in 1992 pushed the BSE to modernise in ways it had resisted for a century. Having two exchanges with competing technology platforms has kept settlement speeds and trading costs lower than a monopolist would have allowed. The same logic is behind the **SME exchanges** — the BSE SME platform and NSE Emerge — created to allow smaller companies that cannot meet the main board's listing thresholds to raise equity. Whether SME exchanges have succeeded in democratising capital access or have instead created a thin market vulnerable to manipulation is the current-affairs question I won't resolve here; the structural intent was to extend capital market access down the size distribution.

I should flag clearly where this chapter ends and the current-affairs layer begins. Specific SEBI regulations passed after 2023, the detailed evolution of F&O restrictions, the algo-trading regulation framework, and the activity in the IPO market in any given year all belong in the current-affairs compilation, not here. This chapter gives you the permanent skeleton: what the market is for, how the key institutions came to be, and what the main instruments are. When a SEBI regulation appears in a news headline, you now have the structure it sits within.

The closing question is the one the chapter has been building toward. For most of independent India's history, the household answer to *where do I put my savings* was a bank deposit — low return, low risk, guaranteed up to a limit by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation. The alternative — equities and mutual funds — was available but required documentation, a physical broker relationship, and a tolerance for volatility that most households, most of whom had never seen a stock chart, reasonably lacked. The shift happening now, driven by SIPs and digital onboarding and two decades of broadly rising markets, is a shift from banking to capital markets as the primary savings destination for the middle class. This matters in two ways. First, it makes more risk capital available for productive ventures — the infrastructure, the startups, the hospitals that the banking system structurally could not finance. Second, it means that crores of households now have direct exposure to market volatility, with limited financial literacy to understand what they have bought or how to respond when prices fall. Both consequences are real. The country is in the middle of a slow, large-scale renegotiation of its relationship with financial risk, and the consequences will not be clear until it is well underway.
