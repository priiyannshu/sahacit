# 7 — Why does the country tax the way it does?

Before there was a state in any modern sense, there was a tax collector. I think this ordering matters. We tend to imagine taxation as something the state imposes on society from above — a power that arrives with government and can be withdrawn if government changes. But historically the flow has been reversed. The state did not invent taxation; it *organised* what had always been extracted. And nowhere is this more visible than in India, where the fiscal architecture of the modern republic can be traced, in almost unbroken line, through the colonial revenue system, through the Mughal experiments in land measurement, and down to the agrarian settlements of village chiefs and headmen who were settling accounts with whoever held military power in their region. The tax is older than the state. The state is, in a real sense, what happened when the tax system became powerful enough to require a bureaucracy.

Begin with land, because that is where almost all pre-modern taxation began. The Mughals were not, in their fiscal ambitions, unique. Every large agrarian empire taxed the land, because the land was where the value was — where the grain grew, where the peasant worked, where the harvest could be measured and a share extracted. What was unusual about Akbar's administration is that it *measured*. The *zabti* system, designed and administered by **Todar Mal**, Akbar's revenue minister, conducted systematic surveys of agricultural land — classifying fields by quality, estimating average yields over a decade, fixing tax demand in cash rather than grain. This was not just fiscal efficiency. It was the beginning of a claim the modern state would inherit: the right to know the land, to categorise it, to assign it a number, and to extract from that number. Todar Mal's surveys in the late sixteenth century were the ancestor of every settlement record in colonial India, and the colonial settlement records are the ancestors of the land registration system you can still look up in district offices today.

The British did not continue the Mughal approach. They *chose* something different — a choice with two centuries of consequences. The **Permanent Settlement** of 1793, designed by Cornwallis, fixed the land revenue demand between the colonial state and the zamindars of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa in perpetuity. Fixed in cash. Fixed forever, or meant to be. The logic was partly administrative (predictable revenue for the Company's treasury, without the cost of conducting periodic surveys) and partly ideological (the zamindars, treated as landowners in the English sense, would invest and improve, like English landlords). What it actually produced was a class of rentier intermediaries with no incentive to invest, a peasantry with no secure tenure, and an agrarian system that was still shaping rural Bengal's political economy when the republic inherited it in 1947. The Ryotwari and Mahalwari settlements in the south and north respectively — which dealt directly with cultivators rather than intermediaries — produced their own pathologies, but they at least kept the state in direct fiscal contact with the land. The central point I want you to hold is that the British, by choosing landlord-mediated collection in the most populous part of the country, created a template for fiscal extraction that kept the actual cultivating population at arm's length from the revenue system. That arm's length would have consequences for everything that came after.

The shift that really defines modern taxation — the shift that makes it modern — is the move away from land as the primary tax base. In 1860, the colonial government faced a fiscal emergency. The suppression of the 1857 uprising had been enormously expensive. The new government in London needed to rebuild its finances. **James Wilson**, the first Finance Member of the Viceroy's Council, introduced the income tax. Not a tax on what you grew or owned, but on what you *earned* — a direct claim on the flow of income rather than the stock of property. It is the fiscal equivalent of the shift from feudal tenure to wage labour. The income tax was introduced as a five-year emergency measure; it became permanent in 1886, though it remained marginal — applicable only to a thin urban and commercial elite — until well into the twentieth century. The inheritance independent India received was therefore a tax system still dominated by customs and excise duties, with an income tax that existed but reached almost nobody.

This brings me to the distinction the examiner will always test, and which deserves a moment's explanation rather than a definition. **Direct taxes** are levied on the person or entity that bears the burden — the income tax you pay is borne by you, on your income. **Indirect taxes** are levied at an earlier point in the supply chain — on the manufacturer, the importer, the trader — but are passed forward in the form of higher prices. The consumer bleeds, not the producer. Ramesh Singh's formulation is sharp: in a direct tax, the person who is hit is the same person who bleeds; in an indirect tax, the person who is hit does not bleed, someone else does.

The choice between leaning on direct or indirect taxation is partly technical and partly political. Technically, direct taxes are harder to collect. They require income declarations, audit capacity, and a population of identifiable taxpayers with formal incomes. In an economy where large fractions of economic activity are informal — where the farmer sells grain without an invoice, where the carpenter works without a contract, where the street vendor transacts entirely in cash — there is simply no income to declare and no way to verify it. Indirect taxes are technically cheaper to administer because they are collected at fewer points of production and sale, even if they ultimately reach every consumer through prices. Politically, indirect taxes have a different quality: they are less *visible*. The person who pays income tax is conscious of paying it; the person who pays a customs duty embedded in the price of a mobile phone is not thinking about the duty at all. Every government in every democracy discovers that the taxes people can see are harder to raise than the taxes people cannot feel. India's heavy reliance on indirect taxation — which has historically accounted for roughly 55 per cent of total tax revenue — is a straightforward consequence of these two pressures operating together.

The narrow base of direct taxation is a real puzzle, and I think it is worth sitting with rather than skating past. Only about 6 per cent of the population pays personal income tax — a figure that sounds scandalously low until you remember that a large share of the workforce is in agriculture (exempt by historical convention) or in informal employment. Within that 6 per cent, the top sliver — perhaps 1–2 per cent of taxpayers — pays the overwhelming majority of personal income tax collected. The corporate tax base is broader in some sense, but the 2019 decision to cut corporate tax rates significantly — from 30 per cent to 22 per cent for existing companies, and to 15 per cent for new manufacturing companies — had a meaningful fiscal cost, estimated at over Rs. 1.45 lakh crore per year. The stated logic was to compete with East Asian economies — Vietnam, Thailand, Bangladesh — for manufacturing investment. The benefit, in terms of actual investment materialising, has been mixed and is still being assessed. The 2020 personal income tax revision introduced a dual-regime: a *new regime* with lower rates but no deductions, and an *old regime* with higher rates but full deductions. The coexistence of two parallel regimes for the same tax is itself a signal that the government has not decided what it wants the personal income tax system to be.

Now I want to turn to what I think of as the constitutional moment of Indian indirect taxation — the Goods and Services Tax. The idea of a single national market in goods is as old as the constitution itself. But from the 1950s to the 2010s, India's indirect tax system was a layered, fragmented tangle. The Centre levied excise duty on manufactured goods and service tax on services. Each state levied its own sales tax, then its own VAT, plus octroi, entry tax, luxury tax, and a dozen other levies depending on which state you were in. A truck crossing three state borders to deliver goods paid at each border. The tax paid at one stage was taxed again at the next — the *cascading effect*, the tax-on-tax, which inflated prices invisibly and distorted the cost structure of every business in the country. The cascading effect was well understood from the 1980s onwards. The solution — a single tax, collected on value added at each stage, with full credit for tax paid at earlier stages — was proposed as far back as 2000, when an Empowered Committee of State Finance Ministers was set up to design it. Eleven years of negotiation followed, driven primarily by states' fears that they would lose revenue. The 2011 Constitution Amendment Bill lapsed. A revised version came before Parliament in 2014. The **101st Constitutional Amendment Act** was finally passed in 2016, empowering both Parliament and state legislatures to levy GST. On 1 July 2017 — seventeen years after the Empowered Committee first met — the Goods and Services Tax was launched.

The architecture of the GST is worth understanding structurally. For a transaction within a state, two taxes apply simultaneously: the **Central GST (CGST)**, collected by the Centre, and the **State GST (SGST)**, collected by the state — each on the same base, each at half the effective rate. For a transaction between two states, a single **Integrated GST (IGST)** applies, which is then apportioned between origin and destination states through a clearing mechanism. The four main rate slabs are 5 per cent, 12 per cent, 18 per cent, and 28 per cent, with zero-rated essentials and a cess on sin goods. What GST solved is substantial: the cascading tax-on-tax problem, the physical border-check bottlenecks, the multiplicity of state levies, and, conceptually, the fragmentation of the national market. What it did not solve is also worth naming. Petroleum and alcohol remain outside the GST — the states' most productive revenue sources, excluded at launch because including them was politically infeasible, with the understanding that they may eventually be brought in. Compliance remains a burden for small businesses, who must file returns monthly. The GST Council's rate-slab complexity generates constant lobbying pressure. And the *self-policing* mechanism that was supposed to reduce evasion — the input-credit chain, whereby a business can claim credit for GST paid only if its supplier has also filed and paid — has proven less automatic in practice than the design assumed.

The **GST Council** itself is one of the more interesting institutional inventions of post-1991 India. It is a constitutional body — its creation is mandated by Article 279A — comprising the Union Finance Minister as chair, and the finance ministers of all states and union territories as members. It operates on weighted voting: the Centre holds one-third of the votes, and the states together hold two-thirds. Decisions require a three-quarters supermajority. In practice this means the Centre holds a blocking minority — no change the Centre opposes can be forced through — and that any radical proposal (like bringing petroleum into GST) requires the Centre to persuade enough states to clear the threshold. The Council is, in this sense, a working model of cooperative federalism: it forces negotiation rather than imposition. That it has functioned through governments of different parties in New Delhi and the states is itself a small institutional achievement.

The other body that arbitrates the federal compact in taxation is the **Finance Commission**, established under Article 280 of the Constitution. The Finance Commission — a quasi-judicial, five-year body appointed by the President — recommends how the *divisible pool* of central taxes should be shared between the Centre and the states. The asymmetry it addresses is structural: states collect only about 37 per cent of total tax revenues but are responsible for spending about 62 per cent of public resources — health, education, police, local infrastructure. The gap is bridged partly by Finance Commission transfers and partly by Centrally Sponsored Schemes, which give the Centre policy leverage even over state subjects. The **14th Finance Commission** (2015–20), chaired by Y. V. Reddy, made the sharpest upward revision in decades, raising states' share in the divisible pool from 32 per cent to 42 per cent. The **15th Finance Commission** (2021–26) kept it at 41 per cent. The difference between 32 and 42 may sound technical; it was, in practice, a rebalancing of fiscal power toward states, and toward the relatively poorer states that the formulae of population, income distance, and forest cover are designed to favour.

I should flag, without going into detail, that the current-affairs layer carries the ongoing arguments here: the latest Finance Commission cycle and its recommendations on the use of 2011 versus 2011 census population data, recent GST rate-rationalisation proposals, the recurring conversation about a new Direct Tax Code to replace the Income Tax Act of 1961, and the evolving taxation of digital assets and crypto. The mechanics of these belong to the current-affairs pass; this chapter has tried to set the structural ground they rest on.

The question I want to close with is this: why, after all these reforms — GST, corporate tax cuts, digital payment infrastructure, Aadhaar-linked direct benefit transfers — does the tax base remain so narrow? Why does formality remain so costly for so many enterprises that they choose to stay informal even when formality would give them access to credit and contracts? The answer, I think, is that the tax system reflects the economy more than it shapes it. In an economy where the cost of formal compliance — in time, in accountant fees, in regulatory interaction — exceeds the benefit of formal status for a small enterprise, most small enterprises will remain informal. The input-credit chain of GST was supposed to make formality attractive: you can only claim credit if your supplier is formal, so formalising your supplier is in your interest. This logic is correct but insufficient. It assumes that the benefits of credit outweigh the costs of visibility — but for enterprises that have survived on informal credit for generations, and that are at risk of harassment by formal authorities once they become visible, the calculus does not obviously tilt toward formalisation. Until that calculus changes — until formality is genuinely cheaper than informality for small producers — the structure of taxation will stay roughly where it is: heavily weighted toward indirect taxes on formal transactions, with direct taxes collected from a thin urban layer, and the largest share of the economy continuing to move without generating a tax receipt. The tax system does not produce this outcome. It inherits it.
