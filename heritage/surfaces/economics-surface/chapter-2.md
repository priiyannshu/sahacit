# 2 — How did India arrive at the economy it has?

There is a habit, common in textbooks and in political speeches alike, of beginning the story of the Indian economy at 1947. It is a tidy starting point. The flag went up, the British left, and a new government sat down to make decisions. What is left out of this beginning is that those decisions were made by people who had spent two decades watching what colonialism had done to an economy — and they made them in a hurry, against a particular inheritance, with particular intellectual equipment. The economy you will encounter in every later chapter is not a single project that has been refined over time. It is three projects, layered on top of one another, each of which partly succeeded and partly failed, and all three of which you are looking at simultaneously when you read today's numbers. This chapter tries to name the three projects and say honestly what each one left behind.

The first project was not chosen at all. It was imposed.

I think the thing hardest for a contemporary reader to feel about the economy India inherited in 1947 is how thorough the dismantling had been. Two centuries of British administration had not simply extracted revenue. It had *deindustrialised* the country — reduced an economy that, in the eighteenth century, produced a substantial share of the world's manufactures to one that exported raw materials and imported finished goods. India's textile producers — the weavers of Dacca muslin, the artisans of Surat, the silk workers of Bengal — had, before the East India Company's consolidation, enjoyed worldwide markets. By the mid-nineteenth century, machine-made cloth from Lancashire had priced them out of their own country. No comparable industrial replacement was built in their place. The colonial economy ran on a deliberate logic: suppress Indian manufacturing, guarantee Indian raw materials for British industry, open Indian markets to British finished goods. The railways built in this period are often cited as a colonial gift, but their routes followed extraction logic, not integration logic — they were built to move raw materials to ports and armies to their positions, not to connect India's internal markets to one another.

The economic account of this process was first articulated systematically by **Dadabhai Naoroji** in 1867. He called it the *drain of wealth*: India's persistent trade surplus — it exported more than it imported, year after year — was not returning to India as gold or productive investment. It was leaving as payment for Britain's administrative expenses charged to India's account, for profits repatriated by British firms, for pensions paid to British officers living in England. It was, in other words, a unidirectional flow disguised as trade. **R. C. Dutt**, in his *Economic History of India* published in 1901-03, followed the argument through with archival detail, showing precisely how colonial tariff and land policy had jointly structured the impoverishment. The intellectual inheritance independent India received was not only an economically broken country — it was a highly developed critique of *how* the breaking had been done. The framers of independent India's economic policy were not starting from ignorance. They were starting from a diagnosis.

By 1947, roughly 85 per cent of India's population depended on agriculture, most of it subsistence or near-subsistence. The industrial base that existed was thin and structurally distorted: cotton textiles in Maharashtra and Gujarat, jute in Bengal, a single iron and steel company that stood as the exception. No capital goods industry to speak of. Life expectancy at birth was thirty-two years. Literacy stood at 17 per cent. Per-capita income in 1947 was, by some calculations, roughly comparable to what it had been in the 1870s — the economic historian Angus Maddison estimated per-capita growth during the entire colonial period at roughly 0.2 per cent per year. This is the economy the republic received, and nothing about it pointed obviously toward what to build next.

The second project begins with a question the framers had to answer almost immediately: what economic philosophy would the new republic adopt?

I want to be clear that there were real alternatives in serious contention, and naming them matters, because the path that was eventually chosen was not the only path available. Three visions competed.

There was the Gandhian vision — a decentralised rural economy built on the village as its unit, cottage industries rather than factories, a conscious resistance to the industrialism that Gandhi associated with European colonialism itself. This was not a marginal position. It was the moral dominant of the independence movement, carried by the most politically revered figure of the era, backed by a coherent critique of what centralised industrial civilisation did to communities.

There was the capitalist-industrial vision, most clearly articulated in the **Bombay Plan** of 1944, drafted by eight of India's leading industrialists — Tata, Birla, and six others. The Bombay Plan was more sophisticated than its critics allowed: it accepted the need for state investment in infrastructure, envisaged rapid industrialisation, and sketched a workable mixed economy with substantial private enterprise. But its authors were the industrial houses, and in a newly independent country suspicious of concentrated private capital — a country that had spent two hundred years watching foreign capital extract from it — that association gave the plan a political ceiling it could never break through.

And there was the Nehru-Mahalanobis planning vision — a state-led development model, shaped by Fabian socialism and by the Soviet Five-Year Plans, refracted through the mathematical training of **P. C. Mahalanobis**, the statistician who had founded the Indian Statistical Institute in Calcutta and who had studied in inter-war Cambridge under some of the same economists who had shaped the Keynesian turn. Mahalanobis's model argued from a specific logic: in a capital-scarce economy, the fastest path to long-run growth ran through *capital goods industries* — industries that made machines that would then make other things — even at the cost of current consumption. Invest first in steel, in power, in heavy machinery; let consumer goods production follow once the industrial base exists to produce them cheaply. This was not an exotic idea in 1952; it was, with variations, the logic the Soviet Union had followed and which appeared, from the outside, to have achieved rapid industrialisation.

The Nehru-Mahalanobis vision won. The **Karachi Resolution** of 1931 had already committed the Indian National Congress to state ownership of key industries, so the political ground had been laid more than a decade before independence. A National Planning Committee, set up in 1938 on the initiative of Subhas Chandra Bose, had spent years working out blueprints for a planned economy. The Planning Commission was constituted in 1950, and the First Five-Year Plan began in 1951. The Second Plan of 1956-61 set the economy's character for the next thirty years: heavy investment in public-sector steel mills, power plants, railways, and capital goods; private industry controlled through the *license-permit-quota raj* — no new factory without government approval, no expansion of capacity without a license, no import without a permit. The license raj was not an Indian invention. It was the wartime British rationing and allocation machinery, retained after independence and elaborated into a regulatory ecosystem that eventually required multiple permits for a single manufacturing enterprise to function. The choice to ration capital through bureaucratic allocation rather than through price signals — that is, to use licensing rather than tariffs — was partly ideological, partly administrative: the colonial civil service had already built the apparatus, and it was at hand.

I want to say something honest about why this model was chosen, rather than treating it as obviously wrong in retrospect. In 1952, no one in the developing world had demonstrated a viable alternative. Export-led industrialisation — the path East Asia would show in the 1960s and 1970s — had not yet been discovered. The terms of trade for primary exporters looked hostile in the early 1950s, and the 1930s Depression was recent enough that reliance on world markets for development finance seemed dangerous. The choice between import substitution and export-led growth was not then the obvious question it appears in hindsight. There were specific reasons for each specific decision — the licensing rather than tariffs, the public sector rather than supported private enterprise, the capital goods first rather than consumer goods — and each made sense within the constraints and intellectual frameworks of the moment, even if their aggregate consequence was not what anyone intended.

What the planning era produced, over thirty years, was a deeply mixed record. The achievements were real and should not be dismissed. An industrial base was built where there had been almost none — the steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela, and Durgapur; a technical infrastructure, the IITs and IIMs and atomic energy programme, that the colonial economy had never built. The Green Revolution of the mid-1960s and 1970s — centred on high-yielding variety seeds, initially in Punjab and Andhra Pradesh — ended the chronic food imports that had made India dependent on American grain shipments and produced, for the first time, a marketable surplus. What did not work was growth itself, and the failure compounded over time. The economy expanded at roughly 3.5 per cent per year, decade after decade — enough to barely outpace population growth, not enough to substantially reduce poverty or narrow the gap with the economies that were industrialising elsewhere. In 1978, the economist **Raj Krishna** coined the phrase that became unavoidable: the *Hindu rate of growth*. He meant it ironically — there was nothing theologically ordained about the stagnation — but it named something precise. While Taiwan and South Korea were compounding at 8 and 9 per cent, India cycled at 3.5, and the poverty headcount in 1980 was not meaningfully below what it had been in 1950. Industrial licensing had, by that point, become a mechanism that incumbents used to block competition rather than a tool to allocate scarce capital efficiently. Public-sector enterprises accumulated losses the treasury kept covering. The famines did not return; the chronic malnutrition did not retreat.

The third project begins with a crisis, not a plan.

The proximate trigger was the First Gulf War of 1991. The decade of the 1980s had itself been an era of partial opening — limited deregulation under Rajiv Gandhi, heavy external borrowing to finance a modest industrial expansion — but this had raised the fiscal deficit above 8 per cent of GDP and accumulated foreign debt to fragile levels. In mid-1991, the Gulf War spiked oil prices and forced the emergency evacuation of Indians working in the Gulf, collapsing remittances. India's foreign exchange reserves fell to roughly *two weeks of imports*. The government pledged gold and flew it to London as collateral for an emergency loan. The International Monetary Fund was approached.

The conditions attached to the IMF's support were not designed by the Indian government. Devaluation of the rupee by 22 per cent in two steps. Abolition of industrial licensing for most sectors. Opening to foreign direct investment. Beginning of public-sector disinvestment. Drastic reduction of peak import tariffs from 130 per cent to 30 per cent. These were the standard elements of what was being called, in that period, the Washington Consensus — the structural adjustment template that the IMF and World Bank were applying to balance-of-payments crisis economies across the developing world. India, in a very specific sense, *inherited* a reform package: the timing was not chosen, the conditions were not negotiated from a position of strength, and the package itself was prescribed rather than designed.

> *No power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come.*

Those are Victor Hugo's words, and they are the words **Manmohan Singh**, the Finance Minister, chose to quote in his budget speech of 24 July 1991, the address that announced the reform programme to Parliament. Notice what the framing does: it converts compulsion into destiny, forced adjustment into historical inevitability, the IMF's conditionalities into an idea the country had been waiting to embrace. Singh was the technical architect of the package. **P. V. Narasimha Rao**, the Prime Minister, was the political cover — a figure of the old Congress establishment, trusted enough to carry the party through a reversal it would have resisted under any other set of pressures.

What followed over the next decade and a half was not the manufacturing boom the Mahalanobis model had always promised. It was something no plan had anticipated: a *services boom*. The IT and business-process outsourcing wave of the late 1990s and 2000s, enabled by cheap international bandwidth and India's accumulated stock of English-speaking engineering graduates, created a major export sector without requiring heavy capital investment or factory employment at scale. Mobile telephony arrived in the early 2000s and compressed roughly three decades of telecommunications infrastructure development into one. By the mid-2010s, the JAM trinity — Jan Dhan bank accounts, Aadhaar biometric identity, mobile connectivity — had assembled a digital infrastructure that allowed the state to transfer funds directly to poor households, bypassing the intermediary leakage that had made the old delivery systems so wasteful. Demonetisation in November 2016 and the introduction of the Goods and Services Tax in 2017 were both attempts to formalise an economy that remained substantially informal; their distributional consequences are still being measured. The COVID pandemic of 2020 produced what economists have called a K-shaped recovery — the formal, asset-owning, digitally connected segment bounced back quickly, while informal workers, migrants, and small enterprises absorbed losses from which many have not recovered.

I should flag clearly: recent industrial policy moves — PLI schemes, Make-in-India, the semiconductor mission — belong to the current-affairs layer, not to this structural chapter. They are the latest attempts to address the manufacturing gap that 1991 opened up but did not close, and whether they succeed is still being decided.

The persistent question that organises the rest of this surface is whether 1991 answered what the planning era could not, or merely deferred it. Manufacturing's share of GDP has not grown the way anyone in 1991 imagined it would. The structural transformation that every prior case of sustained development had shown — agricultural workers moving into factory employment — has not happened at the required scale. Services-led growth, it turns out, does not automatically pull agricultural labour into productive employment the way manufacturing does. India grew at over 7 per cent annually through much of the 2000s and still has a chronic employment problem. This is not a paradox: it is the consequence of the specific growth path that 1991 unlocked.

Hold the following frame for everything that follows. The Indian economy in 2026 is the layered residue of three projects that did not entirely succeed and did not entirely fail. The colonial extraction system built some physical infrastructure and broke the industrial and agrarian base. The planning era built institutions and a technical-scientific capacity but throttled growth. The post-1991 reform unlocked services-led expansion and did not build manufacturing depth. Reading any later chapter — banking, fiscal, agriculture, the external sector — means reading all three inheritances at once. The agriculture chapter is colonial and planning-era inheritance in equal measure. The fintech chapter is post-1991 inheritance. The fiscal chapter is all three, unresolved. That is the orientation this chapter is asking you to carry.
