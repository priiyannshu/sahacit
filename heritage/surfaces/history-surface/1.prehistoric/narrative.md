# 1 — Before History: The Stone Age Subcontinent

The oldest object in Indian history is not a coin, not a manuscript, not a monument. It is a hand-axe. Pick one up in any museum of natural history — notice how it fits the palm. The broader end rests in the curve of the fingers; the pointed end aims forward; the bevelled edges run symmetrically from base to tip, worked by someone who understood the geometry of a cutting surface. People made these for one million years. Not for one generation or one century — for one million years, with virtually no change in form. The hand-axe is not just a tool; it is the signature of a mind that had already, in the deep past, developed a concept of shape and worked stone toward it. Our story begins here, not with kings or texts but with a hand fitting a stone.

The first thing this era wants you to hold is a sense of scale. If all of subsequent Indian history — from the Indus Valley cities to independence — were compressed into a single day, the Palaeolithic would be the previous two months. The Stone Age was not a brief prelude to history; it was the bulk of it. What came after — cities, empires, constitutions — is the brief and brilliant recent addition to a story made mostly of this: people moving across a subcontinent, making tools, reading the land, surviving, and slowly, over immense stretches of time, becoming capable of something more.

Three images will return through this chapter. First, *the long present* — the Palaeolithic as a vast, slow experiment in which biological and technological change were inseparable, each driving the other. Second, *the hand on the stone* — from hand-axe to microlith to painter's brush — as the through-line that holds the era together. Third, *the thickening* — the Neolithic not as a rupture but as the same long present beginning to accelerate, the way a river thickens as it approaches a delta.

## The long apprenticeship

The lineage that will eventually produce us begins in Africa, 6–7 million years ago, when the evolutionary line separating hominins from chimpanzees diverged. India's pre-cultural fossil record is suggestive but meagre. In the Siwalik hills along the India-Pakistan border, the fossilised skulls of Ramapithecus and Sivapithecus — early hominid forms dated to around 2.2 million years ago — were found in the Potwar plateau of Punjab. They are apes, not yet humans; this Siwalik line went extinct without spreading further. The story of modern humans in the subcontinent does not begin here.

What arrives, eventually, is Homo erectus. It is Homo habilis — "handy man," as the name announces — who first fractures stone deliberately, producing sharp-edged flakes from 2.6 million years ago. But it is Homo erectus who perfects the Acheulian hand-axe: a bifacially worked core tool shaped by repeated percussion to a consistent symmetrical form. I want you to feel the ambition embedded in that object. Making it requires holding a mental image of the finished shape while removing material that doesn't belong to it — a cognitive move that is not obvious, that had to be invented, and that, once invented, was refined and transmitted across a million years and an entire continent. India's earliest Lower Palaeolithic site is Bori in Maharashtra, around 600,000 BCE — already half a million years after Homo erectus appears in Africa, showing that the subcontinent received the species late. The classic Indian Lower Palaeolithic regions are the Soan Valley in Punjab, the Belan Valley at the Vindhyan foothills of Uttar Pradesh, Didwana in Rajasthan, and Hunsgi in Karnataka, where tools were made from locally available limestone near springs. Homo erectus also discovers controlled fire around 1 million years ago. From this point, the relationship between technology and the body runs in both directions — tools shaping behaviour, behaviour shaping the biology of the species that makes them.

In 1982, in the Narmada valley at Hathnora in Madhya Pradesh, a fossilised skull was found. Initially identified as Homo erectus, it is now recognised as archaic Homo sapiens — the most significant hominid fossil found in India, evidence that beings already on our side of the biological divide inhabited central India during the Middle Pleistocene, around 700,000 BCE. The Narmada valley, which will return in many later eras as a corridor of movement and culture, has been a human landscape for longer than we once knew.

## The sharpening mind

The Middle Palaeolithic — roughly 150,000 to 35,000 BCE in India — marks a shift from cores to flakes. The Levallois technique, spreading across the Old World in this period, allows a knapper to predetermine the shape of a flake before striking it: not reacting to the stone but imagining the tool before it exists. The surprise here is not the technical refinement but what it implies about the mind doing it — a mind capable of working from a mental template, of planning a sequence of strikes that only makes sense in light of an anticipated outcome. Didwana in Rajasthan yields stratified tools from all three Palaeolithic phases, Lower through Upper, offering a rare sequential record of this progression.

Homo sapiens — our species, modern humans — emerge in Africa around 200,000–300,000 BCE and begin migrating outward. The Out of Africa hypothesis, supported by mitochondrial DNA analysis, holds that all living humans descend from this African source. The timing of arrival in the subcontinent is striking: the earliest Upper Palaeolithic evidence in the Indian region is not on the mainland at all. Fa Hien Cave in Sri Lanka yields fossils of Homo sapiens sapiens dated to approximately 34,000 years ago — artefacts to 31,000 years ago. No contemporary mainland Indian fossils from this period have yet been found, though this reflects the uneven preservation of the fossil record rather than an absence of people. The more plausible reading is that modern humans entered the subcontinent from the northwest, with populations spreading south and eastward over millennia — the Fa Hien skull is the oldest we have found, not evidence that Sri Lanka was the point of arrival.

The Upper Palaeolithic (35,000–10,000 BCE) is when something shifts that goes beyond the cutting edge of tools. Blade-and-burin industries appear — longer, narrower, more specialised. Bone tools arrive. Language, which most scholars now place in the Upper Palaeolithic phase, becomes the medium through which hunter-gatherer bands transmit accumulated knowledge and form extended alliances. And then the paintings begin.

## The hand on the wall

Stand in the Vindhyan hills, 45 kilometres south of Bhopal. V.S. Wakankar discovered the complex here in 1957–58; UNESCO recognised it in 2003. Bhimbetka: over 500 painted rock shelters distributed across ten square kilometres, continuous in occupation from 100,000 BCE to 1,000 CE, the oldest paintings around 30,000 years old.

The paintings are layered, and the layering is itself the story. Upper Palaeolithic hunters painted large animals — bison, rhinoceros, elephant, tiger — in white, dark red, and green. Mesolithic artists added smaller, predominantly red scenes: group hunts, riders, animals grazing. Chalcolithic painters introduced green and yellow, with battle scenes and men on horses and elephants, bow-and-arrow in hand. Layer upon layer, across thirty millennia, the wall accumulates evidence that this place was returned to repeatedly — that a surface holding images was worth coming back to. The pigments (haematite for red ochre, natural minerals for other colours) were sourced, prepared, applied with intention. The Zoo Rock Shelter at Bhimbetka, named for its extraordinary range of animals — bison, elephant, rhinoceros, tiger, spotted deer, barasingha, snake — is not decoration. It is a conversation across time. And what it tells us is that the representational mind — the ability to make an image of a thing absent, to hold it present on a surface — was fully formed in the people who sheltered in these hills.

Bhimbetka is the most celebrated site, but the tradition is wider. Panchmarhi in the Satpura range, Mirzapur in the Vindhyan sandstone shelters of southern Uttar Pradesh, Jogimara in the Ramgarh hills of Chhattisgarh — these carry the same impulse into other landscapes. Alongside paintings, there are also petroglyphs: images carved or abraded rather than painted, a different technique for the same deep urge to mark the world.

The Mesolithic (roughly 9,000–4,000 BCE in India) is the bridge between the Ice Age hunter and the Neolithic farmer. Around 12,000 years ago the climate warms; grasslands spread; the mega-fauna of the glacial era gives way to smaller, faster, more varied animal populations. Mesolithic people adapt their tools accordingly: microliths — tiny stone blades, often no larger than a thumbnail — are hafted onto bone or wood handles to make composite tools: saws, sickles, arrowheads. Bagor in Rajasthan, well-excavated and occupied for five millennia from around 5,000 BCE, gives us the clearest picture of this way of life. At Bagor and Adamgarh in Madhya Pradesh, around 5,000 BCE, we find the earliest evidence of animal domestication in the Indian subcontinent — a dog kept, a goat tended, the first hesitant moves toward a different relationship with other species. At Sambhar in Rajasthan, lake-bed deposits suggest plant cultivation already by 7,000–6,000 BCE.

The hand on the stone is still there. But now it is also the hand on the wall. And it is beginning to reach toward the soil.

## The first farmers

In the Sulaiman and Kirthar hills of the northwest, wheat and barley were being grown and sheep, goats, and cattle herded around 8,000 BCE. The pivot-site is Mehrgarh, on the Bolan river in the Kochi plain of Baluchistan, at the edge of the Indus plains. Around 7,000 BCE, Mehrgarh is already a settled farming village — compartmented mud-brick granaries, rectangular houses, wheat and barley in the fields, cattle and goats in the enclosures. Called one of the largest Neolithic settlements between the Indus and the Mediterranean, Mehrgarh sustains this way of life through floods and disruptions; the potter's wheel arrives around 4,500 BCE from western Asia and pottery rapidly multiplies. Agricultural expansion from Mehrgarh into the Indus floodplain between 4,500 and 3,500 BCE paves the road to the Harappan civilisation. The thickening has reached its next phase.

Neolithic agriculture spreads across the subcontinent through distinct regional networks, each with its own character. In Kashmir, Burzahom and Gufkral form the northern cluster — pit-houses dug into the ground with steps leading down, polished stone and bone tools, and uniquely at Burzahom among all Indian Neolithic sites, the custom of burying domestic dogs alongside their human masters in their graves. In the east, Chirand in Bihar at the confluence of the Ganges, Son, Gandak, and Ghaghra rivers carries settlement into the Gangetic plain, its bone-and-antler industry echoing Burzahom's pattern. In the south, 850-odd settlements across Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu — Piklihal, Brahmagiri, Maski, Hallur — form the southern Neolithic group; cattle-herders at Piklihal leave ash mounds where seasonal cowpens were burned upon departure, a distinctive signature still visible in the archaeological record. Hallur, by around 1,200 BCE, already shows the earliest iron in peninsular India — the Neolithic sliding into the Iron Age without a clean break.

The Chalcolithic — copper used alongside stone — is not a simple next step after the Neolithic. In many regions, Chalcolithic cultures actually followed the Bronze Age Harappan, not the other way around. The Ahar-Banas culture of Rajasthan (Ahar itself was originally called Tambavati, "place of copper," for its early smelting); the Kayatha culture in Malwa; the Malwa and Jorwe cultures of Maharashtra — these are the world of the second and first millennia BCE. At Inamgaon in Pune district, on the Ghod river, a Jorwe settlement has yielded specialist craftsmen — potters, smiths, ivory carvers, lime makers — stratified housing that separates elites in large rectangular structures from others in round huts, and a chief's house that may have stored tribute in kind. Inamgaon, by around 1,400 BCE, stands at the edge of something that might be called urban. In the Ramgarh hills of Chhattisgarh, the Jogimara caves hold paintings dated to around 1,000 BCE — murals in what scholars describe as the world's oldest theatre room, a non-sacred performance space, the final layer before rock art becomes historic-period art.

## What the era leaves us

Prehistoric India does not end with a battle or a decree. It ends with a thickening — the gradual accumulation of population, technique, and exchange that makes an urban civilisation not just possible but, in the Indus floodplain, almost inevitable. The long present of the Palaeolithic has given us: the body adapted to the subcontinent, the hunter-gatherer's encyclopaedic knowledge of its land and seasons, controlled fire, the composite tool, and the painted wall. The Mesolithic adds: the first domesticated animals, the beginnings of cultivation. The Neolithic adds: the stored grain, the mud-brick house, the cultivated field, the settled community, the potter's wheel. The Chalcolithic distributes copper, begins to sort people into specialists and chiefs, and gestures toward the hierarchy that urbanism will formalise.

There are no quotable texts in this era. The Bhimbetka walls are the texts — layered, anonymous, continuous. A hunter painted a bison in white thirty thousand years ago; a Mesolithic artist painted a deer in red eight thousand years later; a Chalcolithic warrior painted a battle scene in green two thousand years after that. No one of them signed their name. The conversation was real; only the words are missing.

The next era opens when the conversation becomes, for the first time, audible — not with a rupture but with the thickening reaching the point where Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro rise from the Indus floodplain, and the silence of prehistory is broken by seals, planned streets, standardised weights, and a script that still waits to be read. The Stone Age was preparation; what follows is the first draft.
