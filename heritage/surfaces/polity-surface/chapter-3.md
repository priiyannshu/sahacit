# 3 — What does the state owe those it governs?

In the previous chapter we asked what lines protect the individual from the state. The answer was a class of walls written in the grammar of negation — *the state shall not*. There is, in the same document, a different class of obligations, written in a different grammar entirely. *The state shall strive. The state shall endeavour. The state shall, in particular, direct its policy towards securing —* . These are not walls. They are programmes. And they form, I think, one of the most philosophically interesting parts of the document, partly because they appear, on a first reading, to do nothing at all.

Begin with what they say. They say, in summary: that the state shall work towards a social order in which justice — social, economic, political — informs all the institutions of national life; that the means of livelihood, the ownership of material resources, the operation of the economic system, shall be ordered so that wealth does not concentrate at the expense of the common good; that workers shall have humane conditions of work and a living wage; that children shall not be exploited; that the rural population shall be self-governing through village councils; that the weaker sections of society — those whose ancestors the herd had explicitly excluded — shall be promoted in their educational and economic interests; that the standard of nutrition and the level of public health shall be raised; that the environment, the forests, the wildlife shall be protected; that the judiciary shall be separated from the executive; that the state shall work towards international peace and the rule of law among nations.

Now read the clause that immediately precedes all of this:

> *The provisions contained in this Part shall not be enforceable by any court, but the principles therein laid down are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country, and it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws.*

This is, I think, an extraordinary sentence. It tells you, in a single breath, that what follows is not enforceable, and that what follows is fundamental. It opens up a category of obligation that is binding without being legally cognisable. The framers knew exactly what they were doing here, because they had borrowed the device. The Irish constitution of 1937 had introduced into modern constitutional law a class of provisions called *directives* — programmatic commitments that could not be sued upon, but that the legislature was nonetheless required to take seriously in framing its laws. The Indian framers picked up this device and expanded it considerably.

Why would a document do this? Why write a chapter of obligations and then declare, in advance, that no court will enforce them? The conventional answer is that the framers made a pragmatic compromise: they wanted to record the substantive commitments of a welfare state, but they recognised that the apparatus, in 1950, did not have the resources to deliver them. To declare a right to adequate livelihood enforceable, when the apparatus did not yet possess the means to provide a livelihood to several hundred million people, would be to put the document into permanent default. So the obligations were entered as programmes, not as rights — to be worked towards, not commanded.

I think this answer is correct as far as it goes, but it is incomplete. There is a second answer, and I think it is the more important one. It is that there are kinds of obligation that a court is not the right body to enforce. A court can stop a state from imprisoning you arbitrarily; it can order an officer to release you, or void a law that authorises your detention. The action is sharp, directed, individual. But a court cannot — should not, perhaps — order the state to *redistribute land*, or to *raise the level of nutrition*, or to *secure a living wage*. These are programmes that depend on legislative judgement, on revenue, on administrative capacity, on the slow accumulation of policy. To enforce them by court order would be to transfer the central work of governance from the legislature to the bench. The framers were not naive about this. They wrote these obligations as binding *political* commitments — binding on the legislature, binding on the executive, binding on the parties competing for office — but not as legally cognisable claims. The claim to enforce them belongs, properly, not in court but in elections.

This is a subtle distinction and it has been misunderstood in both directions. Some have read the non-enforceability clause as making the directives *optional* — pious wishes that the state may pursue or ignore as it chooses. This is wrong. The text says they are *fundamental in the governance of the country*. A government that systematically ignored them would be in violation of the constitutive bargain, even if no court could compel its compliance. Others have read the directives as a wishlist, internally incoherent, that records the framers' enthusiasm without organising it. This is also wrong, but it requires a closer look.

The directives, in fact, carry several distinct strands of post-colonial political commitment, which the framers brought together without forcing them into a single ideology.

There is a *socialist* strand: that material inequality is a problem the state is obliged to work against, that wealth and the means of production should not concentrate, that workers should have a stake in the management of what they work on, that the operations of the economy should be directed towards the common good and not towards the private accumulation of a few. Read against the political economy of 1947 — when a recently extracted colonial economy had to be remade for the population it was supposed to serve — the socialist strand is the demand that the apparatus of the new state actively *oppose* the old distributions, not merely freeze them in place.

There is a *Gandhian* strand: that village councils should be units of self-government, that cottage industries should be promoted in rural areas, that the consumption of intoxicants should be progressively prohibited, that cattle of agricultural value should be protected, that the educational and economic uplift of those communities the herd had excluded — the so-called weaker sections — should be a primary obligation of the state. The Gandhian strand carries the alternative national imagination that did not finally win at the centre, but that the framers refused to leave out of the document. The constitution honours the moral commitments of the freedom movement even where it has not built itself in their image.

There is a *liberal-intellectual* strand: that the state should work towards a uniform civil code applicable to all citizens regardless of community, that the judiciary should be administratively separated from the executive, that free and compulsory education should be provided for all children, that the country should engage internationally through law, peace, and arbitration rather than by force.

The directives are not a single ideology. They are an inventory of the obligations the founding generation believed the state owed to the population — gathered from several different intellectual traditions, retained even where they tug against each other, and left to the political process to reconcile.

This brings us to the most consequential thing that has happened to these directives since they were written, and it is something that could not have been predicted from the text. Over the seventy years that followed, two things took place that changed the *teeth* of the obligation, even though the non-enforceability clause was never altered.

The first is a story I will tell properly in a later chapter, on the question of how the document rewrites itself. In its short form: the directives, the framers told us, were not enforceable, but they were fundamental. As the decades passed, the legislature took some of them more seriously than others, and a confrontation built between those who wanted the directives to be made supreme over the rights of the previous chapter, and those who wanted the rights to remain the supreme constraint. The confrontation reached a constitutional crisis in the 1970s, was attempted to be resolved by a sweeping amendment that placed directives above rights, and was finally restated by the highest court in 1980: that *neither* class of obligation was supreme over the other, that the *balance* between rights and directives was itself one of the structural commitments of the document, beyond the reach of any majority that came later. The directives, on that reading, do not override the rights. But they cannot be ignored either, by any state that wishes to remain within the constitutive bargain. We will return to this.

The second is quieter and, I think, more interesting. The highest court, while continuing to honour the formal non-enforceability of the directives, began over the same decades to *read them into* the rights. The right to life — a single phrase from the previous chapter, four words long — was reread, expanded, made to absorb a great deal of substantive content that had previously lived only in the directives. The right to education. The right to food. The right to shelter. The right to health. The right to a clean environment. The right to legal aid for those who cannot afford it. None of these were written into the rights chapter as such. All of them entered constitutional protection through the expansive reading of *life* — a single word, made to carry the freight of obligations the document had originally entered only as programme. The walls began to absorb the programmes.

So the obligations the state owes its population live in two registers in this document. There are the walls — sharp, individual, court-enforceable, telling the apparatus what it cannot do to the body of any citizen who walks into a court. And there are the programmes — directional, collective, not court-enforceable, telling the apparatus what it must work towards in its laws and policies. The framers were honest that the second class could not be commanded the way the first could. They were also honest that the second class was no less fundamental.

Ambedkar warned, in his closing speech to the Constituent Assembly, that political democracy — equality of vote, equality before the law — was the easier achievement, and that it would be unstable so long as it sat above a society in which social and economic inequality remained intact. The directives are, I think, the document's way of writing into itself the recognition that he was right. They are the part of the constitution that says: the formal political citizenship secured by the previous chapter is hollow without the substantive social and economic citizenship that this chapter commits the state to building. The first cannot survive long without the second.

The walls and the programmes, between them, set out what the state owes the population whose consent it claims.

The next chapter asks what the population, in turn, owes the state — the much shorter, much more recent, and, I think, considerably more contested side of the bargain.
