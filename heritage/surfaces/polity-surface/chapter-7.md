# 7 — Who chooses the governors? How is consent renewed?

Return, for a moment, to the constitutive sentence of the first chapter. *We, the people, having solemnly resolved... do hereby... give to ourselves this Constitution.* I argued there that this is a performative — a sentence that, in being uttered, brings into existence the "we" it claims to discover, and grounds the apparatus that follows on a fiction the country agreed to act as though it were true. Now ask the question I avoided then. *How is that performative kept alive?* The three hundred who uttered it in 1949 are dead. The people of 1950 are mostly dead. What stops the constitutive fiction from quietly running out, the way old stories run out when the last person who could vouch for them is gone?

This chapter is, in effect, the document's answer to that question. The constitutive performative is renewed, periodically, by a procedure that asks the population to re-enact it — to confirm, in the form of millions of individual ballots, that the apparatus continues to act in their name. We call this procedure an *election*. It is, I think, more philosophically interesting than its routine character usually allows.

Begin with the choice the framers made about *who*, exactly, was entitled to participate in this renewal, because their choice was, by the standards of the time, philosophically audacious.

The older democracies — Britain across the nineteenth century, the United States in fits and starts across two centuries — had built suffrage *incrementally*. They had begun by giving the vote to a literate, propertied, male, often racially defined elite, and had expanded it outward, with great difficulty, over decades or centuries. The political philosophers most influential at the time of our framing — Mill, Bagehot, the constitutionalists who had read them — had argued that universal suffrage *required* a literate, propertied, educated population, that to put the vote in the hands of the illiterate was to invite demagoguery, and that the franchise should be expanded only at the pace at which the population qualified itself for it.

Our framers ignored this argument. They wrote, into the document, that elections to the lower house of Parliament and to the legislative assemblies of the States would be held *on the basis of adult suffrage* — every citizen above a specified age would have a vote, regardless of literacy, regardless of property, regardless of caste, regardless of sex. There was no qualification. The age was set at twenty-one (it was lowered to eighteen by an amendment in the late 1980s), and below that age the vote was withheld; above it, every citizen was an elector. This was, in 1950, the largest-scale enfranchisement of an illiterate, poor, hierarchically-organised population in the history of any modern state. It was the practical embodiment of the founding wager: that the people who had been *governed* — who had been the object of governmentality under colonial rule — would, on day one of self-rule, also be the *electors* of those who would govern them. There would be no trial period.

For this performative to be repeatable, it required an *apparatus*. A list of voters had to be drawn up — eventually, more than nine hundred million names. Polling stations had to be set up, often in territory the apparatus had no other regular reach into. Ballots had to be collected, counted, certified. Disputes about who had won had to be resolved by some authority, applying some rules, before the next government could take office. The framers established, for this purpose, a constitutional body whose head's tenure is protected like that of a senior judge, who can be removed only by a procedure as cumbersome as the impeachment of a high judicial officer, and who is given full superintendence over the conduct of every general election to Parliament and to State legislatures, and over the elections to the offices of the head of state and the head of state's deputy. Why give this body so much constitutional protection? Because the body conducts the very mechanism by which the apparatus is renewed. If the apparatus could capture the body, it could capture the renewal. The performative would no longer be a renewal of consent; it would be a periodic ritual ratifying continued power. The framers understood this risk and tried to insulate the body from it by making its independence a constitutional feature, not a political one.

Now consider the *mechanics* by which the population, through this body, chooses its governors. The country uses, for the lower house and most State assemblies, what is called the *first-past-the-post* system. The territory is divided into single-member constituencies. In each, every voter votes for one candidate. The candidate with the highest vote total wins, regardless of whether they have a majority of the votes. A candidate who wins thirty per cent of the vote in a four-way contest may, perfectly legitimately, take the seat. There is no second round, no transfer of preferences, no proportional allocation. The vote is fast, the result is clear, the winner is unambiguous.

This system has a known property: it can produce parliamentary majorities from minority *vote shares*. A national party that wins thirty-five per cent of the votes across the country can, depending on how those votes are distributed across constituencies, win fifty-five per cent of the seats. This is not a flaw. It is a deliberate trade. The framers, having watched the inter-war European democracies collapse partly under the weight of fragmented proportional parliaments, opted for a system that favours decisive outcomes over accurate representation. They chose stability of governance over precision of mandate. Whether they chose correctly is a question that has been argued in every decade since.

The upper house is differently constituted. It is not directly elected by the population at all. It is filled, instead, by indirect election: the elected members of each State legislature vote, by a system of proportional representation, for the State's allocation of upper-house seats. The chamber represents the federal *units*, not the people. And it does not turn over all at once: a third of its seats turn over every two years, in staggered cycles, so that the upper house never experiences the kind of complete renewal that the lower house experiences every five years. The result is a *layering* of consent. The lower house renews on a fast clock; the upper house renews on a slow, staggered one. Different parts of the legislature carry the legitimacy of different moments in the political cycle. This is, I think, an underappreciated feature of the design — a kind of temporal smoothing of the political will, so that the apparatus is never wholly captured by a single electoral wave.

So far, this is the architecture as the framers wrote it. The story takes a turn through the 1960s and 1970s, and the document was amended in response to that turn, in a way I think reshaped the parliamentary system more substantially than is usually recognised.

The problem was this. Through the 1960s and 70s, individual legislators began routinely to switch parties after winning. They were elected on one party's ticket, and then, often within months, sometimes within days, they joined a different group in the legislature. The reasons were various — material consideration, ministerial office, factional disputes, ideological drift — and the practice acquired a popular phrase from a particular legislator in Haryana who, in 1967, had changed his party allegiance three times in a single day. *Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram*, the phrase went. The phenomenon it named was widespread. Governments fell mid-term as members shifted. Coalition arithmetic collapsed and reformed and collapsed again. The mandate the voter had given to a candidate was, in effect, *resold* by the legislator who had received it, often to the highest bidder.

The constitutional response came in 1985. An amendment added to the document a schedule that did the following: a legislator who *voluntarily gave up* the membership of the party on whose ticket they were elected, or who *voted against a direction issued by the party* (the "whip") on a vote, was disqualified from the legislature. The seat was forfeited. Defection was no longer a matter of personal political choice; it was, by the operation of this amendment, a matter of legal disqualification. A subsequent amendment in 2003 removed even the narrow exception for "splits" — a faction of one-third or more breaking away — that the 1985 version had carved out, because that exception had been used, predictably, to legitimise *organised* defections of fractions whose size had been negotiated in advance.

The amendment did, in one sense, what it set out to do. The kind of casual, individual, mid-term defection that had destabilised governments in the 1960s and 70s effectively stopped. Mandates became more honoured. Governments became more stable.

But it also did something else, which I think is more consequential and which the framers of the 1985 amendment did not, perhaps, foresee. *Power shifted, decisively, from individual legislators to party leaderships.* In a parliamentary system, the executive emerges from the legislature, and the legislature is theoretically the body that holds the executive accountable. But when an individual legislator can be disqualified from the legislature for voting against the party whip, the legislature ceases, on most votes, to be a body of independent agents. It becomes an instrument of the parties, and the *parties* themselves become substantively governed by their high commands — the unelected leaderships that issue the whips. The MP's vote on most divisions is no longer the MP's vote. It belongs to the party. The conscience of the individual legislator, which was once the smallest unit of legislative judgement, has, since 1985, been formally absorbed into the party machine. There are votes on which whips are not issued — questions of conscience, certain procedural matters — but on the central business of the legislature, including all confidence votes and most major legislative passages, the whip applies. The cure for one kind of mandate-corruption produced another kind.

There is, then, an enforcement problem the architecture has not solved. The 1985 amendment made the *presiding officer* of the chamber — a politically chosen functionary, elected from among the members of the majority party — the adjudicator of defection cases. This officer is not, structurally, a neutral judge. They are a partisan figure who happens to wear a procedural hat. There have been many cases in which presiding officers have delayed defection rulings to suit the party they came from, and others in which they have accelerated such rulings to suit the same. The highest court has, over the years, ruled that these decisions are reviewable, that they must be made within a reasonable time, that they cannot be insulated from judicial scrutiny on procedural grounds. The structural conflict, however, remains. A partisan officer continues to sit in formal judgment on the partisan loyalty of legislators, and the office's incentives continue to pull in directions the document's architecture cannot fully discipline.

There is, finally, a layer of fragility around the formal architecture that I want to mention briefly even though it cannot be exhaustively treated in this chapter. Money in elections. The asymmetry of access between parties in power and parties in opposition. The role of central agencies during campaigns. The use of the apparatus's own machinery for partisan ends. The control of media. The document writes the rules; the political life of the country has, repeatedly, outrun the rules. The formal architecture of consent renewal is one thing; the practical conditions under which consent is actually registered, in any given cycle, is another. To call an election *free and fair* is to claim, contestably, that the procedures have not deformed the renewal beyond recognition. Whether they have, in any given cycle, is a working political question, not a settled constitutional one. We will return, several times, to the gap between the architecture and its working.

Read this chapter together with the first. The constitutive performative is renewed by elections. The renewal depends on rolls, on a body to conduct it, on a system to count the votes, on rules to keep legislators bound to the mandates they have received. The procedures shape what kind of consent is possible to register. When they work, the population's authorisation of the apparatus is renewed. When they fail — when the body is captured, when the rules are corrupted, when the legislators are bound so tightly to their parties that the legislature stops being a deliberative body — the renewal becomes, increasingly, a performance whose performative force is in doubt.

Which raises the question that hangs over the next chapter. What if the procedure of renewal is *itself suspended* by the apparatus that the procedure was supposed to constrain? What if the apparatus declares, by formal proclamation, that the conditions of normal political life are not in operation, and gives itself, by that declaration, powers it does not normally have? This is the question of *emergency*, and it is the most acute test that the constitutive bargain has so far faced. Once, severely. We will turn to it next.
