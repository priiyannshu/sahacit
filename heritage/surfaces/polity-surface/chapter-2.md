# 2 — What lines protect the individual from the state?

Recall the closing thought of the previous chapter. *Whatever you did not give up, you kept.* This is, I think, the proper way to read the part of the document that draws lines against the apparatus of governmentality. Those lines are not gifts the state hands its subjects. They are the residue of the consenting act itself — the unsurrendered remainder. To read them as concessions is to misunderstand what the founding generation thought it was doing.

But it is one thing to say a line exists. It is another, much harder thing, to make the line bite. The work of this chapter is the difference between those two things.

Begin with a small but decisive question: who, exactly, is bound by these lines? When the document says the state *shall not* — shall not deny, shall not discriminate, shall not deprive — what is the *state* being addressed? Here the framers made a choice that is worth pausing over. They did not address the line only to the central executive in New Delhi. They addressed it to every limb through which the herd is touched: to the central government, to each provincial government, to every legislature, to every local authority — every panchayat, every municipality — and, as the courts would later read it, to every body that draws even a fragment of its power from the public. *Wherever the apparatus of governance reaches you, the line follows.* This is the first and most underappreciated feature of the architecture. The line is not a wall around the centre. It is a constraint that travels with every hand that holds even a fragment of the staff.

Now consider the grammar in which the lines are written. *The state shall not. No person shall be deprived. No citizen shall be discriminated against.* This is the grammar of negation — of forbidding. It is structurally different from the grammar one finds elsewhere in the document, the grammar of programme: *the state shall strive*, *the state shall direct its policy*. Pay attention to that difference. The lines drawn here are not policy goals. They are walls. They tell the apparatus what it *cannot do* to the body whose consent it claims. What the apparatus may do, between those walls, is left silent — and that silence is itself a form of liberty: the residual freedom of the population to act in any way the lines do not prohibit.

The lines come in classes. It is worth attending to what each class is doing.

The first class is the line of *equality*. Here the framers had to perform a difficult double act. On the one hand, they had to declare that the apparatus would not divide its subjects into ranks — that it would treat every person before its laws as an equal, that it would not discriminate on the inherited hierarchies of religion, of caste, of birth, of sex, that the stigma of untouchability was, by the operation of a single sentence, abolished. On the other hand, they had to recognise that a population which is *not in fact* equal cannot be made equal by the simple performance of treating its members the same. To treat unequals equally is itself a form of violence; the framers knew this. So the line of equality permits the state, under specified conditions, to act *unequally* — to make special provision for women, for children, for those whose ancestors were excluded from the herd. Equality, in this constitution, is not sameness. It is a calibrated unevenness whose direction is corrective.

The second class is the line of *freedom* in the older liberal sense — the freedoms that any constitution of the modern type recognises: to speak, to assemble, to move, to settle, to associate, to take up one's livelihood. These are freedoms that pre-existed the modern state and that the modern state, on entering, agreed not to take away. But notice — and this is the most consequential feature of how the lines were drawn — every one of these freedoms in our document carries a clause within itself, a kind of internal knife: the state may impose *reasonable restrictions* in the interests of public order, of decency, of the security of the state, of the integrity of the nation, of friendly relations with foreign states. Look at this clause carefully. It is the seam through which the sovereign claws back the latitude it had just appeared to surrender. Whether the seam holds — whether what counts as *reasonable* is decided by the apparatus or by an institution capable of refusing the apparatus — is a question to which we will return, more than once, before the document is done with us.

A line is drawn around the body itself. No person shall be punished under a law that did not exist when the act was done. No person shall be tried twice for the same offence. No person shall be made a witness against himself. No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty *except according to procedure established by law*. This last sentence is, in some ways, the most contested in the document. Read it narrowly, and any procedure that is followed — however arbitrary the procedure itself — would suffice. Read it broadly, and the *procedure* itself must be just, fair, and reasonable, or no deprivation under it can stand. The text, on its face, does not tell you which reading to take. The reading was done — and redone, and reopened — across the seventy years that follow. We will return to this.

A line is drawn against *exploitation*: traffic in human beings, forced labour, the employment of children in hazardous work. Notice what is happening here, because it is not quite like the other lines. The other lines are addressed to the state — *the state shall not*. These lines are addressed to society as a whole, and enforced *through* the state. They reach into the private contract between two persons and void it, because some bargains are not, by the lights of this constitution, available to anyone, even by consent. A bargain to sell oneself into bonded labour is not a bargain the apparatus will recognise. There are, in other words, transactions the state forbids on behalf of the population *from itself*.

A line is drawn around *belief*. Every person is free to hold a faith, to practise it, to propagate it, to manage the affairs of a religious community — and, equally, to be free of having any faith imposed upon them, in the schools that the state itself runs or pays for. But the line is qualified, again — *subject to public order, morality, and health*. The state retains the latitude to regulate the secular conduct that often surrounds religious practice, and, I think, this calibrated boundary between the religious and the secular is one of the unsolved philosophical commitments of the document. Whether the apparatus has, over the decades, drawn this line in good faith is a question on which honest readers disagree.

A line is drawn around the *culture* of communities the herd has absorbed but not dissolved. A people that speaks a different language, follows a different script, holds a distinct culture, retains the right to conserve it; the minorities, religious or linguistic, retain the right to establish and run educational institutions of their own, on their own terms. This is the recognition that a population of this scale, governed by a single apparatus, is not for that reason a single people in any thicker sense. The herd holds many flocks within itself, and the lines drawn here protect the integrity of the inner boundaries.

And finally, the line that gives all the other lines their teeth. Without this last line, every line above it would be a wish.

*If any of the lines drawn against the state is crossed by the state, the person against whom it is crossed has the right to walk into the highest court in the land and ask that court to issue an order to the state to stop.* The court is given the explicit power to do so — to issue, by any of several specific procedural instruments, an order that the state cannot lawfully refuse. Ambedkar called this provision the heart and soul of the document, and I think he meant something quite specific: the lines drawn earlier in the chapter are substantive promises, but it is *this* line that converts them from promises into rights. A right that cannot be enforced is, at best, a manifesto. The framers, having read the constitutional history of the previous century carefully, refused to leave their substantive lines as a manifesto.

It is worth asking — and the document, as originally written, took a position on the answer — what should happen to these lines when the state itself is in extremis. Can the lines be *suspended* under conditions of grave threat? The text said in effect: yes, the apparatus may, under conditions of emergency, suspend the citizen's right to move the courts for enforcement. In 1975 this provision was used. The lines did not hold. The highest court of the country, in a judgment that has since been treated as a stain on its history, accepted that even the line around life and personal liberty could be put aside if the apparatus said so. The herd, for nineteen months, was told that the staff held by the shepherd had no limit at all.

The country recovered, and when it recovered it amended the document. Certain lines — including, decisively, the line around life and personal liberty — were placed beyond the reach of any future emergency. The lesson of 1975 was, in effect, written back into the text. The lines are now *stratified*: most are contingent, suspended under sufficiently severe conditions of threat; a few are absolute, held even when everything else is contingent. Which lines are absolute, and why those and not others, is a question we will hold open until the chapter on emergency proper.

There is one further thing to notice, and it is a thing about how these lines have *moved*, not stayed still, in the decades since they were drawn. The line around personal liberty, as I noted, was originally written so that any procedure followed by the state would suffice. Sometime in the late 1970s the highest court reread the line — decided, in effect, that *procedure established by law* could not mean any procedure at all, that the procedure itself had to be just, fair, and reasonable, or it failed the line. This is a striking thing for a court to do — to read a line as biting harder than its drafters had written it — and it is, I think, characteristic of how rights of this kind survive. They are not preserved by the words alone. They are preserved by the willingness of an institution to read the words against the apparatus, when the apparatus would rather they read them in its favour. We will return to this institution and its reading habits in a later chapter, because the question of who guards the lines is itself one of the eleven problems this story is organised around.

So: what lines protect the individual from the state?

A class of lines, drawn against every limb of the apparatus, in the grammar of negation — equality calibrated by corrective unevenness; the older liberal freedoms qualified by an internal knife of *reasonable restrictions*; the integrity of the body; freedom from exploitative bargains the state will not recognise even by consent; freedom of and from belief; the protection of the inner cultures of the herd. A line giving these other lines their teeth: the right to walk into a court and have the apparatus stopped. A stratification of which lines are absolute and which are contingent, paid for in nineteen months of national experience. And, above all, a willingness — not guaranteed, but observed — by the institution that holds the staff to read the lines harder than they were written, when the apparatus would rather they were read more softly.

The lines tell you what the apparatus cannot do.

The next chapter asks what the apparatus *must* do — what the state owes to the population whose consent it claims. That is a different mode of obligation, and it carries, as we will see, a different set of teeth.
