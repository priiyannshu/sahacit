# 5 — How is the apparatus organised to act?

The previous four chapters have circled the *bargain* this state strikes with the population it governs — where the authority to govern comes from, what limits the apparatus may not cross, what it must work towards, what the citizen owes in return. The next several chapters take a turn. They look not at the bargain but at the *architecture* through which the bargain is delivered. We are now inside the apparatus.

Begin with the metaphor, sharpened. The shepherd's staff, in this state, is not held by a single hand. It is distributed across many hands, organised at different levels of the herd, held on different rhythms — some hands let go after five years, some at retirement, some never. To understand how the apparatus *acts* is to understand how the staff is laid out across this many-handed structure, and how the hands are made to work together without any one of them gathering the staff entirely to itself.

The first thing to notice — and it is the architectural fact from which most of the rest follows — is that the apparatus exists at *three levels*. There is the central apparatus, in New Delhi, that governs the whole population at once. There is the state apparatus, in each provincial capital, that governs the population of one part of the territory. And there is the local apparatus — the panchayat in a rural village or block or district, the municipality in an urban area — that governs the population of a single locality. These three levels are not, as is sometimes thought, three administrative conveniences. They are three constitutionally recognised holders of the staff, each with its own democratic claim, each with its own sphere of action.

This three-level structure was, I think, more recently consolidated than is generally appreciated. In 1950 the document recognised two levels: the Union and the States. The third level — the local — existed in the inheritance of the country, in panchayats that had been continuous in some form since long before the colonial state arrived, but it had no constitutional standing of its own. It existed, when it existed, by the grace of the State legislature, which could create it or dismantle it more or less at will. In 1992 this changed. Two amendments to the document, taken together, gave the local level a constitutional existence of its own — required panchayats to be constituted at three sub-tiers in rural areas, required municipalities to be constituted in urban areas, required regular elections, required reservation of seats for women and for the historically excluded communities. Whatever happens at the local level after 1992 is, in some sense, a different kind of fact than what happened before. The herd is now constitutionally governed at three levels, not two.

Within each of the upper two levels — the Union and the State — the staff is further divided across three *branches*. There is the executive: the apparatus that does the actual work of governing, that runs the police and the army, that collects taxes, that delivers services. There is the legislature: the apparatus that makes the laws under which the executive acts. There is the judiciary: the apparatus that interprets those laws and that, as we saw in the chapter on rights, refuses or permits the executive's action when challenged before it.

Now look closely at the executive at either of these two levels, because the document does something here that is, I think, the central performative fiction of the parliamentary system. The text says, in plain words, that the executive power of the Union is *vested in the President*; and that the executive power of a State is *vested in the Governor*. Read those sentences and you might conclude that the apparatus, at each level, is operated by a single person sitting at its head — that the President runs the country, that the Governor runs the State.

This is not the case. Read further. The President shall act on the *aid and advice* of a Council of Ministers, with the Prime Minister at its head; the Governor shall act on the aid and advice of a Council of Ministers, with the Chief Minister at its head. And the Council of Ministers, at either level, is drawn from the legislature, holds office only so long as it commands the confidence of the lower house, and falls when it loses that confidence. The vesting of power in the head of state is, in other words, *a fiction of dignity*. The substantive direction of the apparatus comes from a council of ministers that is itself responsible to the elected representatives of the population. The head of state holds the staff in name; the council of ministers holds it in fact; and the legislature, by withdrawing its confidence, can make the council let go.

This arrangement — the parliamentary system — is borrowed from Westminster, where it had developed slowly across several centuries. The framers of our document picked it up because it solved, in their view, a problem the alternative could not solve. The alternative was the presidential system, in which the head of state is also the head of government, elected separately from the legislature and accountable directly to the population. The American republic had built itself around this arrangement, and it has many merits. But the framers thought it carried a structural risk: the separation of executive and legislature could produce a head of government who could neither command the legislature nor be removed by it short of impeachment, leaving the apparatus deadlocked between two democratically-mandated branches that disagreed. The parliamentary system avoids this by making the executive *emerge from* the legislature. The risk it accepts in turn is the consolidation of power in the hands of whoever controls the legislative majority — a risk we will return to in later chapters.

The legislature itself, at the Union level, is divided into two houses. There is the lower house, popularly elected, in which seats are allocated by population and members are chosen by direct vote of citizens; this house represents the *people*. There is the upper house, indirectly constituted, in which the units of the federation — the States — are represented; this house represents the *units*. A bill, to become law, ordinarily requires the assent of both houses. Most States mirror this two-house structure for their own legislatures, though some operate with a single house. The point of bicameralism, in either case, is to slow the legislative process down — to require that any change in the law have the assent of more than one democratic body, organised on more than one principle of representation. We will return to this when we ask how the document rewrites itself.

The judiciary occupies a position somewhat different from the other two branches, and it is worth pausing over the difference. There is *one* judicial hierarchy across the whole country. The apex court sits at the top; the high courts sit one to a State (with some sharing between smaller States); the subordinate courts sit beneath the high courts. This is, I think, an underappreciated commitment of the document, because in many federations — including the one whose architecture our framers studied most carefully, the American — there are *two* parallel court systems, one for federal law and one for state law, with limited overlap between them. Our document refused this duality. The same courts interpret the laws of the Union and the laws of the States. A litigant moves up a single ladder. The integrated judiciary is, in effect, a structural reassertion that the population is one population — that whatever federal differences obtain in legislation, the apparatus by which laws are interpreted and applied to the citizen does not divide the citizen along federal lines.

The apparatus also includes a residual category that is worth a brief mention: *Union Territories*. Some areas of the country, for various historical or strategic or administrative reasons, are not constituted as full States. They are governed centrally, with such legislative and executive arrangements as Parliament prescribes. They are, in effect, parts of the herd that the apparatus governs without the intermediation of a State government. Over the decades several have been promoted to full Statehood; one or two remain.

I have left the most distinctive of the apparatus's limbs for last, because it is the one that does not belong to any single branch and that runs through them all. *The Services.*

The political branches — the executive at every level, the legislatures, even (after some delay) the judiciary at the highest level — turn over with time. Ministers come and go with elections; legislators come and go with elections; even judges retire. The Services do not turn over with elections. They are recruited by competitive examination, conducted by a body insulated from the political apparatus. They serve, in formal language, *at the pleasure of the President* (or the Governor, at the State level), but their tenure is protected by a procedural shield against arbitrary dismissal: they cannot be removed except after an inquiry in which the charges against them are made known and they are given a reasonable opportunity to be heard. The political apparatus may direct them, but it cannot dispense with them. They constitute the *permanent* layer of the staff.

Why does this matter? Because elections produce *discontinuity*. Every five years, at the Union and at most States, the political direction of the apparatus may change entirely. If every limb of the apparatus changed at the same time, the population would experience a kind of reset every five years — files would be lost, ongoing programmes would be abandoned, expertise would walk out the door. The Services are the mechanism by which the apparatus continues *across* the discontinuity of elections. They are the herd's memory of itself. The political branches set direction; the Services preserve continuity; and the document, by insulating the Services from the political branches, prevents either set of governors from disposing of the apparatus to suit itself.

This is what I meant when I said, at the opening of the chapter, that the staff is held on different rhythms. The political offices are held for fixed five-year terms. The judicial offices are held until the holder reaches a retirement age. The Services are held as careers — recruited young, retained for decades, retiring not by political turnover but by age. The apparatus is layered not only in space (Union, State, Local) and not only in branch (executive, legislative, judicial) but also in *time* — different parts of the staff are accountable on different rhythms, and the rhythms are deliberately staggered so that no single moment of political change can capture the apparatus entirely.

Read the architecture as a whole. The shepherd's staff is held by:

— the elected political branches, fixed-term, at Union and State levels, with executive emerging from legislature in a parliamentary fiction inherited from Westminster;

— the elected local councils, also fixed-term, but at a tier the document recognised only after 1992;

— a single integrated judicial hierarchy, slow-turnover, applying both Union and State law without dividing the citizen along federal lines;

— a permanent service apparatus, career-tenured, recruited by examination, insulated from arbitrary dismissal, providing the continuity that the political branches cannot.

No single one of these holds the staff entirely. None of them, by itself, can govern the population. They must work together — and they must, also, work *against* each other when one of them oversteps. This is the architecture's working assumption. Whether it actually works that way is the empirical question to which the rest of this story keeps returning.

There is one large question I have left untouched in this chapter, and the next chapter is built around it. *Where does the line run between the Union and the States?* The architecture above tells you how power is organised *within* each level, but it leaves open the question of how power is divided *between* levels — what the central government is permitted to do that the State governments are not, what the State governments are permitted to do that the central government is not, what happens when the two collide. That is the question of federalism, and it has its own logic, its own history, and its own quiet crises. I think we are now ready to take it up.
