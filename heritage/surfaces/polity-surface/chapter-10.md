# 10 — Who guards the system from capture?

The previous chapter ended on a question we now have to answer carefully. The basic structure of the document, I argued, is the line that even a parliamentary supermajority cannot cross. *But who holds that line?* And, more pressingly, *what gives that holder the authority to hold it, and what stops the holder from being captured by the apparatus the holder is meant to constrain?*

This is the question of guardianship, and it has a logical difficulty at the heart of it. Every guardian-institution this document creates is itself *constituted by* the document it is meant to guard. The apex court that polices the basic structure was created by the same article-writing pen that produced the basic structure. The election body that conducts the renewals we treated in chapter seven was created by the framers themselves, who were aware that an election body could be captured by the apparatus the elections are supposed to legitimise. There is no neutral standing-place. Every watcher is also part of the watched. The framers, and the institutional culture that has grown around the document since, have responded to this difficulty not by pretending that an absolute solution exists, but by *layering the watchers* — distributing the work of guardianship across multiple institutions so that no single capture can extinguish the watching, and providing each layer with structural protections that make capture difficult, even where it is not impossible.

This chapter is about those layers. There are, I think, four of them, and they have grown unevenly across the seven decades the document has been alive. The first is the *judicial* layer — the apex court and the high courts of the States, with the powers we already touched on in the chapter on rights. The second is the *interpretive* layer — the way the courts have, beyond mere enforcement, *read* the document, expanding its protections in directions the framers had not specifically written. The third is the *constitutional-body* layer — independent institutions written into the document itself but operating outside the ordinary chain of executive command. The fourth is the *statutory-body* layer — bodies created later, by ordinary legislation, in response to particular institutional gaps that the original architecture had left unguarded. Take them in order.

The judicial layer is the one we have already met. The apex court at the top of the hierarchy; the high courts of the States below it; the integrated subordinate judiciary below them. The structural design is, on inspection, unusually careful. Judges of the apex court and of the high courts hold office until a fixed retirement age (sixty-five for the apex court, sixty-two for the high courts). Their salaries are charged to a consolidated fund that the apparatus cannot easily withhold. Their conditions of service cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment. They can be removed only by an extraordinary parliamentary procedure — a motion supported by a special majority in both houses, on grounds of proven misbehaviour or incapacity, after an investigation by a committee of jurists. This procedure has been initiated against several judges in the document's history; it has, in its present form, never succeeded. The judges are, in this respect, structurally more secure than ministers, more secure than legislators, more secure than civil servants. They are designed to be the slowest-turning hand on the staff.

The structural design of *who appoints* the judges has, however, shifted across the decades, and it is worth noting that the shift has been substantively contested. In the original design, judges were appointed by the head of state on the advice of the executive of the day, in consultation with the chief justice. In the late 1990s, through a series of judicial decisions, the apex court rearranged this so that, in effect, sitting judges substantially select their own colleagues — through an internal mechanism by which the chief justice and a small group of senior judges propose names that the executive can object to but cannot, in most circumstances, refuse to accept. This was a striking move: the branch whose members are appointed has, in operative effect, taken over the appointment power. Parliament, in 2014, passed an amendment to restore a degree of executive participation in appointments, by establishing a commission with executive and civil-society representation. The apex court, in 2015, struck down the amendment on basic-structure grounds — judicial independence, it held, was part of the basic structure, and the new arrangement compromised it. The matter is, structurally, unresolved. There is a real institutional argument here, and it has not been finally settled.

The judicial layer was, in its original conception, a *court of disputes* — a body that resolved cases brought by parties with standing to bring them. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, in the years immediately after the emergency, the apex court rearranged itself in a second important way. It expanded *who could approach it*. Where before the court had required a complainant to demonstrate that they themselves were affected by the matter complained of, the court now accepted petitions brought by any citizen on behalf of those who, by virtue of poverty or ignorance or marginalisation, could not themselves approach the court. This procedural innovation — usually called *Public Interest Litigation* — transformed the apex court from a court that resolved private disputes into a court that, in effect, supervised governance. Petitions brought under this jurisdiction have, over the decades, addressed prison conditions, environmental degradation, sexual harassment in the workplace, the rights of bonded labourers, the conditions of mental hospitals, the implementation of food schemes for the poor. Many of these matters had no other forum. The court, by widening its door, *manufactured* a layer of guardianship that the document itself had not specifically written.

The doctrine of public-interest standing has been criticised, and not foolishly. From one direction: the court is not equipped, structurally, to do governance. It does not have the staff, the expertise, the political accountability that legislative and executive bodies have. From another: the doctrine has been used unevenly — sometimes against governments the court was inclined to constrain, less vigorously against governments it was inclined to protect. From a third: the doctrine creates a *judicially-led* governance review that has no electoral basis. These criticisms have force. The doctrine has, despite them, persisted, because the alternative — leaving entire constituencies of vulnerable persons with no judicial recourse at all — was, on balance, judged worse.

This brings us to the interpretive layer, which is, I think, harder to see than the judicial one but is not less consequential.

The courts, across the decades, have not merely *enforced* the document. They have *read* it. And the reading has, repeatedly, been more capacious than the framers' words. The right to life, written as four words in the rights chapter, has been read to include — as I noted in the chapter on directives — the right to education, the right to food, the right to shelter, the right to health, the right to a clean environment, the right to legal aid, the right to dignity. The clause that limits deprivation of life and personal liberty to *procedure established by law* has been read to require that the procedure itself be just, fair, and reasonable. The basic structure doctrine itself — which we treated in the previous chapter — was a judicial reading without textual basis. The integration of directives into rights, the expansion of equality jurisprudence, the development of an environmental jurisprudence largely from a few sentences in the directives, the recognition of privacy as a constitutional right derivable from life and liberty — all of these are interpretive moves the framers did not specifically write.

This interpretive technology is itself a layer of guardianship. The courts have, in effect, written into the document protections that the framers did not write, by reading what was written more capaciously when the apparatus would have preferred a narrower reading. The document, on this account, has *grown* over the decades — not by amendment but by reading. The interpretive layer is the form of guardianship that operates without textual authority and that no clause of the document grants. It is, however, the form of guardianship most exposed to reversal. A future apex court could, in principle, read the document back to its framers' words. The interpretive expansions are durable not because any clause makes them durable but because the institutional culture of the courts and the political culture of the country have, so far, accepted them.

The third layer is the constitutional-body layer. The framers, anticipating that some functions of governance must be insulated from the political branches in order to perform their function, created several institutions inside the document itself, with their own sources of authority and their own structural protections. Five of them are worth noting.

The election body, which we treated in chapter seven — the body that conducts the very renewal of the apparatus, with its head's tenure protected like that of a senior judge.

The audit body — an officer with the constitutional duty to audit the receipts and expenditure of the central and state governments, and to report independently to the legislature on whether public funds have been spent in accordance with law. Without an independent audit body, the apparatus could spend the population's money without account; with it, every rupee of public expenditure passes under an external eye. The audit body's head holds office for a fixed term, with salary and conditions protected, and can be removed only by the same parliamentary procedure as a senior judge.

The public service commissions — at the central level and in each State — which conduct the competitive examinations through which the permanent services we treated in chapter five are recruited. Without them, recruitment to the services could be handed to political actors, who would have every incentive to staff the apparatus with their own loyalists. With them, recruitment is, in principle, conducted on merit by a body insulated from political direction.

The finance commission — the quinquennial body that determines the formulae by which centrally collected revenue is shared with the States. We treated this in the federalism chapter. The body's existence is constitutional; its composition is decided afresh every five years, by the head of state on the executive's advice; its recommendations are not technically binding on the Centre but are, in practice, almost always accepted.

The language commissions and the inter-state council — bodies for managing the structural disagreements that the federation, and the linguistic compromise, are bound to produce.

These constitutional bodies are not above the political apparatus. Their members are appointed by the political apparatus. But the structural protections — fixed terms, protected conditions, removal only by extraordinary procedure — make capture by any single political moment hard. The bodies are designed to outlast the government that appointed them. Their incentives, on the design, are to please the document, not the government.

The fourth layer is the statutory-body layer, and it is structurally the weakest. As the document aged, gaps in its guardianship architecture became visible — particular institutional weaknesses that the framers had not specifically addressed. Parliament responded, by ordinary legislation, by creating new bodies. A human rights commission was set up in the early 1990s, charged with investigating violations of the rights chapter and of international human rights instruments to which India is party. A central body for vigilance over corruption in the apparatus existed from the 1960s in advisory form, and was given fuller statutory protection in the early 2000s. A right-to-information mechanism was established in 2005, giving every citizen a procedural right to demand information from public authorities and an information commission to enforce that right when it was refused. An ombudsman for corruption at the highest levels of the apparatus — the *lokpal* — was established by legislation in 2013, after years of public movement.

These statutory bodies have done substantial work. The right-to-information mechanism, in particular, has produced a culture of demanded transparency that did not exist before — a citizen can now require, on penalty of disclosure, the same kinds of facts about the apparatus that the apparatus would, before, have refused to part with. The human rights commission has investigated custodial deaths, communal violence, encounter killings, in cases the police would have preferred to bury. The vigilance body, when properly staffed, has been a check on bureaucratic corruption.

But the statutory bodies are, structurally, more vulnerable than the constitutional ones. A guardian created by ordinary statute can, by ordinary statute, be weakened. A statutory body whose appointments are controlled by the apparatus can be staffed in ways that compromise its independence. A statutory body whose budget passes through the executive can be financially constrained. A statutory body whose enforcement powers depend on cooperation from the apparatus can find that cooperation withheld. The right-to-information mechanism has, in recent years, seen its commissioners appointed in ways that have raised serious concerns about independence. The lokpal has, in operative effect, been weak — for reasons that include but are not limited to delayed appointments and limited staff. The deeper structural problem here is that the very Parliament that created these guardians is the Parliament that, with a single ordinary majority, can amend their constituting statutes to weaken them. The guardians can be defanged by the same body whose conduct they were established to police.

Read all of this together. The document has, between the four layers, a substantial guardianship architecture. The apex court at the apex; the high courts in the States; the integrated subordinate judiciary; the interpretive expansions of the rights chapter and the basic structure doctrine; the constitutional bodies for elections, audit, services, federal finance, and inter-state cooperation; the statutory bodies for human rights, anti-corruption, information, and high-level oversight. No single one of them holds the document alone. Together, they have, across most of the document's history, held it.

But a careful reader of these chapters will, by now, have noticed a pattern. The architecture is not self-enforcing. The architecture *failed* in 1975-77, when the executive, the legislature, and the judiciary all, in different ways, defaulted on their structural obligations, and the population's vote in 1977 rescued the document from a state the architecture itself had let it slip into. The architecture was *partly defanged* by the 42nd Amendment of 1976, until a different bench of the same court restored it in 1980. The interpretive layer is durable so long as the institutional culture of the courts is willing to maintain it. The statutory bodies are durable so long as Parliament wishes them to be. The constitutional bodies are more durable, but they too depend on the appointments process, the conditions of service, and the willingness of the political branches to abide by their findings.

What this means, I think, is the following. The document distributes guardianship across multiple watchers in order to make capture by any single actor hard. But the watching itself, in the end, depends on the watchers' *willingness to watch*, and on the apparatus's *willingness to obey* the watchers when they do. The clauses that establish the watchers can be circumvented; the watchers can be co-opted; the apparatus can simply refuse to comply with a finding it does not like. None of these is a hypothetical scenario. All of them have happened, in lesser or greater degree, within the document's lifetime. What has saved the document, when it has been saved, is that on most occasions enough of the watchers — and enough of the population, supporting them — have continued to watch and to insist on being heard.

The herd metaphor returns one last time at this scale. The watchers are not the shepherds. They are not above the apparatus, and they are not above the population. They are part of the herd, designated by the constitutive bargain to keep an eye on the staff and to call out when it is held wrongly. They watch the shepherds, and they watch each other, and they are watched, in turn, by the population whose consent grounds the apparatus. The document does not stand by itself. It stands because the watching continues. *Whether the watching continues* is, in the deepest sense, the question that hangs over every clause we have considered.

There is one final chapter, and it addresses a question the document has, throughout these chapters, mostly addressed under one frame. The frame has been the population *as a whole* — the herd as one body, the citizen as one figure, the rights and obligations as universal. But the population the document inherited was not, in 1949, homogenous. There were communities the constitutional framers received *as already excluded* — by caste, by tribal isolation, by linguistic margin, by colonial demarcation. The document, at its closing, addresses these communities directly, with provisions that depart from the universalising framework. That is the final question this story has to take up, and it is the subject of the next chapter.
