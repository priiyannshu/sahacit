# 8 — A thin film, extraordinarily full

Of the planet's 6,371 km radius, the zone where life actually functions is perhaps 20 km deep — from the floor of the *Mariana Trench* at roughly 11 km below sea level to the altitude at which the last functioning ecosystems persist, a few kilometres above the *Himalayan* treeline. Twenty kilometres in a planet 12,742 km in diameter is about the thickness of the skin on an apple, relative to the apple. This is the *biosphere* — the term Eduard Suess coined in 1875 for the thin zone where the *lithosphere*, *hydrosphere*, and *atmosphere* overlap and, in that overlap, sustain life. The biosphere is the only one of the planet's four spheres that has agency in the others: it has rerouted rivers, transformed the atmosphere from mostly carbon dioxide to mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and locked hundreds of millions of years of dead organisms into the coal and oil deposits that earlier chapters described. Life is not just a passenger on the planet. It is one of the planet's geological forces — the youngest one, and in some ways now the most consequential.

Within the biosphere, life is organised into *ecosystems* — functional units where living organisms (the biotic component) interact with their physical surroundings (the abiotic component: soil, water, temperature, light, nutrients) as a working system. The term was coined by Arthur Tansley in 1935, and it has an elegant architecture. Energy enters from the Sun. *Producers* — green plants, phytoplankton, any organism capable of *photosynthesis* — capture that energy and fix it into organic matter. *Primary consumers* (herbivores) eat the producers. *Secondary consumers* (carnivores) eat the primary consumers. *Tertiary consumers* eat the secondary. *Decomposers* — fungi, bacteria — break down dead matter at every level and return nutrients to the soil and water. This is a *food chain*, but real ecosystems are not chains; they are *food webs*, with dozens of species at each trophic level and multiple pathways between them. A food web is resilient to the loss of a single species because energy has alternative routes; a chain would collapse.

Energy moves through this architecture with a ruthless accounting rule that *Raymond Lindeman* described in 1942: approximately 10% of the energy at any trophic level passes to the next. The other 90% is lost as heat, used in metabolism, or simply unavailable. This is the *ten per cent rule*, and its consequence is visible everywhere: producers are abundant, herbivores are less common, and apex predators are always rare. An *ecological pyramid of energy* is always upright — you cannot have more energy at the top than you put in at the bottom. This is why the *Bengal tiger*, the *snow leopard*, and the *Asiatic lion* exist in small, geographically restricted populations, and why any disruption to their prey base is catastrophic. Take a *Sundarbans* mangrove web: phytoplankton and mangrove trees fix solar energy → small fish, crustaceans, and invertebrates eat them → larger fish and waterbirds eat those → the *Bengal tiger* at the top, swimming between islands in search of deer and pig. Remove a middle layer and you do not just lose that layer; you shorten the chain from both ends.

Life depends not just on energy flow but on matter cycling. Nutrients do not leave the system; they circulate between organisms and the abiotic environment in *biogeochemical cycles*. The *carbon cycle* is the most immediately important: plants absorb atmospheric CO₂ through photosynthesis and fix it into organic matter; respiration, decomposition, and combustion release it back. Fossil fuels are carbon locked away in the *lithosphere* over hundreds of millions of years; burning them adds it back to the atmosphere faster than the cycle was designed to absorb — the mechanism Chapter 12 will return to in detail. The *nitrogen cycle* converts atmospheric N₂ into biologically usable forms through fixation (by soil bacteria like *Rhizobium* in legume root nodules, by lightning, and by the industrial Haber-Bosch process) and returns it to the atmosphere through denitrification; the human disruption of this cycle — fertiliser runoff driving *eutrophication* in lakes and coastal waters — is one of the quieter ecological crises of the century. The *phosphorus cycle* is the slowest, limited by the rate at which rocks weather; there is no atmospheric reservoir, and the world's accessible phosphate deposits are concentrated in a handful of countries — an agricultural bottleneck that the coming decades will make increasingly visible.

*Biodiversity* — the variety of life — operates at three levels that conservation has to address simultaneously. *Genetic diversity* is the variation of genes within a species: the 50,000–200,000 traditional rice landraces cultivated across India represent a genetic reservoir built up over millennia of selective cultivation in varied microclimates. Lose those landraces to standardised high-yield varieties and you narrow the genetic capital that plant breeders will need when the next disease or climate shift arrives. *Species diversity* is what most people mean when they say biodiversity: the count of distinct species in an area. *Ecosystem diversity* is the variety of habitat types — wetlands, mangroves, alpine meadows, coral reefs, deciduous forests — each with its own assemblage and its own set of services. Lose an ecosystem type and you may lose hundreds of species simultaneously, including many not yet identified.

Species richness follows a gradient from equator to poles: more species per unit area in the tropics, fewer as you move toward higher latitudes. The pattern is old and consistent enough that multiple hypotheses have been offered — greater solar energy input supporting more productivity, greater climatic stability over geological time (the tropics were never glaciated), more time for speciation, the *species-area relationship* (simply more land area in the tropical belt), and the *intermediate disturbance hypothesis* (moderate disturbance prevents competitive exclusion without wiping things out). None of these explanations is complete on its own; the real answer is probably all of them operating together. What matters for India is that the country sits in the tropical and subtropical band, which is part of why it carries so much life on what is only 2.4% of the planet's land area. The other part of the answer is geological: India's inheritance here is the Gondwana-Asia collision that joined two historically separate biogeographic realms — the *Oriental* and *Palaearctic* — across the same landscape, and the topographic complexity that places ecosystems from sea level all the way to permanent snow within a few hundred kilometres.

The 17 *megadiverse* countries — a category defined by the UN Environment Programme's World Conservation Monitoring Centre — hold an estimated 70% of the world's biodiversity between them. India is on that list, alongside *Brazil*, *Indonesia*, *Colombia*, *Ecuador*, *China*, *Mexico*, *Australia*, and others. But megadiversity is a broad frame. A sharper tool came from the British ecologist *Norman Myers*, who in 1988 proposed the *biodiversity hotspot* concept and refined it in 2000. The definition has two criteria: a region must contain at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species, and it must have already lost more than 70% of its original primary vegetation. Endemic means found nowhere else — species that exist in that landscape and, if the landscape goes, go with it. There are currently 36 recognised hotspots globally, covering roughly 2.4% of the planet's land surface but harbouring approximately 50% of the world's plant endemics and 60% of terrestrial vertebrate endemics. The hotspot concept made a moral argument in the language of triage: if you have to choose where to concentrate conservation resources, choose these places, because you cannot afford to lose what only they contain.

India is part of four of those 36 hotspots. The *Western Ghats - Sri Lanka hotspot* — the Sahyadri system — runs along India's southwestern coast and continues across highland *Sri Lanka*; it is one of the world's eight "hottest hotspots", with around 5,000 plant species, a remarkable radiation of freshwater fish (192 endemic species in the *Western Ghats* alone), the *lion-tailed macaque*, the *Nilgiri tahr*, and *shola* grassland-forest mosaics above 1,500 metres that exist nowhere else on Earth. The *Eastern Himalaya hotspot* encompasses *Bhutan*, northeastern India, and parts of *Nepal*; the abrupt altitudinal rise from *Brahmaputra* valley floors to Himalayan peaks within a few hundred kilometres creates a stacking of biomes so dense that this region holds one of the highest concentrations of plant species in the world — *red panda*, *clouded leopard*, *takin*, and hundreds of orchid species call it home. The *Indo-Burma hotspot* extends across northeastern India south of the *Brahmaputra* through *Myanmar*, *Thailand*, and *Indochina*; India's contribution includes the home range of the *hoolock gibbon* (India's only ape), the *Irrawaddy dolphin* in the river systems of the northeast, and several endemic turtle species. The *Sundaland hotspot* reaches into India only at the *Nicobar Islands*, but that toe-hold hosts the *Nicobar megapode*, *leatherback turtle* nesting beaches, and species assemblages more closely related to *Southeast Asia* than to the Indian mainland — a biological signature of the Sundaland plate's distinct geological origin.

I find the numbers worth stating plainly, because they make the conservation stakes concrete. India holds roughly 91,000 described animal species and 46,000 plant species — approximately 8% of all known species on 2.4% of the planet's land. The endemism within that count is what tells the deeper story. The *Asiatic lion* survives in the wild only in the *Gir* forest and surrounding landscape in *Gujarat* — a population down to around 20 individuals in 1900, since recovered to over 600, a conservation success shadowed by the vulnerability of a single-location population. The *one-horned rhinoceros* lives only on the *Brahmaputra plains* — in *Kaziranga* and *Manas* in *Assam* and a few small pockets elsewhere, and nowhere else on Earth. The *Indian wild ass* is found only in the *Rann of Kutch*, centred on *Dhrangadhra*. The *Nilgiri tahr* is found only in the *Western Ghats*. The *snow leopard* ranges across the *Trans-Himalayan* zone. The *Bengal tiger*, more widely distributed, functions as an umbrella species — protecting tiger habitat protects almost everything sharing it.

*Rodgers and Panwar*, working in 1988, divided India into 10 biogeographic zones, each with its own assemblage and conservation requirements: *Trans-Himalaya* (the high-altitude cold desert of *Ladakh* and Lahaul-Spiti, an extension of the Tibetan plateau); *Himalaya* (the mountain chain from northwest to northeast); *Indian Desert* (the arid zone west of the *Aravallis*, incorporating both the sand desert of *Rajasthan* and the salt flats of *Gujarat*); *Semi-Arid* (the Punjab plains and Rajputana transition zone); *Western Ghats*; *Deccan Peninsula* (the large central highland block); *Gangetic Plain*; *North-East India* (the non-Himalayan hill ranges east of Bengal); *Coasts* (eastern and western coasts plus *Lakshadweep*); and *Islands* (*Andaman and Nicobar*). Each zone has its own conservation imperatives. The classification was intended, in Rodgers and Panwar's original design, to serve as the geographic skeleton for planning the protected area network — where to locate reserves, where to maintain corridors, which habitats are underrepresented. India is not one biological territory; it is ten.

The *IPBES* — the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services, the biodiversity equivalent of the IPCC — identified five main drivers of biodiversity loss in its 2019 Global Assessment, sometimes called the "evil quintet." *Habitat loss and fragmentation* is the largest: deforestation, wetland drainage, agricultural expansion, and urban growth destroy and isolate habitats, leaving populations too small to maintain genetic diversity and too separated to interbreed. *Overexploitation* — hunting, poaching, overfishing, illegal wildlife trade — takes individuals faster than populations can replace them. *Pollution*, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus runoff, degrades aquatic habitats. *Invasive alien species* arrive when humans carry organisms across natural barriers: *Lantana camara* now dominates the understory of vast tracts of Indian forest, outcompeting native shrubs and reducing habitat quality for prey; *water hyacinth* (*Eichhornia crassipes*) chokes lakes and rivers from *Loktak* to *Chilika*; *Prosopis juliflora* has replaced native vegetation across the *Thar* and semi-arid zones. *Climate change* shifts species' ranges, disrupts the phenological timing that pairs pollinators with flowers and prey with predators, and is already pushing mountain species toward higher altitudes and eventual summits they cannot cross.

The *IUCN Red List* — maintained by the International Union for Conservation of Nature, founded in 1948 and headquartered in *Gland*, Switzerland — is the global inventory of extinction risk. Categories move from Not Evaluated and Data Deficient through Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered, Extinct in the Wild, to Extinct. The *Bengal tiger* is Endangered. The *Indian elephant* is Endangered. The *snow leopard* is Vulnerable. The *great Indian bustard* — found in the *Thar* and grasslands of *Rajasthan* — is Critically Endangered. The *gharial*, a fish-eating crocodilian found in the *Chambal* and *Girwa* rivers, is Critically Endangered, reduced from perhaps 10,000 individuals in the 1940s to around 700 today.

India's response to biodiversity loss is layered: constitutional, statutory, and institutional. The constitutional anchors sit at two levels. **Article 48A** (a Directive Principle, added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976) requires the state to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife. **Article 51A(g)** (a Fundamental Duty, same amendment) requires every citizen to protect and improve the natural environment. Neither is directly enforceable in court the way fundamental rights are, but courts have used both as interpretive tools in environmental litigation. Jurisdiction falls on the Concurrent List under **Article 246**, meaning both Parliament and state legislatures may legislate — which produces significant variation in implementation quality across states.

The statutory spine runs chronologically. The **Wildlife Protection Act 1972** is the foundational statute, passed in the same year as the Stockholm UN Conference on the Human Environment, and carrying the signature of Indira Gandhi's environmentalism. It created the schedule system for protected species (Schedule I animals receive the highest protection), established the framework for national parks and wildlife sanctuaries, and gave states authority to notify protected areas. The **Forest Conservation Act 1980** ended the earlier practice of state governments diverting forest land for non-forest use — mining, dams, industry — without central approval; after 1980, any such diversion requires central government permission. The **Environment Protection Act 1986**, passed in the aftermath of the *Bhopal* gas tragedy of 1984, gave the Centre sweeping rule-making powers over environmental standards, creating an umbrella law under which hundreds of regulations have since been issued. The **Biological Diversity Act 2002** implemented India's obligations under the *Convention on Biological Diversity*: it created the National Biodiversity Authority at the centre, State Biodiversity Boards at state level, and Biodiversity Management Committees at local level, and introduced the access-and-benefit-sharing framework requiring foreign entities seeking access to India's biological resources to obtain approval and share benefits with local communities. The **Forest Rights Act 2006** broke with the earlier framework by acknowledging that forest-dwelling communities — *Scheduled Tribes* and other traditional forest dwellers — had been dispossessed of their customary rights by colonial forest enclosure, and began a long-overdue restitution by granting individual and community forest rights. This created a structural tension with WPA 1972's inviolate core zones; it has not been resolved and is a live feature of Indian conservation law. The **Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act 2016 (CAMPA)** created a mechanism to collect and deploy funds when forest land is diverted: the developer funds compensatory afforestation elsewhere, through an accountability structure designed to unlock money that had historically sat unspent in state treasuries.

The protected area network these statutes govern is one of the largest in the world. *National Parks* — 106 in number — are the most strictly protected category: no grazing, forestry, cultivation, or permanent human habitation; boundaries fixed by legislation. *Jim Corbett National Park* in *Uttarakhand* was the first, established in 1936. *Kaziranga* in *Assam*, a World Heritage Site, holds the world's largest one-horned rhinoceros population. *Sundarbans National Park* in *West Bengal* is the largest mangrove forest on Earth. *Gir* in *Gujarat* is the sole wild refuge of the *Asiatic lion*. *Periyar* in *Kerala* protects the elephants of the *Western Ghats*. *Wildlife Sanctuaries* — over 566 — allow more human activity than national parks and have more flexible boundaries. *Conservation Reserves* and *Community Reserves*, added by a 2003 amendment, provide lighter-touch protection for buffer landscapes and community-managed areas.

**Project Tiger**, launched in 1973, created a network now numbering more than 54 *Tiger Reserves* across the country, each with a strictly protected core and a buffer zone where human activity is managed. Tigers were estimated at around 1,800 in 1973; the 2022 all-India count placed them at approximately 3,682 — India now holds more than 75% of the world's wild tigers, and the recovery is one of the few unambiguous conservation successes in the country's recent history. It has also pushed tigers into human-dominated landscapes as populations expand beyond reserve boundaries, sharpening human-wildlife conflict across corridor districts of *Madhya Pradesh*, *Maharashtra*, *Karnataka*, and the foothills of *Uttarakhand*. **Project Elephant**, launched in 1992, covers over 33 *Elephant Reserves*, focused on maintaining corridors between fragmented forest patches; the *Asian elephant* requires unbroken habitat connectivity over hundreds of kilometres that no single reserve can provide. *Biosphere Reserves* — 18 in India, 12 in UNESCO's World Network of Biosphere Reserves — use a concentric-zone model of core, buffer, and transition zones. The *Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve*, designated in 1986, was India's first and remains the largest. *Nanda Devi*, *Gulf of Mannar*, *Nokrek*, *Manas*, and *Sundarbans* are among those in the UNESCO network. India has designated more than 80 *Ramsar wetland sites*: *Chilika* Lake in *Odisha* (the largest coastal lagoon in Asia), *Loktak* Lake in *Manipur*, *Wular* Lake in *Kashmir*, *Keoladeo* in *Rajasthan*, *Sambhar* Salt Lake.

The international legal architecture can be reduced to a few load-bearing pieces. The *Convention on Biological Diversity* (CBD), opened at the *Rio de Janeiro* Earth Summit in 1992, has three pillars: conservation of biodiversity, sustainable use of its components, and equitable sharing of benefits from genetic resources. India was a strong proponent at Rio — the argument from developing countries was that biodiversity exists mostly in the South while the biotechnology industry that extracts value from it sits mostly in the North, and the CBD's access-and-benefit-sharing framework was the diplomatic outcome. The *Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety* (2000) is an additional agreement to the CBD dealing with living modified organisms — genetically engineered organisms — and their international trade; India ratified in 2003. The *Nagoya Protocol* (2010, in force 2014) strengthened access-and-benefit-sharing provisions, requiring that genetic resources be accessed with prior informed consent and that benefits be shared fairly; India ratified in 2014. The *Ramsar Convention on Wetlands* (1971, *Iran*) predates the CBD; India joined in 1982. *CITES* — the *Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora*, 1973 — regulates trade through three appendices: Appendix I bans commercial trade in the most threatened species (*tigers*, *elephants*, *rhinoceros*); Appendix II regulates and monitors trade in species that could become threatened; Appendix III lists species protected in at least one country. The *Convention on Migratory Species* (CMS, *Bonn* 1979) addresses species crossing national boundaries. *IPBES* (2012) provides the global science-policy interface for biodiversity, producing assessments for governments as the IPCC does for climate. The *Aichi Biodiversity Targets*, set at CBD COP 10 in 2010, expired in 2020 largely unmet; they were succeeded by the *Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework* adopted at CBD COP 15 in 2022, which set a "30×30" goal — 30% of land and oceans under effective conservation by 2030.

Several flagship species programmes run alongside the protected area system. *Project Snow Leopard* (2009) focuses on the *Trans-Himalayan* zone. *Project Rhino* aims to expand one-horned rhinoceros populations beyond *Kaziranga* and *Manas* to reduce the catastrophic risk of single-site dependency. *Project Lion* centres on the *Gir* population and the long-contested question of establishing a second lion population at *Kuno-Palpur* in *Madhya Pradesh* as an insurance colony. *Sea Turtle Conservation* programmes protect the *Olive Ridley turtle*, which mass-nests (*arribada*) at *Gahirmatha* beach in *Odisha* in one of the world's largest such aggregations. The vulture story deserves its own sentence. In the 1990s, three Indian vulture species — white-backed, long-billed, and slender-billed — crashed by more than 95% in less than a decade. The cause, identified after years of investigation, was the veterinary drug *diclofenac*: vultures eating carcasses of livestock treated with diclofenac suffered kidney failure and died within days of feeding. India banned veterinary diclofenac in 2006 — one of the fastest regulatory responses to a documented wildlife crisis in the country's history. *Vulture-Safe Zones* have been established around breeding colonies. Populations remain far below their 1990 levels, because vultures take four to five years to reach breeding age and recovery is slow; but the collapse has been arrested.

A reasonable question is whether conservation and human welfare in India can be reconciled, or whether they are structurally at odds. The *Forest Rights Act 2006* said explicitly that the protected area regime had been built on a dispossession — colonial forest enclosure had criminalised the forest use of millions of people, and post-independence conservation had often deepened it in the name of wildlife. The tension between WPA 1972's inviolate core zones and FRA 2006's community rights has not been resolved. Human-wildlife conflict is intensifying as both wildlife populations and human settlements expand into shared landscapes. The *Western Ghats* eco-sensitive zone debate captures the same tension at policy scale: the Gadgil Committee report (2011) recommended declaring nearly 64% of the *Western Ghats* ecologically sensitive, restricting mining, quarrying, and certain construction across large parts of *Kerala*, *Karnataka*, *Goa*, and *Maharashtra*; the Kasturirangan Committee (2013) reduced that to about 37%, privileging development aspirations. Neither report has been implemented in full; the political economy of the region has stalled both. The tension is not a failure of policy design. It is the actual shape of the problem.

The biosphere is the inherited substrate on which every Indian lives. The next chapter asks who lives on it, in what numbers, and where they have chosen — or been forced — to cluster. Chapters 10 and 11 will ask what those people do on the substrate and how they move across it. And Chapter 12 returns here: to what we are now doing back to the biosphere, and what a warming planet is laying down as the new inheritance on top of the four hotspots, ten biogeographic zones, and 91,000 animal species that the planet placed here.
