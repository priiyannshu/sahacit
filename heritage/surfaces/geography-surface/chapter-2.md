# 2 — The mountains that aren't done yet

There is a useful way to think about every landform on earth. The planet is running a ledger with two columns: forces from inside pushing material up, and forces from outside wearing it back down. The forces from inside — *endogenic forces*, driven by the earth's own geothermal heat and radioactive decay — produce mountains, plateaus, rifts, and volcanoes. They operate across wildly different time scales: an earthquake takes seconds; a collision that builds a mountain range takes tens of millions of years. The forces from outside — *exogenic forces*, driven ultimately by solar energy acting on the surface — do the wearing. *Weathering* breaks down rock in place, without moving it. *Erosion* carries the broken pieces away. *Deposition* drops them somewhere else, lower down. Every landform on earth is the instantaneous reading of this running balance. Where endogenic forces have recently outpaced exogenic ones, you get high relief, steep gradients, young rock at the surface. Where exogenic forces have had a long uninterrupted run, you get low, subdued terrain — a landscape that has been ground down toward something approaching a flat plain. Mountains are not permanent; they are the temporary winners of a contest that has no permanent winner.

The *Himalayas* are the most dramatic evidence, right now, that endogenic forces are winning. The *Aravallis* are the most instructive evidence that this never lasts.

The mode of wearing-down is not uniform. Different exogenic agents dominate in different climates. In the *Himalayas*, the most important weathering process is *frost wedging*: water seeps into cracks in rock, freezes at night, expands by about nine percent, and progressively splits the rock apart. Over generations of freeze-thaw cycles, entire cliff faces fracture into the angular rubble that fills Himalayan valley floors. In the lower plains to the south, where the climate is warm and wet, *chemical weathering* dominates — carbonic acid in rainwater dissolving minerals, hydration expanding silicate structures, oxidation rusting iron-bearing rocks into soft red laterites. The great rivers of the north work the third process: *fluvial erosion*, cutting downward into the still-rising rock faster than in any other river system on earth, carrying an enormous sediment load toward the plains. The *Ganga* alone transports roughly one billion tonnes of sediment per year. These three processes — frost in the mountains, chemical decay in the tropics, fluvial transport in between — are running simultaneously, at different altitudes, in the same watershed.

The young fold mountains of the world cluster into three roughly continuous arcs, all of them running along convergent plate boundaries where two plates collide and the crust has nowhere to go but up. The longest by far is the *Alpine-Himalayan belt*, which runs almost continuously from the *Atlas Mountains* of Morocco eastward through the *Alps* of central Europe, the *Caucasus* between the Black and Caspian Seas, the *Zagros* of Iran, the *Hindu Kush* of Afghanistan, the *Karakoram* and *Pamir* knot where those systems converge, and then the *Himalayas* proper, before bending south through *Myanmar* as the *Burmese arc* and continuing through the Indonesian archipelago. The second arc is the American *Cordilleras* — the *Rockies* running the full length of North America, handing off to the *Andes* along the western coast of South America, the world's longest continuous mountain chain. The third arc is the East Asian island chains — Japan, the Philippines, the western Pacific arcs — formed not by continent-continent collision but by the subduction of oceanic plates diving under the Asian margin. All three arcs came into existence in the last hundred million years or so; all three are still tectonically active; none of them are finished. India sits at the heart of the first and longest, at the most spectacular point of the entire Alpine-Himalayan run.

The specific story — how the Indian plate detached from *Gondwana* and spent 90 million years sprinting north across the widening *Indian Ocean*, closing the *Tethys* Sea, colliding with Eurasia around 50 million years ago, and is still pushing at roughly 5 centimetres a year — was set out in the last chapter. The task here is to look more carefully at what that collision produced, and is still producing.

The *Himalayas* run for roughly 2,400 kilometres from the great gorge of the *Indus* in the west to the great gorge of the *Brahmaputra* in the east. The clarifying fact about their structure is that they are not a single wall of rock but three or four broadly parallel ridges, stacked from north to south, each with a different age and character. The northernmost zone — sometimes grouped as the *Trans-Himalayas*, including the *Karakoram* and the *Ladakh range* — sits on the inner side of the collision. This is where the absolute altitudes peak: *K2* at 8,611 metres, *Gasherbrum*, *Broad Peak*, and the largest mass of glacial ice in the system. Then comes the *Greater Himalayas*, also called *Himadri*, the main watershed range. This is the high, nearly continuous spine that most people picture when they hear the word Himalayas — almost never below 6,000 metres on the crests, permanently snow-covered, composed largely of ancient granite that the collision has pushed upward from the Indian basement. It carries every peak in the world above 8,000 metres: *Sagarmatha* (Everest, 8,849 metres), *Kanchenjunga* (8,586 metres), and many others. *Nanga Parbat* (8,126 metres) anchors the western end of this range at the sharp syntaxis where the mountains turn; *Namcha Barwa* (7,782 metres) anchors the eastern end at the point where the range bends south. South of the Greater Himalayas lies the *Lesser Himalayas* — also called *Himachal*, or the Middle Himalayas — a range of 3,700 to 4,500 metres average altitude. This is where the famous hill stations sit, where the climate allows habitation and cultivation: *Shimla*, *Mussoorie*, *Nainital*, *Darjeeling*. The Lesser Himalayas contain three named sub-ranges the examiner tests by name: the *Pir Panjal* (the longest, running through Jammu, Kashmir, and Himachal Pradesh), the *Dhauladhar* (through Himachal), and the *Mahabharat* range (through Nepal). The *Kashmir Valley* itself — one of the most celebrated intermontane basins on earth — sits cradled between the *Pir Panjal* to the south and the *Greater Himalayas* to the north. The *Kangra Valley* and *Kullu Valley* are similar features, carved by erosion and shaped by tectonic down-warping between the thrust ridges.

The seam between the Greater and Lesser Himalayas is the *Main Central Thrust* — a major fault plane where slabs of Himalayan rock have been shoved southward over each other. The seam between the Lesser Himalayas and the southernmost range is the *Main Boundary Thrust*. These are not just names to memorise; they are where earthquakes originate, where the landscape is most actively being rearranged, where the work of the collision is most legible at human scale.

South of the Lesser Himalayas, and youngest of the ranges, are the *Shivaliks* — also called the Outer Himalayas. At 900 to 1,100 metres, these are modest hills by any Himalayan standard, and they are structurally different from what lies behind them. They are made not of ancient compressed rock but of unconsolidated sediment — gravel, sand, and clay that rivers have been washing down from the ranges above for millions of years, depositing at the front, and that the still-advancing collision zone has then picked up and folded into low ridges. The *Shivaliks* are, in a real sense, the leading edge of the mountain system still sweeping southward. Between the *Shivaliks* and the *Lesser Himalayas* lie longitudinal valleys called *Duns* — *Dehradun* is the most famous, but *Patli Dun* and others exist — where softer rock between two ridges has been carved out to make flat-floored linear valleys that gradually fill with sediment and become productive agricultural land.

Following the *Himalayas* from west to east is a journey through pronounced personality changes. In the west, the *Punjab Himalayas* and *Kashmir Himalayas* are wide — up to 400 kilometres across the full mountain system — heavily glaciated, with the densest concentrations of high peaks and glacier ice. The *Kashmir Valley* here is one of the broader intermontane basins in Asia. Moving east into *Kumaon* and *Garhwal* in Uttarakhand, the system narrows somewhat; this is the realm of *Nanda Devi* (7,816 metres, the highest summit entirely within India) and of the source glaciers of the Ganga system — *Gangotri*, *Yamunotri*, *Milam*. The Nepal Himalayas carry the range to its global apex at *Sagarmatha*, with *Kanchenjunga* marking the transition into the eastern section. In *Sikkim* and *Darjeeling*, the range becomes narrower and wetter; the vegetation changes register before the altitude does. Then comes *Arunachal Pradesh*, and at the far eastern end of the arc, the great syntaxis: the point where the Himalayan front sweeps abruptly south. Beyond this bend, the range does not end — it continues, now running north to south rather than east to west, under the name *Purvanchal*. The *Patkai* hills on the *Assam-Myanmar* border, the *Naga hills*, the *Manipur hills*, the *Mizo hills* — these are all continuations of the same fold system, the same collision front, reoriented because the plate boundary bends here. The *Purvanchal* eventually grades into the *Burmese arc* and continues south through Myanmar and Indonesia. India's eastern flank is, in this sense, geologically the same story as its northern mountains, told at a different angle.

There is one detail in the geology of the western Himalayas that I find among the most instructive in the whole syllabus. If you walk through the *Spiti* valley or cross into the high cold desert of *Ladakh*, you will find marine fossils at altitudes of four to five thousand metres. Ammonites. Sea-floor organisms that have no business being at the top of the world. They are there because the rock they inhabit was, for hundreds of millions of years, the floor of the *Tethys Sea* — sediment that accumulated in shallow warm water while India was still far to the south. When the Indian plate arrived and the *Tethys* was squeezed shut, that sea-floor sediment had nowhere to go but upward. It folded, stacked, and thrust itself into the sky. A marine fossil at 5,000 metres in *Ladakh* is a piece of ancient ocean floor that took the long way up. This is, to me, one of the most visceral single pieces of evidence for *plate tectonics* that the planet offers you for free, without a laboratory.

The same collision process has loaded the Himalayas and their western neighbours with ice on a scale that almost nothing else on earth matches. The cluster of ranges at the western end of the arc — *Karakoram*, *Hindu Kush*, *Pamir*, *Himalayas* — together holds the largest mass of glacial ice outside *Antarctica* and *Greenland*, a concentration informal enough to be called the **third pole**. The major glaciers include *Siachen* in the *Karakoram* (at roughly 76 kilometres, one of the world's longest mountain glaciers and the source of the *Nubra* river), *Hispar* and *Biafo* in the same range, *Gangotri* and *Yamunotri* in *Garhwal*, *Bara Shigri* in *Himachal Pradesh*, and *Zemu* in *Sikkim*. Glaciers do more than store water; they also shape the landscape they move through. When ice flows slowly downslope, it plucks rock from the back and sides of the hollow it occupies, carving out amphitheatre-shaped bowls called *cirques*. It abrades the rock beneath it, deepening river valleys from V-shapes into the characteristic flat-bottomed U-shapes — *U-shaped valleys* — that are among the easiest ways to identify glaciated terrain on a map or on the ground. The material the glacier carries — an unsorted mix of boulders, gravel, sand, and clay called *till* — is deposited as *moraines* when the ice retreats: lateral moraines along the sides, terminal moraines at the furthest advance, ground moraines smeared beneath. The major Himalayan valleys have all of these features layered into their walls and floors, evidence of larger past glaciations and of the ongoing work of the current ice.

These are not scenic features on a tourist map. Every major river of northern India has a partial but significant dependency on glacial meltwater: the *Indus*, the *Ganga*, the *Yamuna*, the *Brahmaputra*. The glaciers are the slow reservoir, releasing water through the summer when snowmelt from the lower ranges is running out. Agricultural calendars across the *Indo-Gangetic plains* have been calibrated, over millennia, to the rhythms of this melt. That ice is now retreating under climate change; what that retreat means for river flows across half of Asia is one of the defining questions of this century, and it belongs in the last chapter of this surface, not this one.

Still-rising mountains are also still-dangerous mountains. The *Hindu Kush–Pamir* knot at the junction of multiple converging plates is among the most seismically active zones on Earth. The entire Himalayan front sits above an active collision interface, and the earthquakes that come from it are not modest: *Bihar-Nepal 1934*, *Kashmir 2005*, *Nepal 2015* are the headline events of recent memory, but the underlying process is continuous. The hazard is not randomly distributed; it follows the geometry of the *Main Boundary Thrust* and the deep fault systems beneath the range. Compounding the seismic risk is a more intimate hazard: *glacial-lake outburst floods*, or GLOFs, in which a moraine dam holding a glacial lake suddenly fails and releases a catastrophic surge of water and debris. *Kedarnath* in 2013 is the touchstone — the failure of a moraine above the *Mandakini* valley sent a wall of water and rock through the pilgrimage town in hours, killing several thousand people. The mountains, the rivers, the glaciers, and the population living between them are, in this specific sense, still negotiating the terms of a collision that is not finished.

The *Aravallis* provide the closing perspective. Running for about 800 kilometres from near Delhi southwest to *Palanpur* in *Gujarat*, these are fold mountains made by the same physics — convergent collision, compressional forces, *diastrophism* — as the *Himalayas*. But they began rising in the *Precambrian*, somewhere between two and three billion years ago. The exogenic forces have had a very long time to work on them. *Guru Shikhar* on *Mount Abu*, the highest point of the entire *Aravalli* system today, stands at 1,722 metres — less than a quarter of what the *Himalayas* reach and a small fraction of what the *Aravallis* once were, when they stood perhaps as tall as the *Himalayas* stand now. The difference between the two ranges is not physics. It is time. The *Aravallis* are what weathering does to a mountain, given enough centuries.

The *Himalayas* will, eventually, go the same way. For now, though, they are still building, still seismically violent, still feeding the greatest river systems of Asia. They are one face of a three-faced inheritance. The plain that their rivers have been building south of them, and the ancient plateau the Indian plate carried from Gondwana, and the coasts and islands that the meeting of land and sea produced — those are the other two. The next chapter maps them all.
