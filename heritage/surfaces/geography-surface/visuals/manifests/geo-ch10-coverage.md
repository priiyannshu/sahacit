## Concepts (in narrative order)

### Economic Activity Framework

1. **economic-activity-classification** — Colin Clark's Five-Sector Framework
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch05_primary_activities:geo_activity_classification
   What: Primary (extract from nature — farming, mining, fishing, forestry, herding), Secondary (transform raw materials — manufacturing, construction, processing), Tertiary (services facilitating exchange — trade, transport, banking, education, healthcare), Quaternary (knowledge-intensive services — IT, research, consultancy, information processing), Quinary (apex decision-making — top corporate leadership, senior government, policy-setting bodies). Every economy has all five; what distinguishes developed from developing is the mix — the share of workforce and output each level commands.

2. **india-gdp-workforce-inversion** — India's GDP-Workforce Structural Inversion
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch05_primary_activities:geo_india_economic_structure
   What: ~45% of India's workforce is in primary activities (overwhelmingly agriculture) contributing only ~15% of GDP. Services (tertiary + quaternary) contribute more than half of GDP while employing only ~30% of the workforce. The inversion — most workers in lowest-productivity sector, most output from highest — is not a recent policy failure. It is an inheritance from India's particular industrialisation path: the Green Revolution achieved food security without equivalent labour absorption into more productive work.

### Farming Systems

3. **intensive-subsistence-farming** — Intensive Subsistence Farming
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch05_primary_activities:geo_intensive_subsistence
   What: Dominant in monsoon Asia. Small holdings, very high labour input per hectare, very high yield per hectare, wet paddy as archetype. Terraced rice paddies of eastern India, river deltas of West Bengal, lowlands of Southeast Asia — humanity's most labour-dense use of land, feeding more people per unit area than almost any other system. The foundation of Indian agriculture in its wet zones.

4. **shifting-cultivation** — Shifting Cultivation (Extensive Subsistence)
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch05_primary_activities:geo_shifting_cultivation
   What: Large areas, low labour density, land cleared, farmed briefly, then abandoned and rested on a multi-year rotation. Called jhum in northeast India, milpa in Central America, ladang in Indonesia. Functions at low population density; as density rises and rotation cycles compress, the system degrades and forest cover collapses. Still practiced in northeast India's hill states.

5. **commercial-farming-global** — Commercial Farming Types (Global)
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch05_primary_activities:geo_commercial_farming
   What: Intensive mixed farming of northwestern Europe (crops + livestock, capital-heavy, moderate holdings, crop rotation). Grain belt extensive farming of semi-arid mid-latitudes (wheat prairies of North America, Argentine pampas, Australian interior — enormous volumes, minimal labour because mechanisation substitutes). Mediterranean agriculture (citrus, olives, grapes, dry summers and mild wet winters — California, Chile, Mediterranean basin, absent from India). India fits mostly into intensive subsistence with commercial wheat belts in the northwest and plantation pockets in the highland south.

6. **plantation-agriculture** — Plantation Agriculture
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch05_primary_activities:geo_plantation_agriculture
   What: Large single-crop estates organised around export and cheap labour — the colonial legacy. Tea in Assam and Sri Lanka, rubber in Malaysia, coffee in Brazil. India inherited this overlay in its hillier peripheries: tea estates of Darjeeling, the Nilgiris, and cardamom hills of Kerala. Still visible as large-scale organised sector in contrast to India's sea of small peasant holdings.

### Indian Farming Calendar

7. **india-farming-seasons** — India's Three Crop Seasons (Kharif, Rabi, Zaid)
   Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_crop_seasons
   What: Kharif — opens when monsoon arrives (~June-July), harvested September-October: rice, jowar, bajra, maize, cotton, sugarcane, jute, soybean, groundnut. Rabi — sown October-November using post-monsoon soil moisture, harvested March-April: wheat, barley, gram, peas, mustard, linseed. Zaid — short irrigated summer March to June: watermelon, cucumber, vegetables, fodder. The three-season structure is the monsoon translated directly into agronomy.

### Crop Geography of India

8. **rice-geography-india** — Rice Geography of India
   Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_rice_production
   What: Occupies ~25% of India's cropped area. Needs heavy rainfall or reliable irrigation; grows best on alluvial or clayey moisture-retaining soils. West Bengal is largest producer — three crops annually in the Brahmaputra delta and Gangetic Bengal; a wet-zone advantage no other state matches. Uttar Pradesh produces large volumes. Punjab produces less but exports far more through the MSP-procurement system. Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Bihar also feature. Eastern and southern wet zones are rice territory; the northwest is not, except where canal irrigation carries water to an otherwise unsuitable climate.

9. **wheat-geography-india** — Wheat Geography of India
   Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_wheat_production
   What: Rabi counterpart. Dominant in the Indo-Gangetic plain from Punjab through Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, into Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh. Needs a cool growing season, bright sunshine at ripening, and well-drained loamy soils — conditions the Punjab-Haryana canal-irrigated belt provides almost perfectly. Uttar Pradesh is largest producer by volume; Punjab and Haryana achieve highest yields per hectare. The Green Revolution arrived here first, and the reasons for that geographic concentration are structural.

10. **pulses-geography-india** — Pulses Geography of India
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_pulses_production
    What: Gram, tur, urad, moong — India's protein layer. India is both world's largest producer AND world's largest importer because demand consistently outstrips supply from the rainfed marginal-soil zones where pulses thrive. Madhya Pradesh is largest producer; Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, and Karnataka follow. The structural protein gap is real and persistent — a food-security vulnerability that has not been resolved.

11. **cotton-black-soil-belt** — Cotton and the Black Soil Belt
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_cotton_production
    What: Cotton sits in the Deccan black-soil belt. Regur (black cotton soil) — the product of weathered Deccan basalt — is moisture-retaining, mineral-rich, exactly suited to cotton. Maharashtra, Gujarat, Telangana, and Karnataka are the heart of this belt. The textile industry that grew around it was not placed arbitrarily: raw material proximity follows directly from Weber's logic applied before Weber formalised it.

12. **sugarcane-geography-india** — Sugarcane Geography of India
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_sugarcane_production
    What: Needs heat, moisture, and rich loamy soils. Uttar Pradesh dominates by mill count and total output; Maharashtra leads in yield per hectare and has been gaining share because its longer crushing season is agronomically more efficient. Together they account for roughly two-thirds of national production. Textbook Weberian constraint: sugarcane is bulky and perishable (must be crushed within 24 hours of cutting), so sugar mills cannot be located far from cane fields — and they are not.

13. **plantation-crops-geography** — Plantation Crops Geography (Jute, Tea, Coffee, Rubber)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_plantation_crops
    What: Jute concentrated in the Brahmaputra delta and West Bengal's Gangetic delta — moisture-loving and labour-intensive. Tea almost entirely in Assam (over half of national output), Darjeeling (small volume, extraordinary market premium), and the Nilgiris of Tamil Nadu and Kerala. Coffee geographically most concentrated: nearly all from southern Western Ghats — Karnataka (especially Coorg), Kerala, Tamil Nadu — hill-slope microclimates and laterite soils suit Arabica variety. Kerala holds over 90% of India's natural rubber — heavy rainfall, laterite soils, plantation tradition from colonial economy maintained since.

14. **horticulture-india** — Horticulture Geography of India
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_horticulture
    What: India is world's largest producer of mango and banana, and second-largest of vegetables and fruit overall. The Deccan, Karnataka, and coastal south are primary fruit zones. Mango belt extends across UP, Bihar, AP, Karnataka. Banana belt is strongest in Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, AP. Horticulture adds a high-value layer to the agricultural map distinct from the staple-crop substrate.

### Agrarian Structure

15. **india-land-use-stats** — India's Land Use Statistics
    Source: ncert — 12th_p1_ch05_land_resources_and_agriculture:geo_land_use
    What: Of India's total geographic area: ~46% under net sown area, ~22% forests (by legal classification), fallow, degraded land, and non-agricultural uses account for the rest — the last category growing fastest as urbanisation consumes peri-urban farmland. Cropping intensity — ratio of gross cropped area to net sown area — stands around 140, meaning a given piece of cultivated land is cropped ~1.4 times per year. India lags both China and Bangladesh; the main lever for raising it is irrigation expansion.

16. **landholding-small-marginal** — India's Small and Marginal Landholding Problem
    Source: ncert — 12th_p1_ch05_land_resources_and_agriculture:geo_landholding_structure
    What: Average Indian farm ~1.08 hectares; 86% of India's farmers are small and marginal (under 2 hectares). This is accumulated inheritance from partition across generations under Hindu joint-family norms plus weak policy incentives for consolidation. Small farms can be productive — they cannot easily absorb mechanisation, access formal credit, or survive a bad monsoon year. The structural ceiling on agricultural productivity is partly this fragmentation.

### Agricultural Revolutions

17. **green-revolution** — The Green Revolution (1960s–70s)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch04_agriculture:geo_green_revolution
    What: Norman Borlaug's semi-dwarf high-yielding wheat varieties adapted to Indian conditions by M.S. Swaminathan, rolled out alongside a full package: chemical fertilisers, assured irrigation, extension services, and MSP procurement guarantees backed by the Food Corporation of India (established 1965). India went from near-famine in 1965 to wheat self-sufficiency by the early 1970s. But geography matters enormously: the Green Revolution concentrated in Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh — not Bihar, Odisha, or the eastern plains. The reason is the canal-irrigated inheritance: the Punjab-Haryana belt had the water infrastructure to support the technology package; the east did not.

18. **green-revolution-costs** — Green Revolution — Geographic Concentration and Its Costs
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:green-revolution-unit
    What: Punjab and Haryana, with a fraction of India's farmers, capture a disproportionate share of MSP-procurement benefits — a direct consequence of geographic concentration. Costs are now visible: Punjab's water table in sustained decline from decades of over-extraction for paddy; soil salinity rising; fertiliser-subsidy distortions politically difficult to unwind. The Green Revolution's geography is one of India's most load-bearing inheritances, in both its gifts and its liabilities.

19. **white-revolution** — White Revolution — Operation Flood
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:agricultural-revolutions-unit
    What: Organised under Verghese Kurien and the cooperative dairy model centred on Anand in Gujarat. Connected milk producers in small towns and villages to urban markets through a chilling-and-processing network. India became world's largest milk producer. Structurally different from the Green Revolution — it spread benefits broadly rather than concentrating them geographically. The National Dairy Development Board and Amul model became templates for cooperative processing in other sectors.

20. **blue-yellow-revolutions** — Blue and Yellow Revolutions
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:agricultural-revolutions-unit
    What: Blue Revolution — extended Operation Flood logic to fisheries: coastal aquaculture and inland tank fisheries. India became second-largest fish producer globally. Yellow Revolution — oilseeds drive. India still imports more than 60% of its edible oil, a dependency that sits unresolved; the Yellow Revolution was far less successful than green or white. The oilseed gap is a persistent food-security and forex-drain problem.

### Industrial Location Theory

21. **weber-industrial-location** — Weber's Industrial Location Theory (1909)
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch06_secondary_activities:geo_weber_theory
    What: Alfred Weber's 1909 framework: a manufacturer locates where total cost is minimised, balancing transport to raw materials, transport to markets, labour costs, and agglomeration economies (cluster benefits of shared infrastructure, labour pools, supply chains). Weight-losing industries (raw materials heavier than final product — iron ore and coking coal into steel) locate near raw materials. Market-oriented industries locate near consumers. Footloose industries cluster around skilled labour and connectivity. This logic still broadly holds, though globalisation has introduced labour-cost arbitrage at continental scale.

22. **world-industrial-regions** — World's Major Industrial Regions
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch06_secondary_activities:geo_world_industrial_regions
    What: Historical coalfield-anchored regions: NE USA and Great Lakes belt (Pittsburgh, Chicago); Ruhr of Germany; Lombardy in northern Italy; Moscow-Volga axis in Russia; Pacific belt of Japan (Tokyo-Yokohama through Osaka-Kobe). Globalisation since 1980s has reshuffled the map: Yangtze delta, Pearl River delta, and Bohai Bay region in China now form the world's densest manufacturing zone. Old Ruhr and NE USA rustbelts have contracted. Geography of global manufacturing now follows labour costs and policy arbitrage as much as raw materials.

### India's Industrial Geography

23. **india-mumbai-pune-cluster** — Mumbai-Pune Industrial Cluster
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch06_manufacturing_industries:geo_mumbai_pune_industry
    What: Oldest and most diversified cluster: colonial-era deep-water port, proximity to the Deccan cotton belt, early capital accumulation through colonial trade, skilled urban labour tradition. Cotton textile mills from the 1850s, then petrochemicals, automobiles, and financial services. Jamnagar in Gujarat hosts the Reliance refinery — the world's largest single-site oil-refining complex.

24. **india-hugli-industrial-region** — Hugli Industrial Region
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch06_manufacturing_industries:geo_hugli_region
    What: Grew around jute (West Bengal produces nearly three-quarters of India's raw jute) and colonial port trade, adding engineering and leather industries. Post-partition and post-1947 policy shifts drained investment and the region has not recovered its earlier share. A reminder that industrial geography is not permanent — it can be reversed by policy and infrastructure choices made elsewhere.

25. **india-chotanagpur-steel-belt** — Chotanagpur Steel Belt (Jharkhand-Odisha-Chhattisgarh)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch06_manufacturing_industries:geo_chotanagpur_industry
    What: Weber's logic most purely expressed: iron ore from Odisha and Chhattisgarh, coking coal from Jharkhand — located close enough to minimise transport cost. Jamshedpur holds TISCO (Tata Iron and Steel Company), Asia's first integrated private steel plant, founded 1907. Public-sector plants added in the Nehru-era drive: Bhilai (Soviet collaboration, 1955), Rourkela (German collaboration), Durgapur (British), Bokaro (Soviet). Visakhapatnam and Salem serve the coast and south.

26. **india-bangalore-chennai-coimbatore** — Bengaluru-Chennai-Coimbatore Industrial Belt
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch06_manufacturing_industries:geo_south_india_industry
    What: Three distinct nodes. Chennai — automobile assembly hub (Hyundai, Nissan-Renault, BMW, Daimler); port, engineering labour force, and logistics position; sometimes called the Detroit of India. Coimbatore — textile and precision engineering manufacturing from the colonial period. Bengaluru — post-1947 public sector (HAL, ISRO, Indian Telephone Industries, IISc) built a pool of technically educated workers; when software-export liberalisation arrived in the late 1990s, Bengaluru had exactly the inputs a footloose knowledge industry needed. IT concentrated here not because of coal or iron ore but because of education clusters and international airports.

27. **india-gujarat-corridor** — Gujarat Industrial Corridor
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch06_manufacturing_industries:geo_gujarat_industry
    What: Ahmedabad-Vadodara-Surat-Jamnagar corridor connects cotton textile (historically the anchor), chemicals, petrochemicals, and refining. Surat is also one of the world's dominant diamond-cutting and polishing centres — a craft-scale industry that grew into a global monopoly through accumulated skill and community networks. Gujarat's mix of old-economy (cotton, chemicals) and new (diamond, petroleum) makes it one of India's most industrially diversified states.

28. **india-msme-sector** — Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs)
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:msme-unit
    What: ~63 million units, contributing roughly 30% of manufacturing GDP, employing over 110 million people. Geography is diffuse — reaching almost every district, not anchored to mineral access. Specialised clusters demonstrate agglomeration at small scale, built from craft inheritance and accumulated skill: Tirupur (knitwear), Surat (synthetic textiles and diamonds), Moradabad (brassware), Firozabad (glass), Jaipur (gems and jewellery), Ludhiana (hosiery and bicycles), Aligarh (locks). Khadi and Village Industries Commission maintains a further layer of village-scale production.

### Services Geography

29. **india-it-sector-geography** — IT Sector Geography
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch06_manufacturing_industries:geo_it_sector
    What: Bengaluru leads India's IT exports, followed by Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Noida, and Gurgaon, with secondary hubs in Kochi, Coimbatore, and Bhubaneswar. The Bengaluru story is the clearest illustration of how post-independence policy choices (public-sector R&D investments) compounded over decades can become geographic inheritance — a footloose knowledge industry that clustered around pre-existing human capital, not minerals.

30. **india-financial-governance-geography** — Financial and Governance Services Geography
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch07_tertiary_and_quaternary_activities:geo_services_geography
    What: Banking and finance disproportionately concentrated in Mumbai — Reserve Bank, Bombay Stock Exchange, National Stock Exchange, and headquarters of most major banks sit within a few kilometres of each other. Quinary governance layer concentrates in Delhi. Trade and retail reach everywhere, density tracking population and purchasing power. Services geography is harder to map cleanly than industry because services, unlike steel, are not anchored to mineral outcrops.
