## Concepts (in narrative order)

### Biosphere — System Framing

1. **biosphere** — Biosphere
   Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch15_life_on_the_earth:geo_biosphere
   What: Thin zone (~20 km) where lithosphere, hydrosphere, and atmosphere overlap and sustain life. Term coined by Eduard Suess (1875). Life is a geological force — it rerouted rivers, transformed the atmosphere, and locked dead organisms into fossil fuels.

2. **ecosystem** — Ecosystem
   Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch15_life_on_the_earth:geo_ecosystem
   What: Functional unit where living organisms (biotic) interact with their physical surroundings (abiotic: soil, water, temperature, light, nutrients) as a working system. Term coined by Arthur Tansley (1935). Energy enters from the Sun; nutrients cycle within the system.

### Energy Flow

3. **food-chain-web** — Food Chain and Food Web
   Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch15_life_on_the_earth:geo_food_chain
   What: Producers (photosynthesising organisms) fix solar energy → primary consumers (herbivores) → secondary consumers (carnivores) → tertiary consumers → decomposers (fungi, bacteria) return nutrients. Real ecosystems are food webs (multiple pathways), not linear chains; webs are resilient to single-species loss, chains are not.

4. **photosynthesis** — Photosynthesis
   Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch15_life_on_the_earth:geo_photosynthesis
   What: Process by which green plants, phytoplankton, and other producers capture solar energy and fix it into organic matter. The single entry point for energy into most ecosystems; primary productivity determines the ceiling for every trophic level above.

5. **ten-percent-rule** — Ten Per Cent Rule (Ecological Energy Transfer)
   Source: writer-ai (pending: majid-india)
   What: Approximately 10% of energy at any trophic level passes to the next; 90% is lost as heat, metabolism, or unavailable matter. Consequence: producers are abundant, apex predators are always rare. An ecological pyramid of energy is always upright — you cannot have more energy at the top than you put in at the bottom. (Lindeman, 1942.)
   enhancement_target: true

6. **ecological-pyramid** — Ecological Pyramid of Energy
   Source: writer-ai
   What: Diagrammatic representation of trophic levels showing energy, biomass, or numbers. The energy pyramid is always upright (10% rule). Explains why Bengal tigers, snow leopards, and Asiatic lions exist in small restricted populations — apex predators can only be rare.
   enhancement_target: true

### Matter Cycling — Biogeochemical Cycles

7. **nutrient-cycle** — Nutrient Cycling
   Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch15_life_on_the_earth:geo_nutrient_cycle
   What: Nutrients do not leave the ecosystem; they circulate between organisms and the abiotic environment in biogeochemical cycles. Unlike energy (which flows in and is lost as heat), matter is conserved and reused. Carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycles are the primary ones.

8. **carbon-cycle** — Carbon Cycle
   Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch15_life_on_the_earth:geo_carbon_cycle
   What: Plants absorb atmospheric CO₂ via photosynthesis and fix it into organic matter; respiration, decomposition, and combustion release it back. Fossil fuels are carbon locked in the lithosphere over hundreds of millions of years; burning them adds it back faster than the natural cycle can absorb. (Full climate consequence: ch12.)

9. **nitrogen-cycle** — Nitrogen Cycle
   Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch15_life_on_the_earth:geo_nitrogen_cycle
   What: Atmospheric N₂ fixed into biologically usable forms by soil bacteria (Rhizobium in legume root nodules), lightning, and the industrial Haber-Bosch process; returned to atmosphere by denitrification. Human disruption via fertiliser runoff drives eutrophication in lakes and coastal waters — algal blooms deplete oxygen and kill aquatic life.

10. **phosphorus-cycle** — Phosphorus Cycle
    Source: writer-ai (pending: majid-india)
    What: Slowest of the major cycles; no atmospheric reservoir — limited by the rate at which rocks weather. World's accessible phosphate deposits concentrated in a few countries (Morocco, China, Russia). An agricultural bottleneck increasingly visible as demand for fertiliser grows and known reserves concentrate geographically.
    enhancement_target: true

### Biodiversity

11. **biodiversity** — Biodiversity — Three Levels
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_biodiversity
    What: Variety of life at three simultaneously important levels: genetic diversity (gene variation within a species), species diversity (count of distinct species), and ecosystem diversity (variety of habitat types). Conservation must address all three; losing an ecosystem type can eliminate hundreds of species simultaneously.

12. **genetic-diversity** — Genetic Diversity
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_genetic_diversity
    What: Variation of genes within a species; the raw material of adaptation and plant breeding. India's 50,000-200,000 traditional rice landraces represent a genetic reservoir built over millennia of varied microclimates. Standardising to high-yield varieties narrows the genetic capital breeders will need when the next disease or climate shift arrives.

13. **species-diversity** — Species Diversity
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_species_diversity
    What: Count of distinct species in a given area. Species richness follows a gradient from equator to poles (more species per unit area in the tropics). India holds ~91,000 described animal species and ~46,000 plant species — approximately 8% of all known species on 2.4% of the planet's land.

14. **ecosystem-diversity** — Ecosystem Diversity
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_ecosystem_diversity
    What: Variety of habitat types — wetlands, mangroves, alpine meadows, coral reefs, deciduous forests — each with its own assemblage and set of services. Lose a habitat type and you lose hundreds of species simultaneously, including many not yet identified.

15. **species-richness-gradient** — Latitudinal Gradient in Species Richness
    Source: writer-ai (pending: majid-india)
    What: More species per unit area in tropical regions, fewer toward higher latitudes. Explanatory hypotheses: greater solar energy input, greater climatic stability over geological time (tropics were never glaciated), more time for speciation, the species-area relationship, and the intermediate disturbance hypothesis. India's biodiversity reflects this tropical placement combined with its Gondwana-Asia biogeographic inheritance.
    enhancement_target: true

### Biodiversity Hotspots

16. **megadiverse-countries** — Megadiverse Countries
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:biodiversity-unit
    What: 17 countries defined by UNEP's World Conservation Monitoring Centre as holding ~70% of the world's biodiversity. India is on the list alongside Brazil, Indonesia, Colombia, Ecuador, China, Mexico, Australia, and others. A broad frame — biodiversity hotspot concept is the sharper tool.

17. **biodiversity-hotspot** — Biodiversity Hotspot (Norman Myers)
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_biodiversity_hotspot
    What: Defined by Norman Myers (1988, refined 2000): a region must contain ≥1,500 endemic vascular plant species AND have lost >70% of original primary vegetation. Currently 36 hotspots globally, covering ~2.4% of Earth's land but harbouring ~50% of plant endemics and ~60% of terrestrial vertebrate endemics. Moral argument: concentrate conservation where you cannot afford to lose what only these places contain.

18. **endemic-species** — Endemic Species
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_endemic_species
    What: Species found nowhere else — their existence is tied to a single landscape. India's endemism: Asiatic lion (Gir only), one-horned rhinoceros (Brahmaputra plains only), Nilgiri tahr (Western Ghats only), Indian wild ass (Rann of Kutch only). If the landscape goes, the species goes.

19. **western-ghats-hotspot** — Western Ghats – Sri Lanka Hotspot
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:biodiversity-unit
    What: One of the world's eight "hottest hotspots." Sahyadri system running along India's southwestern coast, continuing into highland Sri Lanka. ~5,000 plant species; 192 endemic freshwater fish species in the Western Ghats alone; lion-tailed macaque, Nilgiri tahr, shola grassland-forest mosaics above 1,500 m that exist nowhere else.

20. **eastern-himalaya-hotspot** — Eastern Himalaya Hotspot
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:biodiversity-unit
    What: Encompasses Bhutan, northeastern India, parts of Nepal. Abrupt altitudinal rise from Brahmaputra valley floors to Himalayan peaks compresses biomes so densely that this region has one of the highest concentrations of plant species in the world. Red panda, clouded leopard, takin, hundreds of orchid species.

21. **indo-burma-hotspot** — Indo-Burma Hotspot
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:biodiversity-unit
    What: Extends across northeastern India south of the Brahmaputra through Myanmar, Thailand, and Indochina. India's contribution: hoolock gibbon (India's only ape), Irrawaddy dolphin in northeastern river systems, several endemic turtle species.

22. **sundaland-hotspot** — Sundaland Hotspot (Nicobar Islands)
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:biodiversity-unit
    What: Reaches India only at the Nicobar Islands. Hosts the Nicobar megapode, leatherback turtle nesting beaches, and species assemblages more closely related to Southeast Asia than to the Indian mainland — a biological signature of the Sundaland plate's distinct geological origin.

23. **biogeographic-zones** — Rodgers and Panwar — 10 Biogeographic Zones of India
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:biodiversity-unit
    What: Working in 1988, Rodgers and Panwar divided India into 10 zones: Trans-Himalaya (Ladakh high-altitude cold desert), Himalaya (mountain chain NW to NE), Indian Desert (west of Aravallis), Semi-Arid (Punjab plains transition), Western Ghats, Deccan Peninsula, Gangetic Plain, North-East India (non-Himalayan hill ranges), Coasts (eastern + western + Lakshadweep), Islands (Andaman and Nicobar). Framework designed to serve as the geographic skeleton for the protected area network.

### Biodiversity Loss — Drivers

24. **biodiversity-threats** — Five Drivers of Biodiversity Loss (IPBES Evil Quintet)
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_biodiversity_threats
    What: IPBES 2019 Global Assessment: (1) habitat loss and fragmentation (largest driver — deforestation, wetland drainage, agricultural expansion, urbanisation); (2) overexploitation (hunting, poaching, overfishing); (3) pollution (nitrogen/phosphorus runoff degrades aquatic habitats); (4) invasive alien species; (5) climate change.

25. **invasive-alien-species** — Invasive Alien Species
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_invasive_species
    What: Organisms transported across natural barriers by human activity. India cases: Lantana camara (dominates understorey of vast Indian forest tracts, reduces prey habitat); water hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes, chokes Loktak, Chilika, and other lakes); Prosopis juliflora (replaced native vegetation across Thar and semi-arid zones).

26. **eutrophication** — Eutrophication
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Nutrient (primarily nitrogen and phosphorus) runoff from agricultural fertilisers triggers explosive algal growth in water bodies; decomposition of algae depletes dissolved oxygen, creating dead zones hostile to aquatic life. A direct consequence of the disrupted nitrogen cycle from Haber-Bosch and fertiliser overuse.
    enhancement_target: true

### Species Conservation Status

27. **iucn-red-list** — IUCN Red List
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_iucn
    What: Global inventory of extinction risk maintained by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (founded 1948, headquartered in Gland, Switzerland). Categories: Not Evaluated → Data Deficient → Least Concern → Near Threatened → Vulnerable → Endangered → Critically Endangered → Extinct in the Wild → Extinct. India: Bengal tiger (Endangered), Indian elephant (Endangered), snow leopard (Vulnerable), great Indian bustard (Critically Endangered), gharial (Critically Endangered).

28. **endangered-species** — Endangered Species — India's Flagship Cases
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_endangered_species
    What: Asiatic lion (Gir, Gujarat): 20 individuals in 1900, recovered to 600+, but single-location vulnerability. One-horned rhinoceros: Kaziranga and Manas (Assam) and nowhere else on Earth. Great Indian bustard: Thar and Rajasthan grasslands, Critically Endangered. Gharial: Chambal and Girwa rivers, ~700 individuals today from ~10,000 in 1940s. Vultures: white-backed, long-billed, slender-billed crashed >95% in 1990s from veterinary diclofenac.

### Constitutional and Statutory Framework

29. **article-48a-51ag** — Constitutional Anchors — Articles 48A and 51A(g)
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:environmental-acts-unit
    What: Article 48A (Directive Principle, 42nd Amendment 1976): state must protect and improve the environment, safeguard forests and wildlife. Article 51A(g) (Fundamental Duty, same amendment): every citizen must protect and improve the natural environment. Neither directly enforceable in court, but courts use both as interpretive tools in environmental litigation. Jurisdiction: Concurrent List (Article 246) — both Parliament and states may legislate.

30. **wildlife-protection-act-1972** — Wildlife Protection Act 1972
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:environmental-acts-unit
    What: Foundational statute passed same year as Stockholm UN Conference on Human Environment. Created the schedule system for protected species (Schedule I = highest protection), established the framework for national parks and wildlife sanctuaries, gave states authority to notify protected areas. Indira Gandhi's environmentalism landmark.

31. **forest-conservation-act-1980** — Forest Conservation Act 1980
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:environmental-acts-unit
    What: Ended the earlier practice of state governments diverting forest land for non-forest use (mining, dams, industry) without central approval. After 1980, any such diversion requires central government permission. Key check on ad-hoc forest loss driven by development.

32. **environment-protection-act-1986** — Environment Protection Act 1986
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:environmental-acts-unit
    What: Passed in aftermath of Bhopal gas tragedy (1984); gave the Centre sweeping rule-making powers over environmental standards. Umbrella law under which hundreds of environmental regulations have since been issued.

33. **biological-diversity-act-2002** — Biological Diversity Act 2002
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:environmental-acts-unit
    What: Implemented India's obligations under the Convention on Biological Diversity. Created the National Biodiversity Authority (centre), State Biodiversity Boards (states), and Biodiversity Management Committees (local level). Introduced access-and-benefit-sharing framework: foreign entities seeking access to India's biological resources must obtain approval and share benefits with local communities.

34. **forest-rights-act-2006** — Forest Rights Act 2006
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:environmental-acts-unit
    What: Acknowledged that Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers had been dispossessed of customary rights by colonial forest enclosure. Granted individual and community forest rights — restitution of a historical injustice. Created structural tension with WPA 1972's inviolate core zones; that tension is a live, unresolved feature of Indian conservation law.

35. **campa-2016** — CAMPA (Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act 2016)
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:environmental-acts-unit
    What: Created a mechanism to collect and deploy funds when forest land is diverted: the developer must fund compensatory afforestation elsewhere, through an accountability structure designed to unlock money that had historically accumulated unspent in state treasuries.

### Protected Area Network

36. **national-parks-india** — National Parks — India's Network
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_national_park
    What: 106 National Parks — most strictly protected category: no grazing, forestry, cultivation, or permanent human habitation; boundaries fixed by legislation. Jim Corbett (Uttarakhand, 1936, India's first). Kaziranga (Assam, World Heritage Site, largest one-horned rhinoceros population). Sundarbans (West Bengal, largest mangrove forest on Earth). Gir (Gujarat, sole wild refuge of Asiatic lion). Periyar (Kerala, Western Ghats elephants).

37. **wildlife-sanctuaries** — Wildlife Sanctuaries and Conservation Reserves
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_wildlife_sanctuary
    What: Over 566 wildlife sanctuaries — allow more human activity than national parks, more flexible boundaries. Conservation Reserves and Community Reserves (2003 amendment) provide lighter-touch protection for buffer landscapes and community-managed areas.

38. **biosphere-reserves** — Biosphere Reserves
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_biosphere_reserve
    What: 18 in India, 12 in UNESCO's World Network of Biosphere Reserves. Concentric-zone model: core (strictly protected) → buffer → transition. Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve (1986, India's first, largest). UNESCO-listed: Nanda Devi, Gulf of Mannar, Nokrek, Manas, Sundarbans.

39. **in-situ-conservation** — In-situ Conservation
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_in_situ_conservation
    What: Conservation within the natural habitat — protected areas, biosphere reserves, national parks, wildlife sanctuaries. Protects species AND the ecosystem they depend on. More effective than ex-situ for maintaining ecological interactions and genetic diversity of wild populations.

40. **ex-situ-conservation** — Ex-situ Conservation
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_ex_situ_conservation
    What: Conservation outside natural habitat — seed banks, botanical gardens, zoological parks, gene banks, tissue culture. Safety net for species when in-situ protection fails; does not preserve ecological relationships. India: National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (New Delhi) holds one of the world's largest seed banks.

### Flagship Species Programmes

41. **project-tiger** — Project Tiger (1973)
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:wildlife-programmes-unit
    What: Launched 1973; now 54+ Tiger Reserves with strictly protected core and managed buffer zones. Tiger count: ~1,800 in 1973 → 3,682 in 2022 (all-India census). India holds >75% of the world's wild tigers — an unambiguous conservation success. Side effect: tigers expanding into human-dominated landscapes, sharpening human-wildlife conflict in corridor districts of MP, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Uttarakhand foothills.

42. **project-elephant** — Project Elephant (1992)
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:wildlife-programmes-unit
    What: 33+ Elephant Reserves, focused on maintaining corridors between fragmented forest patches. Asian elephant requires unbroken habitat connectivity over hundreds of kilometres that no single reserve can provide; the corridor is the conservation unit. Human-elephant conflict is its own dimension of the challenge.

43. **ramsar-sites-india** — Ramsar Wetland Sites — India
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_ramsar_convention
    What: India has 80+ designated Ramsar sites. Key ones: Chilika Lake (Odisha, largest coastal lagoon in Asia), Loktak Lake (Manipur), Wular Lake (Kashmir), Keoladeo (Rajasthan), Sambhar Salt Lake (Rajasthan). India joined the Ramsar Convention in 1982.

44. **project-snow-leopard** — Project Snow Leopard (2009)
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:wildlife-programmes-unit
    What: Focuses on the Trans-Himalayan zone (Ladakh, Spiti, Lahaul, upper Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Arunachal). Snow leopard is Vulnerable (IUCN). Conservation challenge: vast high-altitude range across multiple countries; pastoralist-predator conflict over livestock.

45. **vulture-diclofenac-case** — Vulture Collapse and Diclofenac Ban
    Source: writer-ai
    What: In the 1990s, three Indian vulture species (white-backed, long-billed, slender-billed) crashed >95% in under a decade. Cause identified: veterinary drug diclofenac — vultures eating carcasses of treated livestock suffered fatal kidney failure within days. India banned veterinary diclofenac in 2006. Vulture-Safe Zones established. Recovery is slow (vultures take 4-5 years to reach breeding age) but the collapse has been arrested. One of the fastest regulatory responses to a documented wildlife crisis in India's history.
    enhancement_target: true

46. **olive-ridley-arribada** — Olive Ridley Sea Turtle — Arribada at Gahirmatha
    Source: writer-ai (pending: majid-india)
    What: Olive Ridley turtles mass-nest (arribada) at Gahirmatha beach (Odisha) in one of the world's largest such aggregations. Sea Turtle Conservation programmes protect nesting beaches from light pollution, fishing gear, and human disturbance.
    enhancement_target: true

### International Legal Architecture

47. **cbd** — Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_cbd
    What: Opened at the Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit (1992). Three pillars: conservation of biodiversity, sustainable use of its components, equitable sharing of benefits from genetic resources. The access-and-benefit-sharing framework addressed the North-South asymmetry: biodiversity exists mostly in the South, biotechnology industry sits mostly in the North.

48. **cartagena-protocol** — Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety (2000)
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Additional agreement to the CBD dealing with living modified organisms (genetically engineered organisms) and their international trade. India ratified in 2003.
    enhancement_target: true

49. **nagoya-protocol** — Nagoya Protocol (2010)
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Strengthened CBD's access-and-benefit-sharing provisions: genetic resources must be accessed with prior informed consent; benefits must be shared fairly with the country of origin. In force 2014. India ratified in 2014.
    enhancement_target: true

50. **cites** — CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species)
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_cites
    What: 1973 convention regulating international trade in wildlife through three appendices. Appendix I: bans commercial trade in most threatened species (tigers, elephants, rhinoceros). Appendix II: regulates and monitors trade in species that could become threatened. Appendix III: species protected in at least one country.

51. **ramsar-convention** — Ramsar Convention on Wetlands (1971)
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch16_biodiversity_and_conservation:geo_ramsar_convention
    What: Signed in Ramsar, Iran (1971). Predates the CBD; focuses specifically on wetland conservation and wise use. India joined 1982. Defines wetlands broadly; membership obligates countries to designate Ramsar sites and maintain their ecological character.

52. **ipbes** — IPBES (Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity)
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Established 2012 as the biodiversity equivalent of the IPCC — provides the global science-policy interface for biodiversity assessments. 2019 Global Assessment (the "evil quintet" report) is its landmark document. Not a UN organ directly; intergovernmental body with UN facilitation.
    enhancement_target: true

53. **kunming-montreal-gbf** — Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022)
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Adopted at CBD COP 15 (2022). Succeeded the Aichi Biodiversity Targets (set COP 10, 2010; expired 2020 largely unmet). Key goal: "30×30" — 30% of land and oceans under effective conservation by 2030.
    enhancement_target: true

54. **cms-bonn** — Convention on Migratory Species (CMS, Bonn 1979)
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Addresses species that cross national boundaries during migration. India is a signatory. Relevant species: bar-headed goose, Amur falcon, flamingos, humpback whale. Conservation requires international coordination across range states.
    enhancement_target: true

### Conservation Tensions

55. **fra-wpa-tension** — FRA 2006 vs WPA 1972 — Structural Tension
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:environmental-acts-unit
    What: FRA 2006 grants community forest rights to tribal communities; WPA 1972 requires inviolate core zones in tiger reserves and national parks. The two statutes are structurally in conflict; neither has been fully implemented; the tension is a live feature of Indian conservation law and produces ongoing litigation and policy gridlock.

56. **western-ghats-eco-sensitive** — Western Ghats Eco-Sensitive Zone Debate
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Gadgil Committee (2011) recommended ~64% of Western Ghats as ecologically sensitive, restricting mining, quarrying, and construction across large parts of Kerala, Karnataka, Goa, Maharashtra. Kasturirangan Committee (2013) reduced that to ~37%, prioritising development aspirations. Neither report implemented in full; political economy of the region has stalled both. The tension is not a policy design failure — it is the actual shape of the problem.
    enhancement_target: true
