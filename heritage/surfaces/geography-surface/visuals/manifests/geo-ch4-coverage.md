## Concepts (in narrative order)

### The Hydrological Frame

1. **hydrological-cycle** — The Hydrological Cycle
   Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_water_cycle + 9th_ch03_drainage (introductory framing)
   What: Water evaporates from oceans (502,800 km³/year), most returns as ocean rain; ~9% crosses to land as rain or snow. Rivers are the runoff phase — gravity-driven return to sea. Every river draws on the ocean's evaporative fund.
   Priority: high (foundational water cycle; all rivers downstream of this)

2. **fresh-water-distribution** — Global Fresh Water Distribution
   Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_water_availability
   What: 97% of Earth's water is saline. Of 3% fresh: 69% locked in glaciers/ice sheets, ~30% is groundwater, <1% is surface water (rivers + lakes). India's rivers sustain 1 billion+ people from an extraordinarily thin reserve.
   Priority: high (water scarcity framing; explains per-capita stress)

### Drainage Patterns

3. **dendritic-drainage** — Dendritic Drainage Pattern
   Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_drainage_patterns + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
   What: Tributaries join the main channel at all angles — like branches joining a trunk. Forms on uniformly tilted land with no systematic rock fractures. Most common pattern; dominates the Ganga plains and much of the Indus system.
   Priority: high (syllabus pattern; NCERT classification)

4. **trellis-drainage** — Trellis Drainage Pattern
   Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_drainage_patterns
   What: Tributaries run parallel to ridges before finding a gap to cross — like a garden trellis. Forms in folded terrain with alternating ridges and valleys. Visible in Himalayan foothills where rivers must cut across the fold system.
   Priority: high (syllabus pattern; explains Himalayan foothill drainage)

5. **radial-drainage** — Radial Drainage Pattern
   Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_drainage_patterns
   What: Rivers radiate outward from a central dome or cone. Classic example: Amarkantak (Maikal range summit) — Narmada, Sone, and Mahanadi all begin here and run in completely different directions (west, north, east). A single high point directing three major drainage systems.
   Priority: high (syllabus pattern; Amarkantak as the canonical Indian example)

6. **rectangular-drainage** — Rectangular Drainage Pattern
   Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_drainage_patterns
   What: Rivers make sharp 90° bends where rock is heavily jointed at right angles. Less common. Pattern reveals underlying structural fractures in the rock.
   Priority: medium (syllabus completeness; fourth of the four patterns)

### Water Divides

7. **western-ghats-watershed** — Western Ghats as Master Watershed
   Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_drainage_divides + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
   What: The single most important divide in peninsular India. Almost every drop falling east of the Western Ghats reaches the Bay of Bengal; almost every drop west reaches the Arabian Sea. Controls the direction of all major peninsular rivers.
   Priority: high (watershed concept; fundamental to peninsular drainage logic)

8. **aravallis-indus-ganga-divide** — Aravallis as Indus-Ganga Divide
   Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage + 11th_p2_ch02_structure_and_physiography (Aravallis)
   What: The Aravallis in the northwest mark the divide between the Indus drainage basin and the Ganga basin. Ancient, heavily eroded range running NE–SW. Geologically older than either river system.
   Priority: medium (drainage divide; connects physiography to hydrology)

9. **vindhyas-satpuras-director** — Vindhyas and Satpuras (Narmada–Tapi Director)
   Source: ncert — 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india + 11th_p2_ch02_structure_and_physiography
   What: East-west ranges across central India hold Narmada and Tapi in rift grabens and direct both rivers westward — while almost all other peninsular rivers drain east. The grabens are structural controls, not just topographic accidents.
   Priority: high (structural control on drainage; explains the westward-flowing anomaly)

### Antecedent Rivers

10. **antecedent-rivers** — Antecedent Rivers (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_antecedent_drainage + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: The three great Himalayan rivers predate the Himalayas. They were draining the Tethys margins before the mountains rose; as the Indian plate collided and mountains lifted, these rivers kept cutting downward — producing the deep gorges (Indus at Nanga Parbat, Brahmaputra at Namcha Barwa). The mountain is younger than the river.
    Priority: high (counter-intuitive conceptual anchor; UPSC favourite)

### The Indus System

11. **indus-river-system** — Indus River System
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_indus_system + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Rises near Mansarovar Lake (Tibet), enters India through Ladakh. Five major Punjab tributaries: Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, Sutlej — all join before the Indus reaches the sea in Pakistan. Total length ~3,180 km (one of the longer river systems in Asia).
    Priority: high (major drainage system; Partition context)

12. **indus-waters-treaty-1960** — Indus Waters Treaty (1960)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage (political geography note) + reference-books (political context)
    What: World Bank brokered. Three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) allocated to India; three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan. Partition's hydrological translation — every irrigation canal in Indian Punjab is downstream of this 1960 arithmetic.
    Priority: high (political geography; treaty; agriculture consequence)

### The Ganga System

13. **ganga-river-system** — Ganga River System (Overview)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_ganga_system + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Longest river in India at 2,525 km. Begins as Bhagirathi (Gangotri Glacier, Uttarakhand), joined by Alaknanda at Devprayag. Two sets of tributaries: left-bank Himalayan (Ramganga, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi) and right-bank peninsular (Chambal, Betwa, Son, Yamuna). ~400 million people in the Ganga basin alone.
    Priority: high (largest Indian river system; syllabus core)

14. **yamuna-river** — Yamuna (Longest Ganga Tributary)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_yamuna
    What: 1,376 km — longest tributary of the Ganga. Rises at Yamunotri Glacier. Joins Ganga at Prayagraj (Triveni Sangam — the sacred confluence of Ganga, Yamuna, and mythological Saraswati). Major cities: Delhi, Agra, Mathura along its banks.
    Priority: high (syllabus; sacred geography; urban significance)

15. **kosi-river** — Kosi (Sorrow of Bihar)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_kosi + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Left-bank Himalayan tributary of Ganga. Drains much of Nepal. Shifts course dramatically during floods — sometimes by hundreds of kilometres over decades — due to massive sediment load and flat gradient on plains. One of Asia's most destructive rivers. Also called "Sorrow of Bihar."
    Priority: high (flood hazard; dynamic geomorphology; UPSC recurring)

16. **ganga-brahmaputra-delta** — Ganga-Brahmaputra Delta
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_ganga_delta + 10th_ch02_forest_and_wildlife_resources
    What: World's largest river delta. Ganga splits into distributaries at coast — Hooghly runs through Kolkata; the rest enters Bangladesh as the Padma-Meghna system. Combined Ganga-Brahmaputra delta is still actively growing seaward despite sea-level rise threatening it from the other direction.
    Priority: high (superlative; delta formation; coastal ecology)

17. **sundarbans** — Sundarbans (World's Largest Mangrove)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch02_forest_and_wildlife_resources:geo_sundarbans
    What: World's largest mangrove forest. Occupies the Bengal delta — slowly sinking, storm-scoured, saltwater-saturated. UNESCO World Heritage Site. Tiger reserve (Bengal tiger). Ramsar wetland. Intersection of river, sea, and forest ecology.
    Priority: high (ecology; biodiversity; climate vulnerability; UPSC frequent)

### The Brahmaputra System

18. **brahmaputra-river-system** — Brahmaputra River System
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_brahmaputra_system + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Rises near Mansarovar (Tibet) as the Tsangpo; runs east for most of its Tibetan length at >4,000 m altitude. Makes near-180° hairpin turn at Namcha Barwa, cuts into Arunachal Pradesh as the Siang, picks up Dibang and Lohit, becomes the Brahmaputra in Assam. Braided, sediment-heavy river; floods catastrophically nearly every year.
    Priority: high (major system; unusual course; flood hazard; Assam geography)

19. **tsangpo-siang-brahmaputra** — Tsangpo–Siang–Brahmaputra Nomenclature
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_brahmaputra_system
    What: Same river called different names across its journey: Tsangpo (Tibet) → Siang (Arunachal Pradesh, after the gorge) → Brahmaputra (Assam). Understanding this continuity is a UPSC identification question staple.
    Priority: high (name-change geography; exam identification)

20. **namcha-barwa-gorge** — Namcha Barwa Gorge
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage (antecedent rivers reference) + writer-ai synthesis
    What: Where the Brahmaputra (Tsangpo) makes its hairpin turn and cuts through the Himalayan front. Deeper than the Grand Canyon. Physical evidence of the antecedent river cutting downward while the mountains rose around it.
    Priority: high (antecedent river example; gorge superlative; tectonic geography)

21. **majuli-island** — Majuli Island (World's Largest River Island)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage or 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: World's largest river island, in the Brahmaputra in Assam. Product of the river's braided, unstable course — formed by channel bifurcation. Shrinking due to ongoing erosion and flooding.
    Priority: medium (superlative; river island geomorphology; cultural significance for Vaishnavite monasteries)

### Himalayan vs. Peninsular Rivers

22. **himalayan-perennial-rivers** — Himalayan Rivers: Three-Source Perenniality
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_himalayan_rivers + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Himalayan rivers have three simultaneous sources: monsoon rain (Jul–Sep), Himalayan glacier/snowmelt (Apr–Jun), and year-round groundwater base-flow. This combination ensures perennial flow even in India's driest months. Fundamental livelihood advantage over peninsular rivers.
    Priority: high (perennial vs. seasonal; water security; explains irrigation patterns)

23. **peninsular-seasonal-rivers** — Peninsular Rivers: Seasonal Character
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_peninsular_rivers + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Peninsular rivers have none of the Himalayan advantages — no glaciers, no sustained snowfields, smaller catchments, limited groundwater contribution. Many are nearly dry in summer. Historical response: tanks (small reservoirs) and dams rather than canal systems from perennial rivers.
    Priority: high (seasonal hydrology; explains tank irrigation system of South India)

### The Peninsular Rivers (East-Flowing)

24. **mahanadi-river** — Mahanadi
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_mahanadi + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Rises in Sihawa hills (Chhattisgarh), drains through Odisha, builds delta on Bay of Bengal. Hirakud Dam on Mahanadi was, at construction, one of the longest earthen dams in the world (4.8 km). Major flood-control and irrigation project for Odisha.
    Priority: high (peninsular river; Hirakud dam fact; Odisha geography)

25. **godavari-river** — Godavari (Dakshin Ganga)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_godavari + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Longest peninsular river. Rises at Trimbakeshwar near Nashik (Maharashtra), crosses full Deccan width, reaches Bay of Bengal in Andhra Pradesh. Tributaries include Pranhita, Indravati, Manjira. Called "Dakshin Ganga" (Ganga of the South) for its length and cultural significance.
    Priority: high (longest peninsular river; syllabus core)

26. **krishna-river** — Krishna
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_krishna + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Rises near Mahabaleshwar (Western Ghats, Maharashtra). Major tributaries: Bhima and Tungabhadra. Builds productive delta on Andhra Pradesh coast. Water shared by Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh — ongoing inter-state water dispute.
    Priority: high (major peninsular river; inter-state water dispute context)

27. **cauvery-river** — Cauvery (and Inter-State Water Dispute)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_cauvery + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Rises at Talakaveri in the Brahmagiri hills of Coorg (Karnataka). Flows through Karnataka and Tamil Nadu; fertile delta at coast. Centre of an inter-state water dispute predating independence — originating in arrangements between the princely state of Mysore and the Madras Presidency. Never fully resolved; regularly resurfaces in agitation and court orders.
    Priority: high (peninsular river; water dispute precedent; colonial-era origin; UPSC recurring)

### Westward-Flowing Anomalies

28. **narmada-river** — Narmada (Westward, Rift Valley)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_narmada + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Rises at Amarkantak (also source of radial drainage), flows westward >1,300 km through a rift graben (structural trench) between Vindhyas and Satpuras, enters the Gulf of Khambhat. Narrow graben, steep gradient → no space to meander and deposit silt → builds an estuary, not a delta.
    Priority: high (westward anomaly; rift valley control; estuary-forming river; Sardar Sarovar context)

29. **tapi-river** — Tapi (Westward, Rift Valley)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_tapi + 11th_p2_ch03_drainage_system_of_india
    What: Parallel to Narmada, south of the Satpuras. Also runs through a rift graben westward into the Arabian Sea. Same estuary-not-delta character as Narmada for the same structural reason.
    Priority: high (westward anomaly pair; reinforces rift valley → estuary pattern)

30. **estuary-vs-delta** — Estuary vs. Delta (River Mouth Morphology)
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch07_landforms_and_their_evolution:geo_river_mouths + 9th_ch03_drainage
    What: Chapter 4 owns this at the river-system scale (chapter 3 owned it at the coastal scale). Rift valley rivers (Narmada, Tapi): narrow confined channel, steep gradient, low sediment delivery at mouth → funnel-shaped tidal estuary. Open-slope rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery, Mahanadi): long low-gradient journey, sediment-loaded arrival → distributary delta network.
    Priority: high (synthesis concept; explains coastal morphology from river physics)

### Lakes

31. **wular-lake** — Wular Lake (Largest Freshwater Lake in India)
    Source: ncert — 9th_ch03_drainage:geo_inland_water_bodies + 10th_ch03_water_resources
    What: Largest freshwater lake in India. Kashmir. Occupies a tectonic depression in the Jhelum valley — not glacial, not volcanic, but structural. Jhelum flows through it.
    Priority: high (superlative; tectonic lake; J&K geography)

32. **glacial-lakes-north** — Glacial Lakes (Dal, Nainital, Bhimtal)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_glacial_lakes
    What: Dal Lake (Kashmir) and Nainital, Bhimtal (Kumaon hills) are glacial — left behind when Pleistocene ice retreated. Differ from Wular's tectonic origin. Dal Lake is heavily threatened by eutrophication and encroachment.
    Priority: medium (lake classification; Pleistocene inheritance; tourism/ecology)

33. **lonar-crater-lake** — Lonar Crater Lake
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources or writer-ai synthesis (unique feature)
    What: Saline crater lake in basalt, Maharashtra. Formed by meteorite impact ~50,000 years ago. Only known hypervelocity impact crater in basalt on Earth. Unique geomorphological and chemical environment.
    Priority: medium (unique feature; meteorite impact; UPSC trivia-level but distinctively testable)

34. **oxbow-lakes** — Oxbow Lakes
    Source: ncert — 11th_p1_ch07_landforms_and_their_evolution:geo_oxbow_lakes + 9th_ch03_drainage
    What: Cut-off meander loops left behind when a river shortens its course. Common along the Ganga plains where the river has repeatedly shifted course. Characteristic feature of mature floodplain rivers.
    Priority: medium (fluvial geomorphology; meander process)

35. **sambhar-didwana** — Sambhar and Didwana (Salt Lakes, Rajasthan)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_salt_lakes
    What: Salt lakes in Rajasthan — relicts of a more humid past when the Thar received considerably more rain. Salinity is a paleoclimate record of long evaporation cycles and progressive aridification. Sambhar is India's largest inland salt lake and a significant source of salt.
    Priority: medium (paleoclimate indicator; salt resource; Thar hydrological history)

36. **chilika-lagoon** — Chilika Lake (Largest Brackish-Water Lagoon)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_inland_water_bodies + 10th_ch02_forest_and_wildlife_resources
    What: Largest brackish-water lagoon in India and one of the largest in Asia. Odisha coast. Ramsar wetland site. Migratory birds from Siberia winter here. Commercially central to coastal Odisha fisheries. Barrier-bar lagoon (not backwater).
    Priority: high (superlative; Ramsar site; ecology; UPSC recurring)

37. **vembanad-lake** — Vembanad Lake (Kerala Backwaters)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_lagoons
    What: Long, narrow backwater system in Kerala — the longest lake in India. Boundary between land and water is negotiated rather than drawn. Kerala's famous boat races and traditional fishing nets. Ramsar site.
    Priority: medium (coastal lagoon; Kerala backwaters; longest lake)

38. **pulicat-lagoon** — Pulicat Lagoon
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_lagoons
    What: Second-largest brackish-water lagoon in India. Straddles Andhra Pradesh–Tamil Nadu border. Barrier-bar lagoon (same formation type as Chilika). Important flamingo habitat.
    Priority: medium (lagoon classification; state-boundary geography)

### Dams and River Valley Projects

39. **bhakra-nangal-dam** — Bhakra-Nangal Dam
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_dams_projects
    What: On the Sutlej river, Himachal Pradesh. One of the highest concrete gravity dams in the world (226 m). Feeds the Indira Gandhi Canal, irrigating Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan. Called a "temple of resurgent India" by Nehru — emblematic of post-independence modernism.
    Priority: high (major dam; irrigation system; national symbol)

40. **damodar-valley-corporation** — Damodar Valley Corporation (DVC)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_dvc
    What: Set up 1948, modelled on the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA, USA). First river valley authority in independent India. Damodar had been called the "Sorrow of Bengal" for its catastrophic floods; DVC was built to tame it. Power + irrigation + flood control — the multipurpose template.
    Priority: high (first river authority; TVA model; "Sorrow of Bengal"; UPSC standard)

41. **sardar-sarovar-dam** — Sardar Sarovar Dam (Narmada)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_dams_projects
    What: On the Narmada in Gujarat. Completed after decades of legal and political battle. Narmada Bachao Andolan (led by Medha Patkar) opposed the project on grounds of tribal displacement (hundreds of thousands affected). Feeds Gujarat, Rajasthan, MP, Maharashtra. Landmark in dam/displacement/rights discourse.
    Priority: high (major dam; NBA displacement protest; inter-state water sharing; UPSC recurring)

42. **tehri-dam** — Tehri Dam
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_dams_projects
    What: On the Bhagirathi (upper Ganga tributary), Garhwal Himalaya. Tallest dam in India (~260 m). Built in a seismically active zone. Construction required submerging the old town of Tehri and relocating its inhabitants. Concerns about seismic safety remain unresolved.
    Priority: medium (tallest dam; seismic risk; displacement; heritage loss)

43. **hirakud-dam** — Hirakud Dam
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_dams_projects
    What: On the Mahanadi, Odisha. One of the longest earthen dams in the world at construction (~4.8 km). First major multipurpose dam project in post-independence India (inaugurated 1957). Flood control and irrigation for Odisha.
    Priority: medium (dam fact; first major dam of independent India; Odisha geography)

### Traditional Water Systems

44. **johads** — Johads (Rajasthan Check Dams)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_traditional_water_systems
    What: Earthen check dams built by communities in Rajasthan to capture monsoon runoff and recharge groundwater. Community-built and community-maintained. Many decayed after independence when state canal systems seemed to replace them. Some now being revived as groundwater depletion accelerates in northwest India.
    Priority: high (traditional water harvesting; community management; groundwater revival context)

45. **kuls** — Kuls (Mountain Irrigation Channels)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_traditional_water_systems
    What: Irrigation channels in Spiti and Ladakh cutting from glacial streams to mountain fields. Allocated turn-by-turn by village custom. Precisely calibrated to local glacial hydrology. Represent high-altitude indigenous water management.
    Priority: medium (traditional system; cold desert irrigation; customary water law)

46. **zings** — Zings (Snowmelt Capture)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_traditional_water_systems (possibly writer-ai enhancement)
    What: Small reservoirs in the cold desert regions of Ladakh that capture snowmelt in areas where no river channel runs. Miniature version of water storage in a climate where precipitation is locked as snow for months.
    Priority: low (traditional system; Ladakh specificity; complementary to kuls)

47. **stepwells-vavs** — Stepwells and Vavs (Gujarat)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_traditional_water_systems
    What: Stepped wells (vavs or baolis) in Gujarat and Rajasthan maintained groundwater access through the driest months. Architectural tradition aligned with hydrological function. Long pre-colonial history; many are UNESCO-recognised (Rani-ki-Vav, Patan).
    Priority: medium (traditional technology; cultural heritage; groundwater access method)

48. **tank-irrigation** — Tank Irrigation (Peninsular India)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_traditional_water_systems + 11th_p2 (agriculture context)
    What: Small human-built reservoirs widely used across peninsular India to store seasonal river flow and enable irrigation on seasonal (non-perennial) rivers. Historical response to the lack of glacier-fed perennial rivers in the south. Many tanks maintained for centuries; have declined with large dam systems.
    Priority: high (traditional system; explains why peninsular agriculture differs from north; UPSC water systems)

### Water Stress and Inter-Basin Transfer

49. **india-water-stress** — India's Water Stress (18% population, 4% freshwater)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources:geo_water_stress + writer-ai synthesis
    What: India has ~18% of world population but only ~4% of its fresh water resources. Per-capita availability has been falling steadily. Groundwater depletion is most severe in Punjab, Haryana, and western UP. The core problem is not an absolute shortage but a spatial mismatch — water falls where fewer people are, and vice versa.
    Priority: high (water scarcity framing; UPSC policy context; per-capita numbers)

50. **ken-betwa-link** — Ken-Betwa Link (First Inter-Basin Transfer)
    Source: ncert — 10th_ch03_water_resources (recent projects section) + writer-ai synthesis
    What: Connects the Ken river (Madhya Pradesh) to the Betwa (Uttar Pradesh) — transferring water from a surplus basin to a deficit one. First inter-basin transfer project under actual construction under the National River Linking project framework. Ecological objection: Ken flows through Panna Tiger Reserve. Each subsequent link in the proposed national grid faces harder inter-state negotiations.
    Priority: high (policy; inter-basin transfer; environmental conflict; UPSC policy recurring)
