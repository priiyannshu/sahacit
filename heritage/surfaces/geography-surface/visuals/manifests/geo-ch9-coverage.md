## Concepts (in narrative order)

### Global Population Distribution

1. **population-distribution-global** — Global Population Distribution
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_population_distribution
   What: ~8 billion people distributed extremely unevenly — roughly 90% concentrated in ~30% of the land. Average of ~56 persons/sqkm if evenly spread; reality is dense clusters surrounded by vast near-empty zones (Sahara, Amazon, Tibetan Plateau, Arctic). Distribution follows substrate liveability.

2. **three-population-clusters** — Three Major Population Clusters
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_population_clusters
   What: South Asia (Indo-Gangetic plain and Bay of Bengal deltas — densest), East Asia (China's eastern plains, Japan's narrow coastal strips — largest by number), Western Europe (third, thinner and more evenly spread, product of industrialisation). Smaller dense pockets: Northeast USA, Nile valley, parts of Southeast Asia.

3. **factors-population-distribution** — Factors Determining Population Distribution
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_population_factors
   What: Physical — flat fertile terrain at low altitude preferred; mountains and high plateaus avoided; water availability (river valleys and coastlines attract settlement); temperate and monsoonal climates more supportive; fertile soils (alluvial plains). Economic — mineral concentration attracts industry (Chotanagpur plateau), transport corridors thicken population. Social-political — stable governance, religious centres, kinship networks sustain density; conflict evacuates.

### Demographic Change

4. **demographic-transition** — Demographic Transition Theory
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_demographic_transition
   What: Five-stage sequence: Stage 1 — high birth rate and high death rate, stable low population (pre-industrial agrarian); Stage 2 — death rate falls (vaccines, clean water, reliable harvest) while birth rate stays high from cultural habit, producing explosive growth (sub-Saharan Africa in late 20th century); Stage 3 — birth rate begins falling (female education, lower child mortality, higher urban costs), growth slows (most of Asia in last two generations); Stage 4 — both rates low and stable, population flat (Europe, Japan); Stage 5 — births below replacement level (~2.1/woman), population shrinking (Japan, Germany, South Korea, parts of Southern Europe).

5. **demographic-divide-1921** — India's Demographic Divide — 1921
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_demographic_divide
   What: 1921 is the official demographic divide in India's reckoning — before it, population was essentially stagnant (high birth and death rates cancelling); after it, steady growth began. Four phases: stagnant (pre-1921), steady (1921–1951), explosive (1951–1981, driven by vaccines, Green Revolution, famine prevention), declining growth rate (1981 onwards). India is now in late Stage 3, pressing against Stage 4; growth rate fell from above 2% annually in the 1970s to ~1% now.

6. **demographic-dividend** — Demographic Dividend
   Source: reference — shankar-ias:demographic-dividend-unit
   What: The window during which the working-age share of the population peaks relative to the young and old — a temporary economic opportunity if schools, skills, and jobs follow the demography. India's demographic dividend is currently open and estimated to remain open until roughly 2055. The demography alone guarantees nothing; conversion into actual development requires deliberate policy.

### India's Population Statistics

7. **india-population-size-density** — India's Population Size and Density
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_india_population
   What: ~1.41 billion as of 2023 — recently overtook China as the world's most populous country. 17.5% of world's population on 2.4% of its land area. National average density 382 persons/sqkm in 2011 census; current estimates ~470. Growth rate ~1% annually now.

8. **state-density-variations** — State-level Density Variations
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_state_density
   What: Extreme range. Dense end: Bihar (1,106/sqkm), West Bengal (1,028), Kerala (860), Uttar Pradesh (829). Sparse end: Arunachal Pradesh (17), Mizoram (52), Sikkim (86). Physical substrate explanation runs directly beneath: alluvial plains of Ganga and its tributaries support dense settled agriculture; forested hill states of the northeast do not.

9. **age-structure-india** — India's Age Structure
   Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_age_structure
   What: ~29% under 14, ~64% in the working-age band (15–64), ~7% above 65; median age ~28 years. Relatively young structure — the demographic dividend in numerical form. Workable window remains open for several decades.

10. **sex-ratio-india** — Sex Ratio in India — Spatial Pattern
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:demographic-indicators-unit
    What: 940 females per 1,000 males in 2011 — below world average, persistently poor. Spatial pattern is one of the more revealing maps in Indian social geography. South and east are markedly better: Kerala (1,084), Tamil Nadu (996), Andhra Pradesh (993). Northwest (prosperous agricultural belt) is worse: Haryana (879), Punjab (895), Delhi (868). The northwest's agrarian prosperity coexists with a patriarchal inheritance that has historically treated daughters as liabilities. The south's higher ratios reflect Dravidian cultural tradition with historically stronger female autonomy and, in Kerala's case, a century of investment in female literacy.

11. **pcpndt-act** — PCPNDT Act 1994 (Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act)
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:demographic-indicators-unit
    What: Enacted to address sex-selective abortion practices that the northwest's patriarchal inheritance produces. Prohibits sex-selective abortion and the use of diagnostic techniques (ultrasound) for sex determination before or after conception. Exists precisely as a legislative response to the documented sex ratio deficit in the prosperous agricultural belt.

12. **literacy-india** — Literacy Rate in India
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_literacy
    What: National literacy rate 74% in 2011; males 82%, females 65.5%. Kerala: 93.9% (highest). Bihar: 63.8% (lowest major state). Female literacy is a direct predictor of sex ratio, fertility rate, and child health outcomes — its geographic distribution mirrors and explains several other social indicators.

13. **religious-composition** — Religious Composition of India (Census 2011)
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_religious_composition
    What: Hindu 79.8%, Muslim 14.2%, Christian 2.3%, Sikh 1.7%, Buddhist 0.7%, Jain 0.4%. From the 2011 census. Largest Muslim-majority concentrations in Jammu & Kashmir, Assam, West Bengal (border districts), Kerala, and the UP-Bihar belt.

14. **rural-urban-split** — Rural-Urban Split
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch1_population_distribution_density_growth:geo_rural_urban
    What: 31% urban in 2011; currently estimated at ~36%. India remains a majority-rural country despite its GDP level — unusual for an economy at comparable income, most of which were already above 50% urban. A structural feature of India's development path: service and IT sectors grew without urbanising the population at equivalent pace.

### Human Development

15. **human-development-index** — Human Development Index (HDI)
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch3_human_development:geo_hdi
    What: Introduced in 1990 by Mahbub ul-Haq in collaboration with Amartya Sen's capability framework. Combines life expectancy, education, and per capita income adjusted for purchasing power into a single number between 0 and 1. India sits in the medium-development tier, around 134th out of 193 countries in recent rankings — well below where its economic size alone would place it. The gap between GDP rank and HDI rank is a political fact: it is the country telling you how growth has been distributed and what it has, so far, failed to reach.

### Migration

16. **migration-stream-types** — Four Types of Migration Streams
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch2_migration_types_and_consequences:geo_migration_types
    What: Rural-to-rural (largest by volume, dominated by women moving on marriage), rural-to-urban (dominated by men seeking employment), urban-to-urban, and urban-to-rural. India in 2001: about 307 million people (30% of population) living somewhere other than where they were born — migration is a structural feature of the country's geography, not an exception.

17. **push-pull-migration** — Push-Pull Framework of Migration
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch2_migration_types_and_consequences:geo_push_pull
    What: Push factors make origin places intolerable: poverty, landlessness, flood, drought, slow wages. Pull factors make destinations attractive: jobs, schools, hospitals, the city's possibility of anonymity and reinvention. The framework organises cause without determining direction — the specific corridors are shaped by industrial geography.

18. **inter-state-migration-corridors** — Inter-State Migration Corridors
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:migration-unit
    What: UP and Bihar are dominant net out-migration states (UP net −2.6 million, Bihar net −1.7 million in early 2000s). Maharashtra, Delhi, Gujarat, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu are dominant net in-migration destinations (Maharashtra net +2.3 million; greater Mumbai highest absolute urban in-migration of any city). The flow: poor east to industrialising west and south, feeding construction, textiles, and domestic-service economies of receiving states.

19. **seasonal-migration** — Seasonal/Circular Migration
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:migration-unit
    What: Agricultural labourers from Bihar, Odisha, Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh move for 4–8 months for harvest or construction then return — never counted as migrants in any census. Structurally invisible in normal data. The 2020 COVID lockdown suddenly made this population painfully visible: millions of workers stranded mid-transit, walking hundreds of kilometres home, became a national emergency.
    enhancement_target: true

20. **india-diaspora** — India's External Diaspora and Remittances
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:migration-unit
    What: ~32 million people across 110+ countries — one of the world's largest diasporas. India receives the world's largest remittance flow, ~$125 billion annually. Remittances exceed FDI inflows in most years and are more stable as a financing source.

21. **diaspora-waves** — Three Waves of Indian Diaspora
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:migration-unit
    What: First wave (colonial period) — involuntary indentured labour system sent millions from UP and Bihar to Mauritius, Trinidad, Tobago, Guyana, Fiji, South Africa as plantation workers. Second wave (1970s onwards) — Gulf oil boom drew millions of relatively unskilled workers from Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and others to UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Qatar, Kuwait; transferred remittances, not skills. Third wave (1960s onwards) — skilled professionals and students moving to USA, UK, Canada, Australia, Singapore, Malaysia.
    enhancement_target: true

22. **forced-migration** — Forced Migration Streams
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Tibetan refugees (post-1959), Sri Lankan Tamil refugees (post-1983), Bangladeshi migration (post-1971, ongoing), Rohingya refugees (post-2017). A separate and politically fraught category from voluntary migration: these flows were produced by state violence and persecution rather than economic opportunity. Each stream has a distinct legal and political status in India.
    enhancement_target: true

### Rural Settlement Geography

23. **clustered-settlement** — Clustered (Compact) Rural Settlement
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch4_human_settlements:geo_clustered_settlement
    What: Houses tightly packed, fields outside — dominant in the fertile alluvial plains of the Indo-Gangetic plain and the northeast. Abundance of water and flat farmland makes dense clustering rational; historical needs of defence and irrigation management reinforced it. The most common settlement form in India's most populous regions.

24. **semi-clustered-settlement** — Semi-Clustered Rural Settlement
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch4_human_settlements:geo_semi_clustered
    What: Dominant castes' main village surrounded by smaller hamlets of other communities — partial dispersal reflecting social fracture rather than physical necessity. Appears in Punjab, Haryana, and parts of Rajasthan. Settlement form encodes caste geography at a visible spatial scale.

25. **hamleted-settlement** — Hamleted Rural Settlement
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch4_human_settlements:geo_hamleted_settlement
    What: A single named village on the map is actually several discrete sub-villages (called panna, para, palli, nagla, or dhani in different regional dialects) separated physically but grouped administratively. Common in the lower Ganga plain, West Bengal, and Chhattisgarh. Tends to reflect ethnic and caste separation at a fine spatial grain.

26. **dispersed-settlement** — Dispersed Rural Settlement
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch4_human_settlements:geo_dispersed_settlement
    What: Isolated farmsteads or huts separated by fields and forest — appears where terrain makes clustering impossible or social organisation does not require it: Himalayan slopes, Meghalaya, Uttarakhand, and Kerala's hilly laterite interior. Farming individual plots on steep terrain makes proximity to other households less valuable.

### Urban Settlement Geography

27. **urban-definition-india** — India's Census Definition of Urban Areas
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch4_human_settlements:geo_urban_definition
    What: Triple census criterion: (1) minimum population of 5,000; (2) population density at least 400/sqkm; (3) at least 75% of the male working population in non-agricultural activities. Plus all statutory towns — those declared under a state municipal act regardless of size or density. The third criterion (75% non-agri) is the one that actually distinguishes a genuine town from a large agricultural village; it is the criterion that struggles most with India's semi-urbanised fringes.

28. **statutory-towns** — Statutory Towns vs Census Towns
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch4_human_settlements:geo_urban_types
    What: Statutory towns are declared under a state municipal act and count as urban regardless of satisfying the census criteria. Census towns are settlements meeting the three demographic and occupational criteria but without formal municipal status — a large and growing category as India's semi-urban fringe expands.

29. **census-town-classes** — Census Town Classification (Class I–VI)
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch4_human_settlements:geo_town_classes
    What: Six classes by population: Class I (above 100,000 — roughly 500 in India), Class II (50,000–100,000), Class III (20,000–50,000), Class IV (10,000–20,000), Class V (5,000–10,000), Class VI (below 5,000). Cities above 1 million (~50) are metropolitan; those above 10 million are megacities.

30. **megacities-india** — Indian Megacities and Metropolitan Areas
    Source: ncert — 12th_p2_ch4_human_settlements:geo_megacities
    What: Megacities (above 10 million): Delhi NCR, Mumbai MMR, Kolkata, Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad — in roughly that order. All are multifunctional, though each retains functional origins that shaped their urban morphology. Mumbai simultaneously commercial, industrial, educational, and administrative.

31. **urban-functional-types** — Functional Classification of Urban Centres
    Source: reference — shankar-ias:urban-geography-unit
    What: Administrative (Delhi, Bhubaneswar, Gandhinagar), Industrial (Jamshedpur, Bhilai), Transport and Port (Vishakhapatnam, Mughalsarai, Itarsi), Commercial (Mumbai, Kolkata), Mining (Dhanbad, Singrauli), Religious (Varanasi, Tirupati, Madurai, Amritsar, Ajmer), Educational (Pilani, Roorkee), Tourist (Shimla, Goa). Most large cities eventually become multifunctional as they grow — functional origin shapes urban morphology long after it ceases to dominate.

### Conurbation and Megalopolis

32. **conurbation** — Conurbation (Patrick Geddes, 1915)
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Term coined by Patrick Geddes in 1915 for the merging of adjacent urban areas into a functionally connected urban region. The precursor concept to megalopolis; describes urban expansion that erases the physical gaps between previously separate towns and cities. Standard tool in urban geography since early 20th century.
    enhancement_target: true

33. **megalopolis** — Megalopolis (Jean Gottmann, 1957)
    Source: writer-ai
    What: Coined by Jean Gottmann in 1957 for the largest-scale urban merging. Archetypal case: the Boswash corridor (Northeast USA — Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Washington) — near-continuous urbanised zone of ~50 million people. Pacific counterpart: the Tokaido megalopolis in Japan (Tokyo to Osaka via Nagoya and Kyoto). A twentieth-century invention as automobile infrastructure and industrial expansion dissolved rural buffers between cities.
    enhancement_target: true

34. **india-urban-corridors** — India's Emerging Urban Corridors
    Source: writer-ai (pending: majid-india)
    What: India's equivalents of megalopolis corridors are still forming. The Delhi-Mumbai Industrial Corridor (DMIC) and the Chennai-Bengaluru Industrial Corridor are the two being most actively planned and promoted. Unlike Boswash or Tokaido (which emerged organically from industrial geography), India's corridors are state-planned — industrial investment zones designed to accelerate the merger of urban regions along national highway and freight rail spines.
    enhancement_target: true
