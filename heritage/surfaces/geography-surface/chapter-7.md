# 7 — What the substrate permits to grow

Pick up a handful of soil anywhere in India — in a rice field outside *Patna*, in a cotton row near *Nagpur*, on a forest path in the *Western Ghats* — and you are holding something that is, simultaneously, four things at once. It is weathered rock: fragments of the lithosphere broken down over thousands of years by rain, frost, and chemical dissolution. It is water: held between particles in films and pockets, carrying dissolved minerals in solution. It is air: filling the spaces between particles, carrying the oxygen that plant roots and soil bacteria need. And it is life: bacteria, fungi, earthworms, nematodes, and the accumulated organic matter of every organism that has lived in this spot and decomposed into it. Remove any one of the four and you no longer have soil — you have rubble, or mud, or dust, or compost, but not the thing that grows food. Soil is the meeting point of the four spheres, and all of terrestrial agriculture sits on it.

The layer is thin. A typical productive soil runs to about one metre of depth; occasionally more, rarely much more. And it forms slowly — at something like one centimetre per thousand years, depending on conditions. That is a rate worth sitting with. The wheat field that turns over in four months of growing season is farming a soil that took on the order of a hundred thousand years to produce. Industrial agriculture — compaction, monoculture, chemical over-application — can degrade that thickness in decades. Everything in this chapter is shaped by that asymmetry between the time it takes to build soil and the time it takes to destroy it.

Five factors determine what kind of soil forms in any given place. The first is *parent material* — the rock that weathers. A granite parent and a basalt parent give you quite different children: granite tends to produce sandy, acidic, mineral-poor soils; basalt produces heavy, mineral-rich, dark ones. You will see in a moment why this matters enormously for India. The second factor is *climate*: temperature and rainfall govern both the rate of weathering and its character. Warm, wet conditions promote *chemical weathering* — minerals dissolve, hydrate, oxidise — and break parent material down quickly. Cold, dry conditions favour *mechanical weathering* — freeze-thaw cycles fracturing rock physically, changing little of its chemistry. The third factor is *topography*: rainfall runs off a steep slope carrying fine particles with it, so slopes produce thin, coarse soils; valleys accumulate the fine material from above and produce thick, fertile ones. The fourth is *biological activity*: plant roots penetrate and pry rock, decomposing organisms convert dead matter into *humus*, earthworms mix and aerate. Remove the biology and soil formation essentially stops. The fifth factor is *time*: the same parent material, climate, topography, and biology produce a deeper, more chemically developed soil after ten thousand years than after two thousand.

The product of these five factors working in combination is a *soil profile* — a vertical cross-section showing distinct layers called horizons. At the top is the O horizon: fresh and partially decomposed organic matter. Below it, the A horizon — topsoil, where humus from above mixes with mineral particles from below; this is the layer that farmers care about most. The B horizon is the subsoil, enriched by minerals leached downward from the A horizon by rainfall. Below that, the C horizon is weathered but barely processed parent material. At the base, the R layer: parent rock, essentially unchanged. A mature soil has all these layers in sequence. A young soil — on newly exposed rock, or freshly deposited river silt — may have only the beginnings of an A horizon above unweathered C. The Himalayas are producing new soils every season on freshly exposed faces; the peninsular plateau carries soils whose profiles are old and deep.

The broad message of world soil geography is that *climate is the master variable*, the same climate system that chapter five described now showing up as a soil map that almost exactly overlays the Köppen map. A few types are worth knowing. *Chernozems* — the black earths of the *Eurasian steppes* and the *North American prairies* — are the most productive agricultural soils on earth: deep, humus-rich, formed under temperate grasslands where organic matter accumulates faster than it decomposes. The wheat belts of Russia, Ukraine, and Canada sit on them. At the other extreme, the *latosols* of the tropical rainforest belt — *Amazon Basin*, *Congo Basin*, *Indo-Malayan arc* — look lush but are nutrient-poor: the warm wet climate decomposes organic matter so fast that humus barely accumulates, and intense *leaching* strips soluble minerals from the topsoil into the groundwater. The nutrients are locked up in the living biomass, not the soil. Clear the rainforest and the soil is exhausted within seasons. *Podzols* develop under the cold, coniferous *taiga* of Russia and Canada: strongly acidic, grey, heavily leached of nutrients. *Desert soils* are sandy, low in organic matter, often with a calcium-carbonate or salt layer near the surface that impedes infiltration — though they can be productive with irrigation, as *Rajasthan* has demonstrated since the *Indira Gandhi Canal* arrived.

India's soil diversity is the direct inheritance of its geological and climatic diversity, and if you understand the parent materials and the climate, you can almost predict which soil will be where.

*Alluvial soils* cover roughly forty percent of India's land area — the most extensive soil type in the country. They blanket the entire *Indo-Gangetic plains* from the *Brahmaputra valley* in the east to the *Punjab* plains in the west, and appear again in coastal deltas and river valleys across the peninsula. Their parent material is silt deposited by rivers — Himalayan rivers carrying glacial and rocky debris, peninsular rivers carrying older crystalline material. Within alluvial soils, two subtypes matter. *Bhangar* is older alluvium, lying above the active floodplain: slightly elevated, more compact, darker, containing small limestone nodules called kankar. *Khadar* is younger alluvium, deposited in the active floodplain and renewed with fresh silt every flood season: lighter, sandier, more fertile. Settlement historically went to *Bhangar* for safety; farming went to *Khadar* for fertility. These soils are rich in potash but poor in phosphorus and nitrogen. On them India grows rice, wheat, sugarcane, jute, and oilseeds — the whole agricultural heartland, not by design but by inheritance from where the rivers deposited their loads.

*Black soils* — also called *regur*, also called black cotton soil — occupy a very specific geography: *Maharashtra*, *Madhya Pradesh*, *Gujarat*, *Karnataka*, *Andhra Pradesh* — the *Deccan Trap* zone. Their origin is precise. They are the weathered product of the basalt that the Deccan volcanic eruptions poured onto the landscape 66 million years ago, which chapter three described. Basalt weathers into a clay-heavy soil rich in iron, lime, calcium, and magnesium. The soil swells when wet and cracks deeply when dry, a physical property so marked that it is described as *self-ploughing* — the cracks that open in summer allow organic matter and air to be pulled into the lower soil, doing part of the tillage naturally. The soil retains moisture far longer than any other Indian type, making it ideal for cotton, a crop that needs sustained moisture through a dry season. The cotton belt of India is, in a quite literal sense, the geographical expression of Deccan vulcanism that accompanied the extinction of the dinosaurs. The soil is poor in nitrogen and phosphorus and requires fertiliser inputs for its weakness, but for moisture retention and mineral richness it is irreplaceable.

*Red and yellow soils* occupy the peninsular fringes — *Tamil Nadu*, large parts of *Karnataka* and *Andhra Pradesh*, *Odisha*, *Chhattisgarh*, *Bundelkhand*. Their parent material is the ancient crystalline and metamorphic rock — granite, gneiss — of the old Gondwanan shield. Iron oxide gives red colour; when hydrated, the iron compounds produce yellow, which is why the soil has both names. These soils are porous and friable, low in nitrogen and humus, but respond to fertiliser and irrigation well enough to support millets, groundnut, and pulses.

*Laterite soils* form wherever heavy rainfall alternates with a dry season over a tropical landscape. In such climates, rainwater dissolves silica from the topsoil and leaches it downward and away, leaving behind the less soluble iron and aluminium oxides concentrated in the upper layers. The result — *laterite*, from the Latin for brick — is hard when dry, soft when wet, and poor in most plant nutrients. The *Western Ghats* windward slopes, parts of *Eastern Ghats*, *Kerala*, *Karnataka*, *Maharashtra*, *Jharkhand*, and *Meghalaya* carry laterite soils. Their hardness has historically made them useful as building material: *laterite stone* was quarried to build temples and fort walls across *Kerala* and *Karnataka*. They support cashew, tea, coffee, and rubber with significant inputs — which is why the plantation belt of the *Western Ghats* sits exactly on this soil type.

*Arid soils* cover *Rajasthan* and parts of *Gujarat* and *Haryana*: sandy, low in organic matter, often with a hard calcium carbonate layer that blocks water percolation, and carrying a high salt content that limits crop choice. *Saline and alkaline soils* — locally called *reh*, *usar*, or *kallar* — appear in coastal areas under seawater influence, and more troublingly in the irrigated plains of *Punjab*, *Haryana*, and *UP*, where decades of canal irrigation without adequate drainage have raised the water table and drawn dissolved salts to the surface through capillary action. A white crust forms; the soil becomes hostile to most crops. Gypsum and drainage can reclaim it, slowly and expensively, and the problem is still spreading. *Peaty soils* are found in waterlogged, high-organic-matter environments: the backwaters of *Kerala* (especially *Kuttanad*, where farming happens below sea level), parts of *Bengal* and coastal *Odisha*, and the *Sundarbans* fringe. High organic content, very acidic, poorly aerated — supportive of rice when drained. *Forest and mountain soils* cover the *Himalayan slopes* and parts of the *Western* and *Eastern Ghats*: thin and acidic on steep faces, thicker and richer in valley pockets, supporting the tea gardens of *Darjeeling* and *Assam* and the orchards of *Himachal Pradesh*.

India loses an estimated five billion tonnes of topsoil annually to erosion. The *Chambal ravines* of *Madhya Pradesh* and *Rajasthan* are the textbook instance of gully erosion: overgrazing and vegetation removal over centuries allowed rainfall to cut channels that deepened into gullies, which collapsed into ravines, which now sprawl across tens of thousands of square kilometres of land that was once agricultural. This is the inheritance of a degradation that no single actor started and that no government has fully reversed. In *Rajasthan*, wind erosion moves sand dunes and strips thin soils. In the irrigated plains, salinisation creeps forward season by season. The conservation toolkit is well established — *terracing* on hill slopes, *contour bunding* to slow runoff, *strip cropping*, *shelterbelts* of planted trees along field margins in arid and semi-arid zones, watershed management at the catchment scale. What tends to be missing is not the knowledge but the sustained land-use discipline to apply it.

The world's major vegetation types — its *biomes* — follow the climate map from chapter five so closely that the two almost overlay. *Tropical rainforest* (where rainfall is high and there is no dry season: *Amazon*, *Congo*, *Indo-Malayan* arc) supports the greatest species diversity on earth. *Tropical savanna* sits between forest and desert: grassland with scattered trees, burning and regrowing with wet and dry seasons — the African savannas, the *Llanos* of Venezuela, the *Cerrado* of Brazil. *Desert vegetation* deploys convergent strategies across the *Sahara*, *Thar*, *Atacama*, and *Kalahari*: deep roots, small waxy leaves, water storage, dormancy. *Mediterranean vegetation* — hard-leaved, fire-adapted shrubs and dry forest — rings the *Mediterranean Sea* and appears on the coasts of California, central Chile, and southwest Australia. *Temperate grasslands* carry many names depending on continent: *Pampas* in Argentina and Uruguay, *Steppes* across *Eurasia*, *Prairies* in North America, *Veld* in South Africa, *Downs* in Australia — all sitting on *chernozem*-type soils, all heavily converted to grain agriculture. *Temperate deciduous forests* — oaks, beeches, maples — cover Europe, the eastern United States, and East Asia. *Boreal forest* or *taiga* is the largest forested biome on earth: a belt of coniferous trees across Russia and Canada, on *podzol* soils, with minimal understory diversity. Beyond that, *tundra*: permafrost, mosses and lichens, very short growing seasons. India — compressed by its latitude range and the dramatic altitude gradient of the Himalayas — contains representatives of several of these types within one national territory.

India's vegetation types are sorted primarily by rainfall. *Tropical evergreen forests* develop where annual rainfall exceeds 200 centimetres with no significant dry season: the windward slopes of the *Western Ghats*, the *Andaman and Nicobar Islands*, and the hill ranges of the *northeastern states* — *Meghalaya*, *Arunachal Pradesh*, *Assam* hills, *Nagaland*. The canopy is multi-layered and perpetually green because different species shed and replace leaves at different times rather than all at once. Rosewood, mahogany, ebony, and bamboo characterise the interior. The unique *shola* forests — stunted evergreen patches in the valleys of the *Nilgiri*, *Anaimalai*, and *Palani Hills* above 1,500 metres, interspersed with montane grassland on the hilltops — are among India's most endemic and fragile ecosystems.

*Tropical deciduous forests* are the most extensive vegetation type in India and divide by rainfall into two subtypes. *Moist deciduous* (100–200 centimetres) supports a commercially significant canopy of teak, sal, shisham, mahua, and bamboo across the eastern slopes of the *Western Ghats*, the *northeastern* Himalayan foothills, *Jharkhand*, and *Odisha*. Trees shed leaves for a short dry season of six to eight weeks. *Dry deciduous* (70–100 centimetres) is more open, the canopy thinner, the dry season longer — peepal, neem, sheesham, and dry-adapted bamboo dominate across much of *Madhya Pradesh*, *Maharashtra*'s interior, *Uttar Pradesh*, and the drier parts of the peninsula. Together, the deciduous forest belt is the heart of India's timber economy: teak and sal are the two species around which commercial forestry has historically been organised.

Below 70 centimetres of annual rainfall, *tropical thorn forests* take over — in *Rajasthan*, *Saurashtra*, parts of *Madhya Pradesh* and southern *Punjab*. Trees are stunted, thorned, with deep root systems. Acacia (*babul*), *khejri* (the state tree of *Rajasthan*, around which the Bishnoi community has long organised its ethic of wildlife and forest protection), and wild date palm are representative. Cacti and euphorbia appear as rainfall drops further. Every structural feature of thorn vegetation — small waxy leaves, thick bark, deep roots, ability to go dormant — is a solution to the same problem: water is scarce and the soil holds almost none.

*Montane forests* demonstrate, in vertical compression, the same zonation that plays out across whole latitudinal belts on the plains. Climbing the *Himalayas* from the foothills: tropical and subtropical broadleaf forest in the lower foothills; wet temperate oak and chestnut between roughly 1,000 and 2,000 metres; temperate coniferous forest between 2,000 and 3,000 metres — *deodar*, blue pine, silver fir, spruce; alpine scrub and meadow between 3,000 and 4,000 metres, with rhododendrons dominant in spring; and beyond 4,000 metres, mosses and lichens thinning toward permanent snow. The tea gardens of *Darjeeling* and the upper *Assam* hills are carved into the lower and middle belt; the orchards of *Himachal Pradesh* sit in the temperate coniferous zone. A lower-altitude version of the same zonation appears on the *Western Ghats*, where elevation generates sufficient microclimate differentiation to produce the *shola*-grassland mosaic described above.

*Mangrove forests* occupy the tidal margin — the zone where land and sea negotiate. The *Sundarbans*, straddling the *Ganges-Brahmaputra* delta across West Bengal and Bangladesh, is the largest single mangrove tract in the world. The *Sundari* tree (*Heritiera fomes*), from which the name derives, dominates the canopy; the forest is also the last significant habitat of the Bengal tiger on the coast. Mangroves colonise the deltas of the *Mahanadi*, *Godavari*, *Krishna*, and *Cauvery* rivers, appear at *Bhitarkanika* in *Odisha*, at *Pichavaram* in *Tamil Nadu*, across the *Gulf of Kachchh*, and throughout the *Andaman and Nicobar Islands*. Their structural adaptations are precise: *pneumatophores* — breathing roots that project upward above the waterlogged mud to access oxygen; stilt roots that anchor the tree in shifting tidal substrate; seeds that germinate on the tree before dropping into the water, giving seedlings a head start in the competitive tidal zone. Their ecological role is equally precise. Mangrove forests buffer coastlines against cyclone storm surges, trap sediment that would otherwise erode the delta, and provide nursery habitat for the fish that coastal populations depend on. Their loss is not an aesthetic loss — it is the removal of an inherited coastal defence. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami was a visible reminder of what that removal costs; the coastlines with intact mangroves suffered measurably less damage.

Vegetation is the substrate and cover for what lives in it, and it is worth pausing for a sentence on that dependency. The *Sundarbans* tiger has no alternative habitat if the mangroves disappear. The *Western Ghats* lion-tailed macaque exists within one specific type of multi-layered evergreen forest. The *Kaziranga* one-horned rhinoceros depends on the *Brahmaputra* floodplain grassland that floods seasonally and stays largely undisturbed. Each species is not living in India in general — it is living in one specific vegetation community, and when that community shrinks or is destroyed, the species has nowhere else to go.

The vegetation types described in this chapter, and the animals they support, are the visible expression of a deeper system — the *biosphere* — operating through flows of energy and cycles of matter that make the whole thing self-sustaining, or nearly so. The next chapter is about that biosphere and its keepers: what ecology tells us about how the system works, what the history of the last two centuries has done to it, and what the conservation architecture India has built in response.
