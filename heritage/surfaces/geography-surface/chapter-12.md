# 12 — When the substrate fights back, and the new inheritance

Eleven chapters of this surface have been about what India received. The planet handed over a Gondwanan shield more than two billion years old (ch1), a Himalayan front still being pushed skyward by the same Indian plate that started moving 140 million years ago (ch2), a peninsula of ancient rock dissected by rivers draining to three coasts (ch3), a drainage system redistributing snowmelt and monsoon rain across two million square kilometres of plains (ch4), a monsoon that is the deepest structural fact in Indian agriculture (ch5), mineral belts assembled by geological time in concentrations no other tectonic history could have produced (ch6), soils and vegetation laid down over millennia at the intersection of parent rock and climate (ch7), a biodiversity inheritance built by isolation and evolutionary time (ch8), and a population and settlement pattern shaped by all of the above over ten thousand years (ch9 through ch11). Every chapter has been an account of what deep time gave this country before any human being made a decision about it.

This chapter runs the frame in reverse. It is about what this generation is giving the next. The substrate that took 4.5 billion years to assemble is being altered at a speed and scale that no previous geological epoch has seen from a single species. That alteration operates at three nested scales, covered in order below: first, the hazards already built into the substrate — the ways the geography harms people even without our addition; second, the pollution we have layered onto that substrate at local and regional scale; and third, the climate change we have set in motion at the planetary scale, which is the most consequential inheritance of all.

There is a distinction that disaster science insists on, and it is worth holding before the specifics. A *natural hazard* is an event with the potential to cause harm — an earthquake, a cyclone, a flood. It becomes a *disaster* when it meets a vulnerable population: when the buildings are unreinforced, when the warning system did not work, when the land was settled on a floodplain because there was nowhere else affordable to go. The hazard is geography; the disaster is the interaction between geography and human choice, poverty, and institution. India lives in overlapping hazard zones because of where it sits on the planet. Whether those hazards become disasters is a different question.

*India*'s most frequent natural disaster is the flood. About 40 million hectares of land is classified as flood-prone — roughly 12 percent of the country's total area — a direct consequence of the monsoon's structure (recall ch5: most annual rainfall arrives in four months, and most of that in a handful of extreme events). The two most persistently flooded river systems are the *Brahmaputra* in *Assam* and the *Kosi* in *Bihar*. The *Brahmaputra* carries an exceptionally high sediment load, flows through a narrow tectonic valley, and swells rapidly when the northeast monsoon arrives in force; *Assam* floods almost every year. The *Kosi* is called the "sorrow of *Bihar*" because it does not merely flood — it shifts course, having moved its channel some 100 kilometres westward over the past two and a half centuries, depositing silt, abandoning channels, and flooding unpredictably across *Bihar*'s northern districts. These are inherited behaviours of monsoon rivers on active alluvial plains, not aberrations. Urban floods represent a newer geography overlaid on the inherited one. *Mumbai*'s flood of July 2005 submerged arterial roads under two metres of water; *Chennai* in December 2015 saw its airport shut and its low-lying neighbourhoods inundated for weeks; *Bengaluru* floods repeatedly because the lakes that were the city's natural stormwater storage — built by Mysore engineers centuries ago — have been encroached upon and built over as the city expanded; *Hyderabad* in 2020 lost over fifty people to flooding driven by impervious surface cover, clogged drainage, and an extreme-rainfall event of the kind that the warmer atmosphere makes more probable. Urban floods are where inherited monsoon physics meets decisions about urban land use.

Droughts operate on the opposite side of the water ledger and also express inherited geography. Peninsular India is structurally rainfed and structurally drought-prone. The cropped areas of *Bundelkhand*, *Marathwada*, *Vidarbha*, *Saurashtra-Kutch*, *Rayalaseema*, interior *Karnataka*, interior *Tamil Nadu*, and the northwest — *Rajasthan* and parts of *Gujarat* and *Haryana* — all live on the margin of adequate rainfall, and when the monsoon fails or arrives late, crop failure follows rapidly. About 68 percent of India's total cropped area is drought-prone in some degree. The trigger is typically a weakened monsoon driven by *El Niño* (recall ch5: ENSO's warm phase suppresses Bay of Bengal evaporation and reduces the monsoon's delivery). But the consequences of drought have been compounded by two human additions: the overdraw of groundwater by decades of irrigation, which has depleted the shallow aquifer buffer that used to exist before the dry season ended, and the decline in soil organic matter that makes fields more vulnerable to desiccation. The geography of drought is inherited; the severity of its consequences has been worsened by choice.

*India*'s two coasts live in two different cyclone worlds. The *Bay of Bengal* is the more active cyclone basin — it generates roughly five cyclones a year, a third of them severe, making landfall on the coasts of *Odisha*, *Andhra Pradesh*, *Tamil Nadu*, and *West Bengal*. The *Arabian Sea* was historically far quieter, generating perhaps one cyclone a year, most dissipating before landfall. But the *Arabian Sea* is warming, and since around 2019 the basin has become more active: *Tauktae* in 2021 struck *Gujarat* at severe cyclone intensity; *Biparjoy* in 2023 made landfall on the *Saurashtra-Kutch* coast after an unusually long track. The most important thing to say about Indian cyclone management is that it has improved dramatically, and the numbers prove it. *Odisha*'s *Super Cyclone of 1999* killed approximately 10,000 people; *Cyclone Phailin* in 2013 — of comparable intensity, hitting the same coast — killed fewer than 50. The difference was not in the geography. The difference was early warning, mandatory evacuation, pre-positioned *NDRF* teams, and permanent cyclone shelters built along the coast in the intervening decade. *Cyclone Fani* in 2019, one of the most powerful tropical cyclones to hit east India in two decades, was evacuated from by 1.2 million people in 48 hours. The lesson is precise: hazard is geography; fatality is governance.

India's seismic risk is a direct expression of the plate tectonic inheritance from chapters 1 and 2. The Indian plate is still moving into Eurasia at approximately 5 centimetres a year, loading elastic stress along the Himalayan front, the subduction zones around *Andaman and Nicobar*, and the old suture zones of the northwest. The country is divided into seismic zones II through V (V being highest). Zone V covers *Kashmir*, the northwestern *Himalayas*, virtually all of northeast India — *Assam*, *Arunachal Pradesh*, *Manipur* — *Kutch* in *Gujarat*, and *Andaman and Nicobar*. Zone IV, the second-highest risk band, covers *Delhi* and the national capital region, much of north *Bihar*, and the central *Himalayas*. The historical record is sobering. The *1934 Bihar-Nepal earthquake* killed roughly 10,000 people. The *1950 Assam earthquake* — measuring 8.6, one of the largest continental earthquakes ever recorded — triggered massive landslides across the *Brahmaputra* catchment. The *1993 Latur earthquake* struck in the stable *Deccan* shield, far from any plate boundary, killing ~10,000 people — a reminder that intra-plate faults, hidden and unmapped, can still rupture. The *2001 Bhuj earthquake* (~7.7) killed approximately 20,000 people in *Gujarat*. The *2005 Kashmir earthquake* (~7.6) killed ~80,000 across the Pakistani-administered and Indian sides combined. What Himalayan seismologists keep saying — supported by the accumulated-strain physics — is that a very large Himalayan earthquake has not yet happened in the instrumental record. The stress is there. The question is when, not whether.

India has one modern tsunami in its direct experience: the *26 December 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami*, triggered by a 9.1 magnitude earthquake off the northwest coast of *Sumatra* — the third largest earthquake on the instrumental record. The *Andaman and Nicobar Islands*, closest to the rupture zone, were struck first; the tsunami then radiated outward at jet speed, reaching *Tamil Nadu* and *Andhra Pradesh* coasts within two hours with wave heights in places exceeding ten metres. India lost approximately 16,000 people; Sri Lanka, Indonesia, and Thailand together lost more than 200,000. The disaster led directly to the establishment of the *Indian National Tsunami Early Warning System*, now operational from INCOIS in *Hyderabad* since 2007 — a seismic and ocean-level monitoring network capable of issuing warnings within minutes of an *Indian Ocean* earthquake.

The *Himalayan* slopes and the *Western Ghats* carry a permanent landslide risk. Steep gradients, high rainfall, and young unconsolidated material create an unstable substrate needing only a trigger — extreme rainfall, an earthquake, a road cut — to mobilise. The *Kedarnath disaster of June 2013* in *Uttarakhand* was a catastrophic interaction: a *glacial lake outburst flood* from a moraine-dammed lake above the valley combined with a cloudburst to send a debris-and-rock avalanche down the *Mandakini* valley into the temple town, killing an estimated 6,000 people. The disaster was geographically legible in retrospect: the glacial lake had been growing as temperatures rose, the moraine dam weakening, and the settlement had been built across the natural debris-fan of a mountain valley. In *Wayanad*, *Kerala*, in 2024, a massive landslide on converted plantation slopes killed over two hundred people. The geography of landslides is the geography of slope, rainfall intensity, and human settlement on vulnerable terrain — all three shifting under climate change.

The shift in how India manages these hazards — from relief after the fact to risk reduction before it — is an institutional inheritance assembled since 2005. The **Disaster Management Act 2005** established the *National Disaster Management Authority* (*NDMA*, chaired by the Prime Minister) as the apex policy body; *State Disaster Management Authorities* at the state level; *District Disaster Management Authorities* at the district level; and the *National Disaster Response Force* (*NDRF*), specialised rescue and relief units pre-deployed across the country. The *National Institute of Disaster Management* (*NIDM*) handles training and capacity building. India participates internationally in the *Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030*, the successor to the Hyogo Framework, which has four priorities: understanding disaster risk, strengthening governance to manage it, investing in resilience, and enhancing preparedness for effective response. The shift in vocabulary from "relief" to "risk reduction" is a real one. Hazards cannot be prevented; their conversion into disasters can be interrupted.

Pollution is a different category from hazard. A hazard is what the substrate does to us; pollution is what we do to the substrate. More precisely, pollution is what happens when our outputs exceed the substrate's natural absorptive capacity — and in most of India's major urban and industrial zones, we now routinely exceed that capacity.

India's air-quality problem is most visible in the *Indo-Gangetic plain*, where the winter atmospheric conditions — low wind speed, temperature inversion trapping cold dense air near the surface, the *Himalayas* blocking northward dispersal — combine with some of the highest emission densities on Earth. The sources are multiple: vehicular exhaust, industrial emissions, residential biomass cooking (the traditional *chulha*, cooking on wood or dung-cake fires, generates indoor *PM2.5* that the WHO estimates kills ~500,000 Indians annually from household exposure), construction and road dust, and the seasonal trigger for the worst episodes — paddy-stubble burning in *Punjab* and *Haryana* each October and November, sending a smoke cloud across the IGP that satellite images track for thousands of kilometres. *Delhi*, *Lucknow*, *Kanpur*, *Varanasi*, and *Patna* regularly rank among the most polluted cities on Earth by *PM2.5* annual mean. India's **National Clean Air Programme** (*NCAP*, 2019) targets a 40 percent reduction in *PM2.5* and *PM10* in 130 non-attainment cities by 2026. The **Ujjwala Scheme** (2016) provided LPG connections to 80 million households below the poverty line specifically to reduce indoor air pollution from biomass cooking, with measurable partial effect.

Water pollution in India is most concentrated and most studied along the *Ganga*. The river carries industrial effluent from *Kanpur*'s tanneries — which for decades discharged hexavalent chromium with minimal treatment — and the untreated domestic sewage of cities where wastewater infrastructure has never kept pace with population growth. *Ganga Action Plan I* was launched in 1985; *GAP II* followed; neither adequately solved the problem. **Namami Gange** (launched 2014, ~₹30,000 crore) is the most ambitious attempt, combining sewage treatment plant construction, industrial effluent enforcement, and catchment afforestation. The *Yamuna* through *Delhi* is biologically dead through most of its urban stretch — dissolved oxygen too low for fish life — despite the river flowing only 22 kilometres through the city. The *Cauvery*, *Sabarmati*, *Krishna*, and *Godavari* carry serious agricultural and industrial pollution loads. Agricultural runoff triggers *eutrophication* in receiving waters — the process where nutrient enrichment drives algal blooms, which crash and deplete dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic zones that kill aquatic life. Marine pollution enters coastal waters at urban ports, from the informal ship-breaking yard at *Alang* in *Gujarat* — the world's largest ship-breaking facility — and from the sheer volume of plastic that rivers carry to sea. India generates roughly 62 million tonnes of municipal solid waste annually; the landfill mountains at *Ghazipur* in *Delhi* and *Deonar* in *Mumbai* are visible from aerial photographs and periodically catch fire. The informal e-waste recycling clusters at *Seelampur* in *Delhi*, *Moradabad*, and *Bengaluru* extract metals from discarded electronics using acid baths and open burning, releasing heavy metals and dioxins into local soils. India is among the world's largest e-waste generators and a major recipient of e-waste imports through the informal sector. Globally, the *Great Pacific Garbage Patch* — a convergence zone in the *North Pacific* where surface plastic concentrates — has become the symbol of what happens when plastic production outpaces both collection and degradation; microplastics from this and equivalent accumulation zones are now detected in marine food chains, in Arctic sea ice, and in human blood. India is a significant contributor to the global plastic flow into the ocean, and the **Plastic Waste Management Rules 2016** and the 2022 single-use plastic ban are the legislative response — enforcement is the remaining constraint.

All of the above operates at local and regional scales. Climate change operates at the planetary scale, and it is the inheritance this chapter is most concerned with setting on the record.

The *greenhouse effect* is not anthropogenic; it is the reason Earth is habitable. Without the heat-trapping effect of water vapour, CO2, and methane in the atmosphere, the planet's mean surface temperature would be roughly 33°C colder — well below freezing over most of the surface, and liquid water would not persist. The *enhanced greenhouse effect* is what we are adding. Since the Industrial Revolution, the burning of fossil fuels and the clearing of forests have raised atmospheric CO2 from ~280 parts per million to approximately 420 parts per million — a 50 percent increase in less than 300 years, at a rate without precedent in at least 800,000 years of the ice-core record. The major *greenhouse gases* are CO2 (largest total contributor, from fossil fuels and deforestation — recall ch6), methane (CH4 — from rice paddies, enteric fermentation in livestock, landfills, and fossil fuel system leaks; approximately 25 times CO2's warming potential over 100 years), nitrous oxide (N2O — from synthetic fertiliser use and industrial processes; approximately 300 times CO2's warming potential), and fluorinated industrial gases (HFCs, SF6 — used in refrigeration and electrical equipment; warming potentials thousands of times CO2). Global mean surface temperature has risen approximately 1.2°C above pre-industrial levels; 2024 was very likely the first calendar year to exceed 1.5°C as a single-year average. The *IPCC* — *Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change*, established in 1988, shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2007 — synthesises this science in Assessment Reports; the Sixth Assessment Report (AR6, 2021–2022) is the current baseline.

For India, the impacts are already underway and the geography of vulnerability is precise. Himalayan glaciers — *Siachen*, *Gangotri*, *Hispar* — are losing volume at accelerating rates; since Himalayan glaciers are the dry-season buffer for the *Indus*, *Ganga*, and *Brahmaputra*, reduced glacier mass means reduced dry-season river flow over coming decades even as monsoon flooding potentially intensifies. The monsoon is showing increased variability: the same annual total rainfall now arrives in fewer, heavier events — longer dry spells between more intense downpours, the worst combination for rainfed agriculture and for infrastructure designed for historical rainfall distributions. Sea levels are rising at approximately 3.3 mm per year globally; the *Sundarbans* are losing islands; *Lakshadweep*'s low coral atolls, never more than 2–3 metres above sea level, are in long-term structural jeopardy; *Mumbai* and *Kolkata*, India's two largest coastal cities, sit at elevations that 2100 scenarios place under increasing flood risk. Heatwaves are intensifying — north *India* has registered above 50°C in summer — and heat stress on wheat crops at grain-filling stage is already measurable in yield losses. Fisheries distribution is shifting, with warm-water species moving northward along the west coast. Vector-borne disease ranges are expanding as thermal boundaries that kept certain mosquitoes at lower altitudes move upslope.

India has been present in the international climate regime since its inception. The *UNFCCC* — adopted at the *Rio Earth Summit 1992*, which also produced the *CBD* and the *UNCCD* — is the framework convention. Its organising principle is *Common But Differentiated Responsibilities* (*CBDR*): developed countries, having industrialised first, bear greater historical responsibility for the accumulated atmospheric stock of GHGs, and developing countries have a right to economic development. The *Kyoto Protocol* of 1997 was the first binding operationalisation: developed (Annex I) countries took on legally binding emission-reduction commitments; developing countries, including India and China, had none. The *Paris Agreement* of 2015 — adopted at *COP21 Paris* — changed the architecture: instead of top-down binding targets for selected countries, every country submits *NDCs* (*Nationally Determined Contributions*), updated every five years, with a collective goal of holding warming "well below 2°C" and pursuing 1.5°C. *COP26 Glasgow 2021* saw the first inclusion of "phase-down of unabated coal" in a COP decision text, and India announced a net-zero target of 2070 alongside stronger near-term NDCs. *COP27 Sharm el-Sheikh 2022* agreed in principle to a loss-and-damage fund — compensation for climate-vulnerable developing countries for losses and damages that cannot be adapted to — a demand that had been on the table for decades. *COP28 Dubai 2023* completed the first Global Stocktake and included, for the first time, language calling for "transitioning away from fossil fuels." India's updated NDCs (2022) commit to: 50 percent electricity capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030, a 45 percent reduction in emissions intensity of GDP from 2005 levels by 2030, net-zero by 2070. India's *LiFE* (Lifestyle for Environment) initiative argues that consumption patterns in wealthy countries are the demand side of the climate problem and must be addressed alongside supply-side fossil fuel transitions — a consistent extension of the CBDR equity frame.

Around this spine sit several other binding instruments the exam tests by name. The *Vienna Convention 1985* and *Montreal Protocol 1987* form the ozone layer protection regime; the *Montreal Protocol* is widely considered the most successful multilateral environmental agreement in history, achieving virtually universal ratification and an effective CFC phase-out; the ozone hole over Antarctica is now measurably recovering. The *Kigali Amendment 2016* added HFCs to the *Montreal Protocol*'s phase-down schedule, giving the Protocol a direct greenhouse climate co-benefit. The *Basel Convention 1989* governs transboundary movement of hazardous waste. The *Stockholm Convention 2001* restricts and bans *POPs* — *persistent organic pollutants*, the "dirty dozen" chemicals that bioaccumulate through food chains. The *Minamata Convention 2013* addresses mercury emissions, named after the Japanese bay where industrial mercury poisoning in the 1950s caused neurological disease in thousands. The *Bonn Challenge* (2011, later given a 2020 and 2030 timeline) is a global voluntary commitment to restore forest landscapes. India is party to all of these. The lineage behind this architecture runs from the *Stockholm Conference 1972* — the first UN conference on the human environment, which established *UNEP*, and where India's Prime Minister *Indira Gandhi* said that poverty was the greatest pollutant, setting the equity frame India has maintained ever since — through the *Brundtland Report 1987*, which gave the world the working definition of *sustainable development*, through *Rio 1992*, through the *Millennium Development Goals*, and to the *Sustainable Development Goals* (2015–2030) — 17 goals across poverty, health, ecosystems, climate, and institutions that constitute the current international development compact. At the biodiversity end of this architecture sits *IPBES* (recall ch8), the Biodiversity equivalent of the *IPCC*; both were established to ensure policy is informed by science rather than outpacing it.

There are domestic statutory additions to note. The **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974** established the Central Pollution Control Board (*CPCB*) and State Pollution Control Boards (*SPCBs*). The **Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1981** extended the framework to air. The **EIA Notification 2006** (Environment Impact Assessment) governs the environmental clearance regime for projects above defined thresholds. The **NGT Act 2010** established the *National Green Tribunal* — a specialised environmental court with statutory power to award relief against environmental damage, enforce environmental law, and adjudicate environmental disputes, without requiring the slower general civil courts. The *NGT* has issued landmark judgments on air and water pollution, illegal mining, and construction near protected areas.

This surface opened with a single tutor walking you through how a planet assembles itself, and what India specifically inherits from 4.5 billion years of that assembly. The inheritance was geological first — a Gondwanan shield, a moving plate, a collision still in progress — then climatic, then biological, then human. None of it was given to us by anyone who chose to give it. It arrived by the same lawful processes that have been running on this planet since before life appeared. We received it without being consulted.

The inheritance we are assembling for the next generation is different in one fundamental respect: it is being assembled knowingly. The mechanism is understood, the attribution is clear, and the consequences for India — the floods, the droughts, the retreating glaciers, the rising seas — are increasingly measurable and measured. India finds itself in the unusual position of being simultaneously among the most climate-vulnerable major economies on Earth, and in absolute terms, the third-largest emitter of greenhouse gases. Both of these facts are simultaneously true. The geographer's job is to hold both without letting either cancel the other.

The study of geography does not end with admiring what you received. The point of this whole surface — as each chapter has been building toward — is to understand what the substrate is, why it is the way it is, and what this generation is doing with it and to it. Every chapter before this one covered the first two parts of that sentence. This one is about the last. What we leave in the atmosphere, in the soils, in the rivers, and at the shoreline will be the inheritance of the generation that follows. Not assembled by deep time over billions of years, but assembled by us, in the span of a single human lifetime, with full knowledge of what we were doing.
