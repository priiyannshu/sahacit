8 — What happens under stress?

Every modern constitution faces a question that the older constitutions, drafted for smaller and quieter polities, did not seriously have to answer. What happens when the conditions of normal political life break down? What if the apparatus, suddenly, finds itself unable to govern by the ordinary procedures — what if there is a war on the territory, a rebellion within it, a financial collapse beyond the reach of the regular machinery? The document, on this question, has to choose between two unappealing options. It can refuse to recognise the possibility, and stand silent when the moment arrives, leaving the apparatus to act outside the document or not act at all. Or it can recognise the possibility, write provisions for it, and risk that those provisions will, one day, be used not against the genuine emergency but against the constitution itself.

Most twentieth-century constitutions chose the second option. Ours chose the second option. So have most of the constitutions of post-colonial states. The reasons are not mysterious — these documents were drafted for populations that were, in 1950 and afterwards, much harder to govern than any nineteenth-century constitution had had to contemplate. The framers wanted the apparatus to have, written into the document, the legal authority to act decisively in genuine crisis, rather than be forced to act outside the law and depend on subsequent ratification.

What the framers may not fully have appreciated, though they had good reason to, is that this choice has a sharp risk. The right to declare the moment of exception is, in some sense, the deepest right of sovereignty. The one who can decide that the ordinary rules do not apply is, in any system, the one who finally holds the staff. To write the exception into the document is to lodge that ultimate sovereignty somewhere — and wherever it is lodged, it will, in the wrong moment, be available to be misused. The framers placed it in the office of the head of state, on the advice of the executive of the day, with parliamentary ratification within a defined period. The 1975 emergency would test whether this lodging was safe.

There are, in our document, three kinds of emergency, and they are worth distinguishing because they answer to different threats and produce different consequences.

The first, and the gravest, is the emergency declared when the security of the country, or any part of it, is threatened by war, by external aggression, or by armed rebellion. (The third of these grounds was, originally, framed as internal disturbance — a much wider phrase, broad enough to cover any large-scale political movement; it was narrowed to armed rebellion by an amendment in 1978, in the aftermath of the 1975 emergency, for reasons we will come to.) When such a proclamation is in force, the document itself is partly rearranged. The central executive can issue binding directions to State governments on how their executive power is to be exercised. The central Parliament can legislate on subjects ordinarily reserved to the States. The distribution of revenue between Centre and States can, by presidential order, be altered. The term of the lower house of Parliament can be extended a year at a time, so that the constitutive renewal we treated in the previous chapter — the election — is deferred. And, critically, the freedoms of speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession can be restricted by laws or executive actions that would otherwise have failed against the rights chapter. By a separate order, the right to move the courts for the enforcement of fundamental rights can be suspended for the duration. The walls drawn in the second chapter, in other words, are, on emergency declaration, partly lowered.

The second kind of emergency is the one declared when the constitutional machinery in a State has failed — when, on the satisfaction of the head of state on a report from the State's Governor or otherwise, the government of a State cannot be carried on in accordance with the constitution. The Centre, in such a case, takes over the State directly. The State legislature is dissolved or held in suspended animation. The administration is run from New Delhi until elections can be held and a new State government installed. This is the more frequently used form of emergency; we have already seen it in the federalism chapter, where I noted that its conditions, restrictive on paper, have at various times been read elastically.

The third kind, the financial emergency, would be declared when the financial stability or credit of the country was threatened. It would allow the Centre to direct States to observe canons of financial propriety, to reduce the salaries of all government servants including the highest judiciary. It has never been declared. It exists in the document but not in the country's experience.

Before turning to what happened in 1975, it is worth recording where the framers got this architecture from, because the genealogy is grim. The provisions for emergency rule, including the suspension of fundamental rights, were drawn — knowingly, deliberately, with study — from the Weimar Constitution of the German Republic of 1919. That constitution, in its forty-eighth article, had given its president the power to declare emergency, suspend rights, and rule by decree. In 1933 those provisions were used by an elected chancellor to convert that republic, legally, into a dictatorship. Within months the parliament of that country had been suspended, the opposition imprisoned, the constitution effectively replaced — all under the colour of provisions the framers of Weimar had thought were safeguards. The framers of our document had, all of them, lived through the 1930s and 1940s. They knew this history. They wrote the emergency provisions anyway, with what they believed were stricter safeguards: the requirement that the proclamation be in writing, the requirement of parliamentary ratification within a defined period, the limit on duration. Whether those safeguards would suffice was an empirical question. The 1975 emergency answered the question, and the answer was no — at least, not unaided.

Begin the story of the 1975 emergency where the political record begins it. On 12 June 1975, a single-judge bench of the High Court of the State in which the Prime Minister's parliamentary constituency lay set aside her election. The ground was that her electoral campaign in 1971 had used official machinery — including the State's chief secretary and other government servants — for partisan campaign work, in violation of the electoral law. The judgment found her guilty of corrupt practices, set aside her election, and disqualified her from holding elective office for six years. The apex court was approached for a stay; on 24 June it granted a conditional stay, allowing the Prime Minister to continue functioning as such until the appeal was decided, but declaring that, on the strict letter of the disqualification, she could not vote in Parliament or draw a salary as a member.

Through the days that followed, an opposition movement that had been gathering for some months — under the leadership of a Gandhian socialist named Jayaprakash Narayan, often called J.P. — was gathering strength. On 25 June, J.P. addressed a large rally in the capital. The President was advised that night, on the written communication of the Prime Minister, to declare an emergency on the ground that the security of India was threatened by internal disturbance. The third ground, the broadest of the three available. The proclamation was signed in the small hours of the morning of 26 June 1975. By the time the country woke, the entire opposition leadership — leaders, journalists, students, dissenters of every political colour — had been picked up overnight under the country's preventive-detention legislation. Press censorship had been imposed; newspapers that resisted found their electricity cut off. The constitution's amendment process was, within months, used to retrospectively validate the Prime Minister's election, place it beyond judicial scrutiny, and immunise it from challenge. A subsequent and far larger amendment, passed during the emergency, reorganised the constitution to expand central power, place the directives of the third chapter of our story above the rights of the second, and to reduce the highest court's power of review over future amendments.

Through the twenty-one months that followed, the apparatus governed without effective political opposition, without free press, with the legislatures partly extended past their five-year terms. The most administratively disturbing phenomenon of the period — and, I think, the one that should be most thoroughly remembered, because it shows what an unconstrained apparatus does when it is given the freedom to manage populations directly — was the forced-sterilisation programme conducted, with state apparatus support, under the banner of family planning, largely under the direction of the Prime Minister's son. Estimates of the number of forced sterilisations performed during the emergency range from six to eleven million, mostly on poor men, often by police and bureaucrats meeting "targets" set from above. The herd metaphor is most acute here. The apparatus, given unlimited rein, attempted to regulate the population's reproduction directly — to manage, by physical intervention on bodies, the bare biological processes of the herd. The shepherd, in extremis, had reached for control of the flock's fertility. Foucault's category of biopower — the productive management of life by the modern state — has, I think, no clearer twentieth-century example than what was done to those men.

The judicial branch — the body that, in the chapter on rights, I described as the institution that holds the lines drawn against the apparatus — failed during these months. In a case heard in late 1975 and decided in 1976, a five-judge bench of the apex court was asked the central question of an emergency: when the right to move the courts for enforcement of the rights chapter has been suspended, can a person who has been illegally detained — say, against whom no charge has been framed, or against whom the framed charge is patently false — apply to a court for release? The court held, by four votes to one, that they could not. The right to life and personal liberty itself, the majority reasoned, derived from the constitution; if its enforcement had been suspended, then no court could examine even the bare legality of a detention. The dissent was written by Justice Hans Raj Khanna. He held, in a sentence that has since become canonical, that the right to life and personal liberty is not a creature of the constitution — it pre-exists any constitution; the constitution merely recognises it; and no proclamation, however lawfully made, can authorise its denial. The dissent cost Justice Khanna his promotion to the chief justiceship, which in the ordinary course would have been his. The majority's view was, decades later, formally overruled by the same court, with reference to Khanna's dissent, in a judgment delivered as recently as 2017. But by then, of course, Khanna had been dead for nearly a decade.

The architecture, in 1975-77, did not hold. The lines drawn against the apparatus were lowered. The rights chapter was, in operative effect, suspended. The judiciary failed. The legislature was extended. The press was silenced. The opposition was jailed. By any structural measure, the constitutive bargain had broken.

What rescued the bargain was, in the end, politics, and the rescue was not certain. In early 1977, the Prime Minister — relying on what we now know was bad intelligence about her standing in the country — called a general election. She was, perhaps, looking for a renewed mandate to legitimise what the emergency had done. The election was held under conditions still effectively those of the emergency, but the political opposition — released from jail in the weeks before — campaigned as best it could. The Prime Minister, and the party that had governed since independence, lost decisively. The Janata government that took office was a coalition, brittle, internally divided, but it had been installed by the population, exercising the constitutive performative we treated in the previous chapter, against the apparatus that had attempted to suspend that very performative. The herd, against the shepherd that had tried to over-reach, used the only tool it had — a vote — and used it. The election of 1977 was the residual safeguard the framers, perhaps, had hoped for and could not have written into the document with any certainty.

Once in office, the new government set about repairing the architecture. An amendment in 1978 — the document's most thorough self-correction since its enactment — narrowed the conditions under which a future emergency of this kind could be declared, and the things it could do once declared.

The most important changes were these. The third ground for emergency was changed from internal disturbance to armed rebellion. A peaceful political movement of mass opposition cannot now, on the literal text, be a ground for emergency. The proclamation must be issued only on the written advice of the cabinet as a whole — not, as in 1975, on the advice of the Prime Minister alone. Parliamentary ratification became required within one month, with the special majority of two-thirds of those present and voting and a majority of total membership; continuation became approvable only every six months, with the same majority. A procedure was added for the lower house to disapprove of the proclamation on the requisition of one-tenth of its members.

And, decisively — and this is the part I want most carefully to mark — a clause was added to the article that allows suspension of the right to move courts for enforcement of fundamental rights. The clause says that the suspension cannot apply to two specific articles of the rights chapter: the article that protects against retrospective punishment, double jeopardy, and self-incrimination; and the article that protects life and personal liberty. These two articles cannot be suspended under any emergency, however grave, however properly declared. The lesson of the 1976 judgment we just discussed — the lesson Justice Khanna had offered alone in dissent — was, by this clause, written back into the document. The document acknowledged, in this small textual change, that there are obligations the apparatus cannot abandon even when everything else is contingent.

Read this together. The emergency provisions exist because the framers thought a modern state needed legal authority to act in crisis. They were right about that. They placed those provisions where they thought they would be safe. They were wrong about that, but only partly: the safeguards they wrote were enough to be reasserted after the failure, by the same apparatus and the same population, working through the same constitutional procedures. The architecture survived. But the architecture survived not because the architecture was, by itself, capable of surviving — it had failed structurally for nearly two years — but because the political corrective mechanism (an election) and the judicial corrective mechanism (a single dissent that became the document's later doctrine) eventually re-asserted themselves. The lessons were learned at a heavy human cost — the imprisonments, the censorship, the millions sterilised. The 1978 corrections cannot make those costs unhappen. They can only narrow the conditions under which similar costs could be inflicted again.

The exception remains, in the document, available. It has been narrowed, but it has not been eliminated, and it cannot be — because no document for a state of this scale can refuse to provide for the case of genuine crisis. The 1975 emergency, then, is not a story about a dead architecture or a once-in-history failure. It is a story about the central, irreducible vulnerability of any constitutional order: that the rules cannot, by themselves, enforce themselves; that they depend on actors who may, in any given moment, fail to honour them; and that when those actors fail, the rescue, if it comes, will come from outside the architecture — from politics, from elections, from the willingness of one judge to dissent against four. The architecture is not enough. The architecture has never been enough. What you and I do, in our public life, to keep the architecture from being all that is between the apparatus and the population, is the thing on which the document, in the end, depends.

Once in 1975, the document was nearly converted, by lawful means, into something other than itself. The constitutive question this raises — can the document, by its own amendment process, be amended out of recognition? — is the question that the next chapter is built around. It is the question of how the document rewrites itself, and whether there is any limit, finally, to that rewriting.
