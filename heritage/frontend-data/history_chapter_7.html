7 — The Trader's Till and the King's Treasury: How the Company Became a State

Begin at a trial. January 1858, Delhi. An eighty-two-year-old man sits before a British military commission in a small outbuilding near Humayun's Tomb. His name is Bahadur Shah Zafar, and he holds the title of Mughal emperor. The commission charges him with treason against the Crown. The irony of the charge is difficult to overstate: the Crown whose authority is being invoked is not his, the sovereignty that has been transgressed is not his either. He sits in custody, tried for the crime of existing as the nominal ruler of an empire that was, in any real sense, already gone.

This is where the era ends. The question it answers is: how did you get from Vasco da Gama landing on a beach at Calicut in 1498 to an eighty-two-year-old emperor of nothing being tried for treason in his own capital? The previous era had closed with the Mughal fabric tearing — the third Battle of Panipat in 1761 crushed the Maratha bid to replace the Mughals at Delhi, and a dozen regional successor states filled the vacuum. Bengal under its Nawabs, Awadh under its, Hyderabad under its Nizam, Mysore under Haidar Ali, Punjab under the Sikh confederacy. None of them, however formidable, anticipated what the English East India Company was quietly becoming along the coastline.

The era I want to give you is the story of that becoming. Its spine is the slow conversion of a trading company into a colonial state — not in any single decisive moment, but through a long accumulation of fiscal and military authority that only reveals its full shape in retrospect. The metaphor I want to carry through this chapter is simple: the trader's till became the king's treasury. The Company arrived in India to make money. It left, three centuries later, having remade the subcontinent's agrarian system, destroyed its textile industry, reorganised its education, and fought — and won — every significant war on Indian soil. But at every stage, it maintained a fiction of indirect rule: a puppet Nawab here, a Mughal emperor as nominal sovereign there. This is our second metaphor — the hollow throne: the formal centre of Mughal authority kept technically standing, useful to the Company so long as it needed a historical legitimacy it could not claim on its own. And the third metaphor, which crystallises only as the era closes: the drain — the systematic outward flow of wealth from India to Britain, not yet theorised in 1858, but in operation from the day of Plassey.

Traders before rulers

Vasco da Gama's ships reached Calicut in May 1498. The Portuguese were not the future, but they were the opening move. They did not come to trade politely; they brought muskets and cannon and forced their way into maritime arrangements that had previously been governed by negotiation among Arab, Indian, and Southeast Asian merchants. A fortified base at Goa in 1510 was their beachhead, and it would remain Portuguese until 1961.

Three other European powers organised themselves similarly. The English East India Company was chartered in 1600, three years after a Dutch company of the same name and sixty-four years before the French equivalent. All arrived as trading firms seeking cotton textiles, spices, and saltpetre. The structural advantage was not military but commercial: access to American silver, large-scale pooling of capital, and naval capacity to enforce their terms. The Company factories on the Indian coast — Surat, Madras (Fort St. George, 1639), Bombay (taken from Portugal as part of a royal dowry in 1661), Calcutta (Fort William, 1690) — were trading posts with armed guards, not imperial beachheads. Not yet.

What changed was the dissolution of the Mughal centre. The Carnatic Wars of 1746-63, fought between the Company and the French over who would be the dominant European power in south India, were settled by the Seven Years' War in Europe: France lost, and the Company had no European rival left on Indian soil. It was now the last merchant-army standing in a fragmented landscape, and the successors of the Mughal empire — Nawabs, Nizams, Marathas — were powerful but separately vulnerable. The Company watched, calculated, and waited.

The thirty pieces of silver

On 23 June 1757, two armies faced each other at Plassey in Bengal. Robert Clive's force numbered about three thousand. The Nawab Siraj-ud-daula's army numbered something near fifty thousand. The Company should have been destroyed. It was not, because Siraj's commander-in-chief, Mir Jafar, had already negotiated with Clive: stand the army down, receive a throne. The battle that opened the British empire in India was won not by military genius but by purchased treachery. The Plassey plunder that followed — the vast sums extracted through Mir Jafar — was the first instalment of the drain.

The more decisive engagement was Buxar in 1764. There Hector Munro defeated a tripartite alliance: Mir Qasim, who had replaced Mir Jafar and resisted Company privileges; Shuja-ud-daula of Awadh; and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II himself. This was a real war, contested by real armies. Its outcome, formalized by the Treaty of Allahabad in 1765, settled something that Plassey had only suggested. Shah Alam II, in exchange for Company protection, granted the Company the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa — the right to collect land revenue from a territory of twenty-five million people. In a single act, the trader's till had become the king's treasury.

What followed was the Company's long education in how to be a state. Warren Hastings, the first Governor-General, took direct control in 1772, sweeping away the fiction of a puppet Nawab and beginning the construction of an administrative apparatus where there had been only a trading firm. The Regulating Act of 1773 brought the Company under partial parliamentary oversight — the first acknowledgement in Britain that a private commercial enterprise had acquired something indistinguishable from sovereignty. Pitt's India Act of 1784 formalised the dual structure: a Board of Control in London alongside the Company's Court of Directors, the Governor-General accountable to Parliament. The hollow throne of the Mughals was still technically standing — the Diwani grant, after all, had required a Mughal emperor to bestow it — but the Company was building an alternative sovereignty that needed no Mughal sanction.

The Permanent Settlement of 1793 reorganised Bengal's agrarian order. Lord Cornwallis fixed the revenue demand permanently on roughly twelve thousand zamindari estates and gave zamindars the right to sell land to recover arrears — which meant that zamindars who could not pay lost their estates at auction. The buyers were typically Calcutta moneylenders and merchants, urban absentees with no interest in agricultural improvement and every incentive to extract maximum rent. The Bengal zamindari class was replaced, partly, with this new class of urban purchasers; below them, the jotedars — the rich peasants who controlled local trade and credit — exercised more real power over the village than the dispossessed zamindars above them. The peasants paid.

The company that swallowed a continent

The years between 1798 and 1849 are the years of the great territorial acquisitions, and understanding them requires holding two dynamics at once: the Company's own aggressive expansion, and the fragmentation of the Indian polities it expanded against.

Lord Wellesley arrived as Governor-General in 1798 with an instrument of extraordinary efficiency: the Subsidiary Alliance. An Indian ruler who signed agreed to maintain a Company troop contingent within his territory at his own expense, accepted a British Resident at his court, and surrendered independent foreign relations. He remained nominally sovereign; the Company provided military security; the Resident exercised real political oversight; and the payment for the troops steadily drained the state's treasury. Hyderabad signed in 1798, the first major Indian power to enter what was, in retrospect, a gilded cage.

Tipu Sultan of Mysore would not enter it. He had watched the Company's advance with clarity — he sent embassies to revolutionary France and to the Ottomans seeking alliance, modernised his army with rockets and disciplined infantry, and won twice against Company forces. The fourth Anglo-Mysore War of 1799 was different. Wellesley's forces converged on Seringapatam from multiple directions; Tipu died defending his capital. He is buried there. The tiger of Mysore, whose sword of wootz steel inscribed with Quranic verses had become a symbol of Indian resistance, did not surrender. His defeat was not the defeat of Indian military capacity alone; it was the first moment where the industrial gap between British and Indian armament — guns and logistics produced by a society mid-revolution — began to tell decisively.

The Marathas fell in three wars. The First (1775-82) ended in stalemate; the Second (1803-05), fought by Wellesley against Sindhia, Bhonsle, and Holkar, gave the Company the Delhi-Agra region and placed the Mughal emperor himself under British protection — the hollow throne was now literally in Company custody; the Third (1817-18) ended Maratha power entirely, with Pune annexed and the last Peshwa Baji Rao II pensioned off to Bithur. His adopted son, Nana Sahib, will reappear in 1857.

Ranjit Singh's Sikh kingdom in Punjab was the last major Indian military state to resist absorption, and it did so through a combination of military discipline, diplomatic intelligence, and Ranjit Singh's personal ability to read the Company's interests accurately. After his death in 1839, the succession crisis that followed played directly into Company hands. The First Anglo-Sikh War (1845-46) cost the Sikhs Kashmir; the Second (1848-49) cost them Punjab. Lord Dalhousie annexed it in March 1849. The Company's frontier now ran to the Khyber Pass.

Dalhousie's other instrument was the Doctrine of Lapse — refusing to recognise adopted heirs of Indian rulers and annexing their states. Satara fell in 1848, Jhansi in 1853 (Rani Lakshmibai's husband had died without a natural heir), Nagpur in 1854. Awadh, despite having a living Nawab, was annexed in 1856 on the pretext of misgovernment; Wajid Ali Shah, patron of Kathak and a court of great refinement, was deposed and exiled to Calcutta. Nana Sahib lost his pension. The taluqdars of Awadh lost land and authority. Tens of thousands of sepoys from Awadh watched their families bear the weight of the new revenue arrangements. The Charter Acts that punctuated this period — 1813 ending the Company's Indian trade monopoly, 1833 ending its China trade and making it purely administrative, 1853 introducing competitive entry to the Indian Civil Service — registered the Company's transformation from merchant enterprise to governing machine. But no charter act described what that machine was doing to the people it governed.

What the company did to India

Beneath the territorial expansion, less visible but more pervasive, was the reorganisation of Indian society.

The three revenue systems are the foundation of everything else. The Permanent Settlement operated in Bengal. The Ryotwari system of Thomas Munro and Alexander Read operated in Bombay and Madras Presidencies, settling revenue directly with individual cultivators. Holt Mackenzie's Mahalwari system settled revenue with the village unit across the North-Western Provinces. Each created different rural hierarchies and different vulnerabilities. What all three shared was a revenue demand calibrated to the Company's military and administrative expenditure — not to what Indian agriculture could sustainably provide. In the Bombay Deccan, peasants fell into debt when cotton prices collapsed; British courts enforced creditor rights through foreclosure; the land passed to the moneylenders. The pattern repeated across every region in different forms.

The destruction of Indian textiles was the industrial consequence of this dispensation. Before the Company's ascendancy, Indian muslin and cotton cloth were exported across the world — the finest Bengal weaving had no rival anywhere. The Company, after acquiring the Diwani, no longer imported silver to purchase Indian goods; it used its Bengal land revenue to buy textiles for export to Britain, the proceeds remaining in Britain. By the 1830s, machine-made British cotton cloth was flooding Indian markets; by the 1880s, two-thirds of the cotton clothing worn in India was of British manufacture. Thousands of weavers and spinners lost their livelihoods and were forced into agricultural labour. The wootz steel tradition — whose products had fascinated European metallurgists including Faraday — collapsed by mid-century as British conquest disrupted the smelters. The Company's railways, which first ran from Bombay to Thane in 1853, were celebrated as a gift of modernity. They were also the extractive infrastructure of a colonial economy: lines built to connect raw-material hinterlands to port cities, channelling cotton and indigo and opium toward British mills, not toward Indian manufacture.

The education question was the most ideologically charged reorganisation of all. The Orientalists — William Jones and Henry Thomas Colebrooke, founders of the Asiatic Society of Bengal in 1784 — had believed that the British ought to study and promote Indian classical learning. The Anglicists, led by James Mill and culminating in Thomas Babington Macaulay, disagreed. Macaulay's Minute on Education in 1835 was not subtle in its contempt:

A single shelf of a good European library is worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia.

He did not win the argument by its merits; he won it because English-educated Indians would staff the lower rungs of the colonial administration at lower cost than imported Britons. The English Education Act of 1835 redirected the educational fund from Oriental to English-medium schooling. Charles Wood's Despatch of 1854 extended this logic: universities at Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras followed in 1857 — the very year of the revolt. The system produced, as Macaulay had theorised, an educated class trained in English thought and employed in the colonial apparatus. It also, inadvertently, produced the generation that would organise the freedom struggle in the next era.

The Bengal Renaissance was partly a product of this educational transformation and partly its critique. Rammohun Roy — who published in Bengali and Persian, who protested the Press Regulations of 1823, who founded the Brahmo Samaj in 1830 — was a man who had read both the Sanskrit tradition and the European Enlightenment and found neither complete. His campaign against sati used ancient textual authority to argue that the practice had no scriptural sanction, a strategy that succeeded when the Company banned it in 1829. His method — deploying tradition against custom, using the coloniser's administrative authority to achieve social reform — was replicated by every reformer who followed him. Ishwarchandra Vidyasagar used the same argument from texts to win the Widow Remarriage Act of 1856. In Pune, Jyotirao Phule and Savitribai Phule opened India's first girls' school in 1848 — Savitribai walked to it each morning through upper-caste crowds that threw mud at her, because her literacy was itself a transgression of the order that had assigned her a place. The Bengal Renaissance and the Maharashtra reform movements are not simply a colonial byproduct; they are the first articulations of a conversation about what Indian society wants to be, conducted by Indians, in Indian languages, on Indian terms.

The century of resistance

Before we reach 1857, I want to complicate something. The standard account treats the revolt as the beginning of Indian resistance to British rule. It was not. It was the culmination of nearly a century of continuous resistance. The suppression of that century's revolts is why 1857 is remembered as the first rather than the latest.

Bipin Chandra estimates more than forty major rebellions between 1763 and 1856, apart from hundreds of minor ones. The Sanyasi-Fakir Rebellion in Bengal and Bihar began in 1763 — displaced peasants, demobilised soldiers, and religious ascetics led by dispossessed zamindars, active for nearly four decades. Bankim Chandra Chatterjee set his novel Anand Math in this rebellion; the source of Vande Mataram is here, in this first organised resistance. The Chuar Uprising (1766-1816), the Poligar revolts of south India — including Virapandya Kattabomman of Tirunelveli, executed by the British in 1799 — the Velu Thampi Dalawa revolt in Travancore (1805), the Paika Rebellion in Orissa (1817) where Buxi Jagabandhu briefly seized Puri and Cuttack: these are not footnotes. They are the texture of a century that never accepted British authority without contest.

The tribal uprisings run deeper still. The Kol Rebellion in Chhotanagpur (1820-37) mobilised adivasi communities against moneylenders, outside settlers, and revenue administration. The Santhal Hool of 1855-56 — led by the brothers Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu, who mobilised tens of thousands of Santhal peasants against diku (outsider) moneylenders and zamindars with a claim to divine sanction — was brutally suppressed; the colonial response was to carve out a separate Santhal Parganas district, half-acknowledging that the land had been wrongly taken. The religious reform movements carried anti-colonial politics inside devotional language. The Wahabi movement, built on Shah Waliullah's tradition and given organised form by Sayyid Ahmad Barelvi in the 1820s, was an explicitly anti-British current suppressed through the 1870s. The Faraizi movement in Bengal, founded by Haji Shariatullah and expanded by his son Dudu Miyan, crystallised Muslim peasant resistance to indigo planters and zamindars as an Islamic revival. Even the Indigo Rebellion of 1859-60 — Bengal peasants refusing to grow indigo under the plantation system's coercive advance structure, the dadon — was a continuation of this pattern, though it sits technically across the boundary into the next era.

By 1857, the subcontinent had a century of accumulated resentment and a vocabulary of resistance ready to be mobilised. The revolt that broke out at Meerut in May of that year was not a mutiny that accidentally became a rebellion. It was a rebellion waiting for a match.

The fire at Meerut

The match, when it came, was small. The new Enfield rifle required that its cartridge be bitten open before loading. The rumour spread through the Bengal Army that the cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat — offensive to every Hindu and Muslim sepoy who handled them. But the cartridge was not the cause; it was the crystallisation. The causes ran deeper: the General Service Enlistment Act of 1856 requiring sepoys to serve overseas, which violated the caste prohibitions of the high-caste Hindu recruits who formed the bulk of the Bengal Army; the persistent racial condescension of British officers; and, above all, the dispossession that had followed every Company expansion and that had hit the families — most from Awadh — of the Bengal Army's soldiers directly. The army was recruited from precisely the communities most hurt by Dalhousie's annexations.

On 29 March 1857, at Barrackpore, Mangal Pandey of the 34th Native Infantry attacked his British officers in protest over the cartridges. He was overpowered, court-martialled, and hanged on 8 April. His regiment was disbanded. On 10 May 1857, the sepoys at Meerut killed their officers, marched through the night, and at dawn on 11 May were at the Red Fort in Delhi, calling on Bahadur Shah Zafar to lead them. He accepted. The hollow throne was suddenly asked to be solid.

Notice what is being asked here. A man of eighty-two, with no army and no real power, is being offered the formal leadership of a rebellion that had broken out without him. He accepts because he has no language in which to refuse — he is the Mughal emperor, and this is his empire. Half of the Company's Bengal Army joined the revolt. Delhi was held by the rebels for four months. At Lucknow, Begum Hazrat Mahal led the revolt in the name of her young son Birjis Qadr, the deposed Nawab's heir. At Kanpur, Nana Sahib — whose pension the Company had denied — declared himself the restored Peshwa and organised resistance. At Jhansi, Rani Lakshmibai fought the Company that had annexed her state under the Doctrine of Lapse; she captured Gwalior Fort with Tantia Tope, and died fighting in June 1858. General Hugh Rose called her 'the best and bravest' of the rebel leaders. In Bihar, Kunwar Singh — a zamindar nearly eighty years old — led with a speed and effectiveness that astonished his British opponents.

The revolt was not only sepoy. In Awadh, dispossessed taluqdars rallied their peasants; artisans, clergy, and the urban poor attacked symbols of Company authority. The civilian dimension gave the revolt its breadth — it was, in Awadh especially, a genuine popular uprising, not merely a military mutiny. The British put it down with great force. Delhi fell in September 1857. Lucknow in March 1858. The reprisals were punitive beyond military necessity — executions, confiscations, the blowing of rebels from cannon. Bahadur Shah Zafar was captured, tried, and deported to Rangoon, where he died in 1862 writing Urdu poetry, having asked to be buried in Delhi and being buried in Burma instead. His exile is the closing image of the hollow throne finally dismantled.

The Government of India Act of 1858 transferred power from the East India Company to the British Crown. The Company, that hybrid of trading firm and colonial state, was wound up after 258 years. Queen Victoria's Proclamation of November 1858 announced the new dispensation, promising equal protection of the law to all subjects regardless of religious faith, committing the Crown to non-interference in Indian religion and custom, and pledging respect for the rights of Indian princes. Indian nationalists in the next era would quote this proclamation back at the British whenever they violated it — which they did, frequently and comprehensively. The gap between the promise of 1858 and the practice of what followed is one of the defining ironies of the high-colonial era that opens as this one closes.

What the era leaves us

The era closes with a paradox. The revolt that ended Company rule was the largest indigenous uprising the British had faced anywhere in the empire. Its defeat did not end colonialism; it intensified and systematised it. The Crown's direct administration would build the railways and telegraph, codify Indian law, conduct a census that categorised every Indian by religion, caste, and tribe, and construct the Indian Civil Service as a machine for governing three hundred million people through a European-educated minority. These structures were built, in part, from the ruins of the Company's defeat.

But what the era has also built — and this is the thread to carry forward — is the India that the next century will have to work with. The revenue settlements created modern property rights where there had been overlapping customary claims, and those property rights, however unjustly distributed, became the terrain on which agrarian politics would be fought for the next hundred years. The English-educated class — small, urban, anxious, fluent in the language of constitutional rights — is forming. It will found the Indian National Congress in 1885. The Bengal Renaissance and the Maharashtra reform movements have established that Indian society can argue about itself, in its own languages, on its own terms. And the resistance — tribal, peasant, religious — though crushed piecemeal over a century, has produced a cultural memory that the next century's mass movements will draw upon. The Sanyasi rebellion that Bankim Chandra fictionalized in 1882 had happened a hundred years before.

In parallel, while all of this was unfolding on the subcontinent, the world that made it possible was transforming. The Industrial Revolution in Britain — steam engines, cotton mills, railways, the factory system — was the engine of military and logistical advantage that compounded year on year, making it harder for any Indian state to close the gap. The French Revolution's ideas of popular sovereignty and equal rights were, with bitter irony, being denied to India by the same European civilization that had articulated them. The settler colonialisms in the Americas and Australia — the trail of tears, the Australian frontier wars, the terra nullius doctrine — were running in parallel, showing that the British way of handling subject peoples was not an Indian peculiarity but a global project.

The drain, meanwhile, continues. The trader's till is now the imperial exchequer, and the flow of wealth from India to Britain — through tribute, trade imbalances, the payment of European salaries and pensions from Indian revenues, the profits of indigo plantations and opium cultivation — is the structural fact beneath every other fact of this era. Dadabhai Naoroji is taking notes. In the next era, he will publish the calculation.

Hold the three images as you open the timeline: the till that became a treasury, the throne that was hollow long before it fell, and the drain that was always running. The timeline will show you Plassey and Permanent Settlement and Macaulay's Minute and the Santhal Hool and Meerut as individual cards, each precise in its date and consequence. What this chapter tried to show you is not the cards but the logic that connects them — the slow accumulation by which a merchant firm, over three centuries, became the sovereign power of the subcontinent, and was then displaced by the Crown it had always served. Bahadur Shah Zafar is in exile in Rangoon, writing verses about the city he cannot return to. The till is the treasury now. What comes next is the full apparatus of the colonial state, and the generations who will eventually dismantle it.
