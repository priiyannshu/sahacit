9 — From Non-Cooperation to Partition: The Mass-Mobilisation Era

Begin, if you will, in April of 1919, the year just before our era formally opens. A British brigadier orders his troops to fire, without warning, on an unarmed crowd in a walled garden in Amritsar. The garden is called Jallianwala Bagh; the crowd is gathered, in part, to protest a recently-passed law that lets the colonial state imprison Indians without trial; the firing continues for ten minutes; the dead, by the lowest official estimate, number three hundred and seventy-nine. We could begin the story of this era anywhere — at the death of Tilak in August 1920, at the Treaty of Sèvres which dismembered the Ottoman Caliphate, at Gandhi's return from South Africa some years earlier — but I think Jallianwala is the right opening, because what happens next, in essence, is the answer of a colonised population to that garden.

The previous era had shown the apparatus of the British Indian state at the height of its self-confidence. Railways, jute mills, the census, the Indian Civil Service, the codified Penal Code. The educated Indian middle class had spent the last three decades politely petitioning that apparatus to make a small place for them within it. The Moderates of the Indian National Congress had drafted memoranda; the Extremists, briefly, had broken away to demand more; the Morley-Minto Reforms of 1909 had granted the appearance of constitutional concession without the substance. By the spring of 1919, this politics of petition had run out of breath. The Rowlatt Act made petition itself look naive. Jallianwala made it look obscene. The era we are now entering — call it the era of mass mobilisation, or the era of the freedom struggle proper — is the era in which the Congress stops asking and starts pulling, and in which the apparatus of the Raj, asked to put down a population it had once governed with the polite consent of its elites, discovers the difficulty of governing without that consent.

This era runs from 1919 to 1947. It is twenty-eight years long. It contains three full national mass movements, two world wars, a global depression, four great parliamentary acts of constitutional reform, the founding and intensification of three ideological currents that will reshape post-colonial India (the communist left, the Hindu nationalist right, the Muslim nationalist project), and ends, on the night of 14-15 August 1947, with the partition of the subcontinent into two states that begin their independent existence in the middle of the largest forced migration in modern history. Roughly fifteen million people will move; somewhere between two hundred thousand and two million will be killed. We will get to all of this. But it is worth noticing, at the outset, that the freedom struggle is not one thing. It is a pattern. The pattern is — mass movement → state repression → withdrawal → constructive interlude → renewed mass movement. Three times this rhythm will run its course (1920-22, 1930-34, 1942), and each time it will leave the colonial state weaker than it found it, and each time the constructive interlude that follows will leave behind an institutional residue that the next mass movement will draw upon. The freedom struggle, in this view, is best understood not as a story of heroes (though it has them) but as the slow accumulation of this pattern's residue, until the apparatus of the Raj, asked once too often to govern by force alone, simply decides it is no longer worth the cost.

The first mass experiment

In December 1920, at a special session of the Congress in Calcutta and then again at the regular session in Nagpur, Gandhi persuades the Indian National Congress to do something it has never done before: to commit itself, as a body, to the non-cooperation of a colonised population with its coloniser. The proximate trigger is twofold. There is, at home, the Punjab Wrong — Jallianwala, the brutal aftermath, the failure of the Hunter Committee to deliver any real accountability. There is, abroad, the Khilafat Wrong — the Treaty of Sèvres in May 1920 has broken up the Ottoman Empire and stripped the Caliph of his temporal authority, and Indian Muslims, led by the Ali Brothers (Mohammed Ali and Shaukat Ali) in an extraordinary alliance with Gandhi, have launched the Khilafat Movement to defend his position. Gandhi takes these two wrongs and welds them, briefly, into a single Non-Cooperation programme: surrender of titles, boycott of legislative councils, of British courts, of British schools, of foreign cloth.

Notice what is being asked. It is not the storming of arsenals; it is the withdrawal of legitimacy. A government cannot rule a population that refuses, methodically and at scale, to do business with it. The Tilak Swaraj Fund collects a crore of rupees in six months. The Congress Volunteer Corps drills openly in the streets. The Prince of Wales, arriving in Bombay in November 1921 to a state visit that the Congress has called Indians to boycott, lands to a city in hartal. Foreign cloth imports halve between 1920 and 1922. For the first time, an organised political movement is making the apparatus of the Raj feel its own dependence on the cooperation of the people it claims to govern.

And then, in February 1922, in a small market town in the United Provinces called Chauri Chaura, a procession of non-cooperators, harassed by the local police, turns on the police station and burns it to the ground; twenty-two policemen die. Gandhi, to the bewilderment of the entire Congress leadership, calls off the movement. He does not call it off because the movement is failing. He calls it off because the movement is succeeding without the discipline that, in his view, alone makes it ethically tenable. Non-violence, for Gandhi, is not a tactic to be deployed when convenient and abandoned when frustrating; it is the moral condition of legitimacy itself. A movement that becomes violent forfeits, in his account, the right to claim authority over the apparatus it would replace.

I want you to feel the strangeness of this. A movement at its peak, voluntarily wound down by its own leadership, on a principle that almost no one outside Gandhi entirely understood. The decision will be debated in Congress for years. C.R. Das, in jail at the time, calls it a betrayal. Many of the younger nationalists — including the boy who in nine years will fire a pistol into the central legislative assembly, and the man who in two decades will lead the Quit India revolt — take from Chauri Chaura the lesson that Gandhi's method, whatever its merits, is not the only method, and perhaps not even the right method. The freedom struggle, from this moment forward, will not be a single column under a single banner. It will be a many-threaded fabric, and the threads will pull, sometimes, against one another.

The long interregnum

What follows the withdrawal is, in the conventional account, "stagnation." I think this is wrong. The period from 1922 to 1929 is one of the most generative in the entire freedom struggle, not despite the absence of a national movement but because of it. The ferment that goes underground in these years is the ferment that surfaces, with great force, in 1930.

Consider what is happening at once. C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru, persuaded that the boycott of councils is futile if those councils are going to be the only legal arena left to nationalist politics, found the Swaraj Party in January 1923 and contest the elections to the central legislative assembly under the 1919 Act. They go in, in their phrase, to wreck the council from within; for two years, with Vithalbhai Patel as President of the Assembly, they do exactly that — defeating ministry after ministry and exposing the constitutional fiction of dyarchy as the limited concession it always was. The Gandhian "no-changers" — the wing of the Congress that refuses to enter the councils — turn instead to the Constructive Programme: khadi, village uplift, the reform of the Kaliparaj community in Gujarat (renamed by Gandhi, deliberately, to Raniparaj — "people of the queen"), the slow construction of the Congress as an organisation with roots in every district. Both strategies are, in their way, preparation for the next mass movement. When Vallabhbhai Patel leads the Bardoli Satyagraha of 1928 to victory against an unjust revenue settlement — the success that earns him the title Sardar — he is using infrastructure that the Constructive Programme has been quietly building since 1922.

Meanwhile, on the radical edge, a different politics is being born. In October 1924, in a quiet meeting in Kanpur, a group of veterans of the earlier revolutionary tradition — Sachindranath Sanyal, Ramprasad Bismil — found the Hindustan Republican Association. They are joined, over the next four years, by a young man from Punjab named Bhagat Singh, who has read Marx and Lenin and who, with his friend Sukhdev and the elusive Chandrashekhar Azad, will reconstitute the HRA in September 1928 as the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association — explicitly socialist, explicitly aimed at not just driving the British out but transforming the social order beneath them. The Kakori train robbery of August 1925, the assassination of the British police officer Saunders in December 1928 (mistaken for the man who had ordered the lathi charge that killed Lala Lajpat Rai a fortnight earlier), the bomb thrown by Bhagat Singh and B.K. Dutt into the central legislative assembly in April 1929 — to make the deaf hear, as the leaflet they scattered put it — these are not, in their own self-understanding, acts of terrorism for its own sake. They are propaganda of the deed. They are the assertion that a colonised people, denied the right to be heard, will make itself heard. Bhagat Singh's "Why I Am An Atheist," written in jail in 1930, and the "Philosophy of the Bomb" of the same year, are political texts of a sophistication that the Congress mainstream of the time could not equal. Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev are hanged on 23 March 1931, by which point Bhagat Singh, twenty-three years old, is more popular in some surveys than Gandhi.

In parallel, a third current is forming. M.N. Roy, in Tashkent in October 1920, founds an émigré Communist Party of India — the first international communist party constituted by Indians, four years before the Communist Party of India is founded on Indian soil at Kanpur in December 1925. The British, alarmed, prosecute the Peshawar Conspiracy Cases, the Kanpur Bolshevik Conspiracy Case, and most consequentially the Meerut Conspiracy Case of 1929-33, which puts thirty-three communists and trade unionists on trial for a sedition the prosecution itself struggles to define. The Workers' and Peasants' Parties of the late 1920s — Dange in Bombay, Muzaffar Ahmed in Bengal — are the first attempt to organise an Indian working class as a political force in its own right. The All India Trade Union Congress, founded in 1920, holds the formal infrastructure of this organisation; the Bombay Textile Strike of April-September 1928 — six months long, a hundred and fifty thousand workers, led by the Girni Kamgar Union — is its first great demonstration of capacity.

And in parallel to that, the communal organisations that will reshape the entire era are being founded. The Hindu Mahasabha, formally constituted in 1915, takes ideological shape with V.D. Savarkar's Hindutva of 1923, in which the conceptual move that will haunt the next century is made: the redefinition of the Hindu not as a religious community but as a nation, a rashtra, a people whose holy land and fatherland are the same. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, founded by K.B. Hedgewar in Nagpur in 1925, gives this idea organisational form. The Muslim League, in this period, is moribund — until Jinnah, who has spent the 1920s as a constitutional negotiator drafting the Fourteen Points of March 1929 in response to the Nehru Report, will revive it from 1936 onwards as a mass party.

Hold this many-threaded picture. The "stagnation" of the 1920s is, in fact, the period in which all the players for the next two decades take the field. When the Civil Disobedience Movement opens in 1930, it does not open into an empty plain; it opens into a contested landscape on which the Swarajists, the Constructive Programmers, the revolutionary socialists, the communists, the trade unions, the Akalis (whose movement to free Sikh gurdwaras from corrupt mahants has, between 1920 and 1925, produced the SGPC, the Sikh Gurdwaras Act, and the political party of the Akali Dal), the Hindu nationalists and the rebuilt Muslim League are all already arrayed.

Salt, Karachi, and the Pact

In November 1927, the British announce that a commission, headed by Sir John Simon and consisting entirely of Englishmen, will tour India to recommend the next round of constitutional reform. The all-white composition of the Simon Commission becomes the gift the Congress did not know it needed. The protests that greet the Commission's landing in Bombay in February 1928 — Simon Go Back — and that follow it through Lahore (where the lathi charge that injures Lala Lajpat Rai will, three weeks later, kill him) and through every other major city, give the Congress a pretext to break out of the long interregnum. The Madras Congress of December 1927 has already passed an Independence Resolution. The All-Parties Conference of 1928 produces the Motilal Nehru Report — the first Indian-drafted scheme of constitutional reform, important for what it commits to (universal adult franchise) and for what it cannot bring the Muslim League to accept (separate electorates). Jinnah's response of March 1929, the Fourteen Points, marks the moment from which the constitutional separation of Hindu and Muslim political projects becomes, in his account at least, irreversible.

In December 1929, at the Lahore Congress on the banks of the Ravi, the Congress passes the Purna Swaraj Resolution. The 26th of January 1930 is fixed as Independence Day, on which the Pledge will be read in every village and town the Congress can reach. (Twenty years later, when independent India fixes the date on which its constitution will come into force, it will choose this date deliberately, to braid the older fight into the new founding.)

In March 1930, after delivering an Eleven-Point Ultimatum to Lord Irwin that goes unanswered, Gandhi sets out from his ashram at Sabarmati with seventy-eight chosen volunteers and walks two hundred and forty miles to a beach at Dandi. On 6 April he picks up a fistful of natural salt from the sand. The state monopoly on salt — a tax of which the principle is that even the poorest Indian, who cannot afford to be taxed on anything else, must be taxed on the seasoning of his food — is the most regressive of all colonial impositions; it is also, by virtue of being so universal, the perfect ground on which to mount a movement that wants to draw in everyone. The Civil Disobedience Movement that erupts in the months after the Salt March is the largest mass movement the country has yet seen. Women enter Indian political life in unprecedented numbers — Sarojini Naidu leading the raid on the Dharasana Salt Works in May, the Vanar and Manjari Senas drilling in cities and villages. The Garhwali Rifles, ordered to fire on a crowd in Peshawar in April, refuse. The North-West Frontier, organised by the towering figure of Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan into the Khudai Khidmatgars (the Red Shirts), enters the freedom struggle with a discipline that astonishes Gandhi himself. In Sholapur, textile workers seize the city for a week. In the United Provinces, the No-Rent Campaign defies the British and the zamindars in a single gesture. In Bihar, the Chowkidara Tax Refusal Movement does the same. In Chittagong, in April 1930, Surya Sen — Masterda — leads an armed raid on the British armoury, holding the town for four days; Pritilata Waddedar, who fights with him, dies in 1932; Kalpana Dutt is captured and tried. The Civil Disobedience Movement is non-violent in Gandhi's central command, but along its edges the older revolutionary tradition has not been displaced.

The British, alarmed, convene the First Round Table Conference in London in November 1930 — without the Congress, which is in jail. They convene the Second in September 1931, with Gandhi, after the Gandhi-Irwin Pact of March has bought a brief truce. Gandhi goes alone, as the sole Congress representative, in a loincloth and a shawl, to negotiate with the Empire from a position the British cannot quite parse and cannot accommodate.

But it is the Karachi Congress of March 1931 — held in the immediate aftermath of the hanging of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev, on a delegate body still raw with that loss — that I think contains the deepest commitment of the entire freedom struggle. The Karachi Resolution on Fundamental Rights, drafted by Jawaharlal Nehru and adopted by the Congress, lays down for the first time the rights and economic guarantees that an independent India will guarantee its citizens: free speech, free worship, free assembly, free movement; equality before law without distinction of caste or creed or sex; free and compulsory primary education; a living wage for the industrial worker; the right to organise; the protection of women workers; the abolition of untouchability. The line from Karachi 1931 to the Constituent Assembly of 1946-49 is, in my view, the spine on which the eventual Constitution will be hung.

The Civil Disobedience Movement, like Non-Cooperation before it, will be wound down by Gandhi — once in 1931 after the Pact, then resumed in 1932 in protest at Ramsay MacDonald's Communal Award. The Communal Award awarded separate electorates to the Depressed Classes, and Gandhi opposed it not because he opposed the rights of those classes but because he believed that separate electorates would freeze them outside Hindu society forever. He undertook in September 1932 the Epic Fast unto death, which produced the Poona Pact with B.R. Ambedkar — reserving seats for the Depressed Classes within a joint electorate. (The relationship between Gandhi and Ambedkar in this episode, and what Ambedkar took from it about the limits of Gandhian moral pressure, will shape Indian constitutional politics for the next two decades.) Gandhi follows the Pact with the Harijan Tour of 1933-34, throws himself into temple-entry agitation across the country (the Vaikom Satyagraha of 1924-25 in Travancore, led by K.P. Kesava Menon and supported by Periyar, had laid this ground; the Guruvayur Satyagraha of 1931-32, led by K. Kelappan and A.K. Gopalan, carried it forward; the Travancore Temple Entry Proclamation of November 1936 marks its first great victory). Civil Disobedience is finally withdrawn in April 1934. The 1935 Government of India Act, the longest single statute the British Parliament has ever passed, is in part the Raj's response: a grant of provincial autonomy that hopes to absorb the nationalist energy into the routine politics of provincial ministry-making. (Read the 1935 Act carefully, because much of its administrative scaffolding will be borrowed wholesale into the Constitution of independent India.)

The gathering on three flanks

It is in the second half of the 1930s, between the withdrawal of Civil Disobedience and the outbreak of the Second World War, that the freedom struggle accumulates the institutional and ideological depth it will need for the final decade. Three currents thicken at once.

The first is the consolidation of the left. The Congress Socialist Party is founded in October 1934 by a group around Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev, and Minoo Masani; the All India Kisan Sabha is founded in April 1936 by Swami Sahajanand Saraswati and Professor N.G. Ranga, joining the older Oudh Kisan Sabha (1920) and Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (1929) into a national peasant organisation; the All India Progressive Writers' Association is founded the same month, joining a cultural left that includes Premchand, Saadat Hasan Manto, Faiz Ahmad Faiz, Ismat Chughtai, Mahadevi Verma. The CPI, banned in 1934, returns underground; the Dutt-Bradley Thesis of 1936 commits Indian communists to working from within the Congress. Nehru's Whither India? of 1933, his Lucknow Congress presidency of 1936, the National Planning Committee he chairs from 1938 — all of these mark the absorption of socialist thinking into the central nervous system of the Congress itself. The Karachi Resolution made the social commitment formal; the late 1930s give it institutional weight. (Notice, in passing, what the Indian capitalists are doing in this same period: they are not opposing nationalism but trying to shape it. The founding of FICCI in 1927 by G.D. Birla and Purshottamdas Thakurdas, the Bombay Plan of 1944 in which J.R.D. Tata and other big industrialists actually propose state-led economic planning — these are the moves of a domestic capital that has decided independence will, on balance, serve it better than the colonial dispensation.)

The second current is the experiment with provincial government. The 1937 elections, held under the limited franchise of the 1935 Act, return Congress majorities in eight of eleven provinces. The Congress, after the long Office Acceptance Debate of 1936-37, agrees to take office. The Congress Ministries that govern from July 1937 to October 1939 — twenty-eight months — are the first sustained Indian experiment in modern executive responsibility, and they teach lessons that will shape every choice in the next decade. They show that a Congress ministry can govern; they show, also, that ministerial office tends to dilute the radical edge of the movement. The Tripuri Crisis of March 1939, in which Subhas Chandra Bose wins re-election as Congress President against the Gandhian establishment's candidate Pattabhi Sitaramayya and is then forced to resign by the engineered non-cooperation of the Working Committee, marks the first great schism within the Congress itself. Bose founds the Forward Bloc; he will, three years later, leave India for Berlin and then Tokyo, and return on the wings of an army that fights against the Empire in Burma.

The third current is the deepening of communalism. The Muslim League, revived under Jinnah in 1936, contests the 1937 elections poorly; it learns, from that loss, that it cannot compete with the Congress on the ground of secular nationalism, and pivots, after 1937, to a politics of communal mobilisation. The Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940 commits the League to the demand for "independent states" in the Muslim-majority regions of the subcontinent — the demand that, by 1946, will have hardened into the demand for Pakistan. M.S. Golwalkar's We, Or Our Nationhood Defined of 1939 takes Savarkar's Hindutva of 1923 and pushes it to its logical limit: India is a Hindu nation; Muslims and Christians live in it on sufferance. Iqbal's Allahabad Address of December 1930 had already imagined a separate Muslim-majority polity in the north-west; Choudhry Rahmat Ali, a student in Cambridge, had named it Pakistan in a 1933 pamphlet. By 1940 the two communalisms are feeding each other.

I want you to notice, especially, the temporal closeness of these moves. Lahore Resolution: March 1940. Quit India: August 1942. Cabinet Mission: March 1946. Direct Action Day: August 1946. Independence: August 1947. The endgame, when it comes, will come fast. The structures that will determine its outcome were in place by 1939.

The war, the Quit, the Army

September 1939: Britain declares war on Germany and announces, without consulting the Indian legislatures, that India is at war as well. The Congress Ministries, which have not been consulted, resign in October-November in protest. The Muslim League, which has, declares the day of resignation "Deliverance Day." The Viceroy, Linlithgow, makes the August Offer of 1940 — promising dominion status after the war and accepting the principle of a constituent assembly to be formed afterwards. The Congress rejects it; instead, Gandhi launches the Individual Satyagraha of October 1940, a deliberately limited campaign in which selected Congressmen — Vinoba Bhave first, Nehru second, in carefully staged sequence — court arrest one by one, to register protest without paralysing the war effort.

The British position deteriorates rapidly through 1941 and into early 1942. Singapore falls in February 1942; the Japanese take Rangoon in March; the British retreat from Burma into India, with refugees streaming up the same passes. The Cripps Mission of March-April 1942, sent by a Churchill government desperate for Indian support, offers dominion status after the war, the right of provinces to opt out, the constitution to be framed by an Indian assembly — and on the question of who governs in the meantime, offers nothing. Gandhi calls it a post-dated cheque on a failing bank. Sir Stafford Cripps returns to London empty-handed.

On 8 August 1942, at the All India Congress Committee meeting at Gowalia Tank in Bombay, the Congress passes the Quit India Resolution. Gandhi's address to the meeting contains the most consequential two words he will ever utter: karenge ya marenge, do or die. Within hours, the entire Congress leadership is arrested. The movement that follows — a genuinely leaderless mass uprising in many places — is the most violent of the three great satyagrahas: police stations burned, telegraph lines cut, parallel governments declared in Satara (Nana Patil's Prati Sarkar), in Tamluk in Midnapore, and elsewhere; an underground radio operated by Usha Mehta, until her arrest. Aruna Asaf Ali, Sucheta Kripalani, Ram Manohar Lohia keep the movement going from the underground. The British put it down with great force — the most coercive military deployment within India of the entire colonial period — and by the end of 1943 the open phase is over. Gandhi, in jail, undertakes a twenty-one-day fast in February-March 1943 that brings him close to death.

But what has changed, in 1942 and after, is something the British perhaps do not yet recognise. The Indian Civil Service is now overwhelmingly Indian; the army is overwhelmingly Indian; the police are overwhelmingly Indian. The colonial state has been Indianised at every level except the very top, and the loyalty of those Indianised levels can no longer be assumed. In the same months as Quit India, in Singapore, the captured Indian soldiers of the British army are being reorganised by Captain Mohan Singh and then by Subhas Chandra Bose — who has reached Asia via Berlin — into the Azad Hind Fauj, the Indian National Army. The Provisional Government of Free India (Azad Hind Government) is declared in October 1943; Rash Behari Bose's Indian Independence League, founded the previous year, provides the political wing; the Rani of Jhansi Regiment, an all-women combat unit led by Captain Lakshmi Sahgal, fights alongside the men. The INA's military campaign — the Battles of Imphal and Kohima, March-July 1944 — ends in defeat. But its political consequences, when the captured INA officers are put on trial in the Red Fort in November 1945, will be far larger than its military ones. The trial of Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Kumar Sahgal and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon — a Muslim, a Hindu and a Sikh, defended by a Congress legal team led by Bhulabhai Desai and including Nehru — produces the largest expression of national feeling since Quit India. The British, faced with a country that now openly identifies with men who had taken up arms against the Crown, drop the charges. Three months later, in February 1946, the ratings of the Royal Indian Navy mutiny in Bombay; the entire Indian fleet is in revolt for nearly a week. The British realise, perhaps fully for the first time, that they no longer hold the apparatus of force.

In Bengal, in 1943, three million people die. The Bengal Famine — caused not by absolute scarcity but by the combination of British wartime requisition policies, the loss of Burmese rice, hoarding, the denial of relief, and the indifference of a Churchill government that decided other theatres were more important — is the single largest catastrophe of British rule in India. The Indian People's Theatre Association, formed that same year, makes the famine the subject of K.A. Abbas's Dharti Ke Lal (1946). Of all the indictments of the Raj, the famine is the indictment from which the Raj has, in any honest accounting, never fully recovered. The same years see the Tebhaga Movement of Bengal sharecroppers (1946-47), the Telangana Rebellion against the Nizam of Hyderabad (1946-51), the Punnapra-Vayalar uprising in Travancore (1946) — the rural ferment that the Quit India period unleashes is not contained when Quit India ends.

The endgame

A Labour government takes office in Britain in July 1945. Attlee, the new Prime Minister, has neither the appetite nor the resources for a long colonial war; the Wavell Plan and the Simla Conference of June 1945 represent the first serious British move towards transfer of power, and they fail not on Indian objections but on Jinnah's insistence that the Muslim League is the sole representative of Indian Muslims. The 1945-46 elections to the central and provincial legislatures confirm the polarisation: the Congress sweeps the general seats, the Muslim League sweeps the Muslim seats. The Cabinet Mission of March-May 1946 — three British ministers sent to negotiate the structure of a transfer of power — proposes a three-tier federation in which Muslim-majority provinces are grouped together at an intermediate tier, with the central government left with only defence, foreign affairs and communications. The Congress and the League both, briefly, accept the plan. Then the Congress, through Nehru in July, qualifies its acceptance in a way that suggests the groupings will not bind. The League withdraws.

On 16 August 1946 — Direct Action Day — Jinnah, now persuaded that constitutional negotiation will not deliver Pakistan, calls on Indian Muslims to demonstrate. In Calcutta, the demonstration becomes the Great Calcutta Killing: four days of communal carnage in which between five and ten thousand people are killed. The riots spread to Noakhali in October, to Bihar in November. Gandhi spends these months walking from village to village in the Noakhali countryside, alone, asking the survivors to forgive each other and to live together. He is, by this point, the most isolated figure in Indian politics. The Interim Government of September 1946, with Nehru as de facto Prime Minister and the League initially boycotting and then joining only to obstruct, governs a country sliding into civil war.

In February 1947, Attlee announces that the British will leave by June 1948 at the latest. Mountbatten arrives in March 1947 as the last Viceroy and brings the date forward, by his own decision, to August 1947. The Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947 accepts partition; the Indian Independence Act of July 1947 enacts it; the Radcliffe Boundary Commission, headed by a man who has never visited India and who will refuse, throughout his life, to accept his fee for the work, draws lines through Punjab and Bengal that he himself will not see executed. The princely states — five hundred and sixty-odd of them, held in suspended animation through the entire freedom struggle by an All India States Peoples Conference (founded 1927) that the Congress recognised but did not absorb — are now told by the Empire that paramountcy will simply lapse, leaving each ruler theoretically free to accede to either dominion or to neither. The integration of these states, between June 1947 and 1949, is the unfinished business of the freedom struggle that Sardar Patel, with V.P. Menon as his secretary, will complete in the next era.

On the night of 14-15 August 1947, in the Constituent Assembly that has been sitting in Delhi since the previous December and that will continue, in another mode, as the constitution-making body of the new republic, Nehru rises to deliver the address that the entire era has been driving towards:

Long years ago we made a tryst with destiny, and now the time comes when we shall redeem our pledge, not wholly or in full measure, but very substantially. At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom.

Read the qualifying clause. Not wholly, or in full measure, but very substantially. It is the most honest sentence in the speech. The pledge of Lahore 1929 has been redeemed, but not in the form it was made. The unitary Indian republic that the Karachi Resolution of 1931 imagined has been split. The price of the freedom that the era has won is the partition of the subcontinent, and the violence of that partition — fifteen million displaced, somewhere between two hundred thousand and two million dead, women abducted and reclaimed and refused, families broken across a line drawn in haste — is the wound the new republic will carry into its first years, and arguably into all the years that follow.

Gandhi is not in Delhi on 15 August. He is in Calcutta, fasting, trying to stop the killing. He will be assassinated in five months by a man who believes Gandhi has betrayed the Hindu nation by the simple act of refusing to hate.

What the era leaves us

The era closes with two simultaneous beginnings. The apparatus of the Raj transfers, on 15 August 1947, into Indian hands; the apparatus of constitution-making, which has been in Indian hands since the Constituent Assembly first met on 9 December 1946 (under Sachchidananda Sinha as temporary president, then under Rajendra Prasad's elected presidency, with B.N. Rau as constitutional advisor and Ambedkar's seven-member Drafting Committee at its centre), continues for two more years and three months. The Constitution is adopted on 26 November 1949 and brought into force on 26 January 1950 — the date the Lahore Pledge had named, twenty years earlier. The era of the freedom struggle, in this sense, does not end on 15 August 1947; it ends on 26 January 1950, when the document the freedom struggle has been writing in fragments since the Karachi Resolution of 1931 finally takes legal force.

But before you go forward into the next era, hold what this one has built. It has built mass-mobilising politics — three full satyagrahas, with the methods, the cadres, the institutional residue. It has built the Congress as a national party with roots in every district. It has built the constitutional commitments — universal franchise, fundamental rights, equality of citizenship — that will be enacted into the Constitution. It has built, in parallel and against the grain of the Congress mainstream, a labour movement, a peasant movement, a women's movement, a cultural left, a revolutionary tradition that fed Bhagat Singh and the INA in turn, and a princely-states democratic movement that carries the freedom struggle into the half of the country the Congress could not directly enter. It has built, also, the antagonisms that the Constitution will not resolve: the communal cleavage, hardened into partition; the agrarian question, deferred to the post-independence land reforms; the question of who, in a republic of nominal equals, will actually exercise power. And it has produced, in Gandhi, the most original political figure of the twentieth century, and in Bhagat Singh, Bose, Ambedkar, Patel, Nehru, Jinnah, Azad — and in the women who entered Indian political life in this era and will not leave it again — a political generation whose like the country has not since seen.

In parallel, while all of this was happening on the subcontinent, the world outside was undergoing the convulsions that frame the era from outside: the Russian Revolution had, in 1917, set the political imagination of an entire generation of Indian leftists; the Great Depression of 1929 had dragged down the export markets of Indian agriculture and pushed Indian capitalists toward planning; Hitler's rise from the Enabling Act of 1933 through the Nuremberg Laws to the Holocaust had given the Indian nationalist movement an ethical occasion to define its own anti-fascism (the Munich Agreement of 1938, V.K. Krishna Menon in London, Dr Dwarkanath Kotnis in China, the Congress Foreign Department's solidarity with the Spanish Republic and with Ethiopia and with Vietnam under Ho Chi Minh); Japan's entry into the war had brought the front of that conflict to Imphal; the Bretton Woods system of 1944 had begun to construct the post-war economic order into which independent India would be born. The era of the freedom struggle is also the era in which Indian nationalism becomes a global political identity, claiming a place not as the local affair of a single colonised population but as one chapter of the worldwide unmaking of European empire — a chapter whose closure in August 1947 will, within a decade, set the example that decolonisation movements from Ghana to Indonesia to Vietnam will follow.

When you open the timeline that follows, you will see all of this laid out as concept cards, arranged across the twenty-eight years and the twenty-five thematic streams. What I have tried to give you here is the spine that holds the cards together. The cards are the trees; this was the shape of the forest.
