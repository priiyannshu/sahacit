10 — The Republic and Its Tests: Nation-Building to the Edge of 1991

Picture a polling booth in a village in the United Provinces in February 1952. The booth officer holds a ballot for a woman who cannot read. She does not know the name of the candidate she is choosing; she knows the symbol — a pair of oxen, a hut, an ear of wheat. She has been told, through weeks of preparatory effort by the Election Commission under Sukumar Sen, that this act — dropping a folded paper into a steel box — is the act by which she becomes, for a brief moment, the equal of any person in the republic. She does it. She walks out. Somewhere nearby, in the same village, a man who was a zamindar six months ago stands in the same queue as his former tenant. The queue is the republic's first argument: that the citizen, abstractly, is one.

India in 1952 had an adult literacy rate of eighteen percent. No established democracy had tried universal adult franchise at this scale, and most political theorists thought the experiment would fail within a decade. They were wrong. But it is worth sitting with the ambition for a moment before you follow the story forward, because the era we are about to enter — forty-four years of building, testing, breaking, and restoring the Indian republic — is, in its deepest sense, the story of what that booth officer's act was eventually asked to carry.

The previous era had ended with the tryst and the wound: independence in August 1947, and a partition that killed between two hundred thousand and two million people and moved fifteen million more across lines that had not existed a month before. The Constitution was still being written when the blood dried. The republic was being assembled, simultaneously, out of the wreckage of partition, the unfinished integration of five hundred and sixty-odd princely states, the assassination of the man who had held the Congress together, and the daily logistics of governing three hundred and fifty million people who had never been governed by themselves before. The era we are entering is the era in which that assembled republic — fragile, improvised, stubbornly optimistic — is asked to prove what it can sustain. The answer, across forty-four years and twelve prime ministers, is more than you might expect, and less than the founders hoped.

The era has a shape. There is a long arc of building — the Nehru years, in which the republic's institutions, plans, and external posture are laid down — followed by the Indira years, in which the same institutions are pressed to breaking point and beyond. And running through both, as a thread beneath the surface, is the era's central jurisprudential idea: that the Constitution has a basic structure which Parliament itself cannot amend away. It is this idea — call it the constitutional core, the republic's skeleton, the limit that holds — that will be this chapter's recurring metaphor. The Emergency of 1975 is the moment the basic structure is attacked most directly; the Supreme Court's Kesavananda Bharati judgment of 1973 is the moment it is formally declared inviolable. Between those two dates — and long after — the history of Indian democracy is, in large part, the history of who is trying to amend the basic structure and who is trying to preserve it.

The republic assembled

Before the Constitution, there was Patel. Vallabhbhai Patel and his secretary V.P. Menon spent the eighteen months between June 1947 and January 1950 persuading, cajoling, threatening, and on one occasion simply deploying the army to bring five hundred and sixty-two princely states into the Indian Union. The legal mechanism was the Instrument of Accession; the political mechanism was Patel's combination of personal authority, bureaucratic precision, and a willingness to use force where words failed. Junagadh, whose Nawab had signed accession to Pakistan despite a Hindu majority population, was reclaimed after a plebiscite in November 1947. Kashmir, whose Hindu Maharaja had dithered and then signed accession in October 1947 as Pakistani-backed tribal raiders closed on Srinagar, became the first Indo-Pak war and a wound that would not close. Hyderabad, the largest holdout — a Muslim Nizam over a largely Hindu population, dreaming of independence — fell to the Police Action of September 1948, a seventy-two-hour military operation that ended five centuries of Hyderabadi sovereignty. By the time the Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950, the map that most Indians would recognise as India existed, more or less.

That Constitution is one of the great documents of the twentieth century. Its architect, the chairman of the Drafting Committee, was B.R. Ambedkar — the man the Congress mainstream had spent three decades in uncomfortable negotiation with, the man Gandhi had fought over the Poona Pact, the man who had resigned from Nehru's first cabinet in 1951 when the Hindu Code Bills stalled. It is one of history's ironies that a man who had so little reason to trust the Indian state was the principal engineer of the document that constrained it. The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950 — a date chosen deliberately to recall the Purna Swaraj declaration of that date in 1930, braiding the new founding into the older fight. Its Preamble opened with words that no other Indian document had dared:

WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity of the Nation...

Read those words against the country they described in 1950 — fractured by partition, scarred by communal violence, with a third of its population below any measure of subsistence. The Preamble is an act of extraordinary optimism. Or of extraordinary ambition. The question this era will spend forty-four years arguing is whether ambition and optimism, in the absence of the social conditions they assume, are enough.

Six weeks after the Constitution came into force, Gandhi had been dead for two years. Nathuram Godse, an ex-RSS man who believed Gandhi had betrayed the Hindu nation by demanding India pay Pakistan its share of pre-partition assets, had shot him on 30 January 1948 at Birla House. The RSS was banned until July 1949. Gandhi's assassination had, paradoxically, given the Congress government a brief political opening to push through things the Mahatma had resisted: industrialisation, the Hindu Code Bills, engagement with the Soviet Union. But his absence also removed the one figure who could speak across the constituencies the freedom struggle had assembled. What remained was a coalition — and coalitions need to be managed.

The first general election of 1951-52, the largest democratic exercise the world had seen, returned the Congress with 364 seats in a 489-seat Lok Sabha. Nehru had campaigned everywhere, travelling forty thousand kilometers by air, rail, and car, addressing rally after rally across a country whose voters had never voted before. The Congress System — the party that simultaneously held the government and claimed to represent the movement — had its first great democratic validation. The question of whether it could hold that validation without the energy of the freedom struggle to sustain it would take another decade to answer.

The Nehruvian wager

The republic that Nehru built in the fifteen years between 1947 and 1962 rested on three pillars: the Constitution, the Plan, and the Cabinet. The Constitution was Ambedkar's; the Plan was Nehru's; the Cabinet, for the first decade, was anchored by Patel's authority until his death in December 1950. After Patel, Nehru governed in a style that was simultaneously visionary and one-directional — a man certain he was right, surrounded by a party gradually learning to tell him only what he wanted to hear.

The wager Nehru made, and India made with him, was what he called the "socialistic pattern of society" — the Avadi Resolution of the Congress in 1955 committed the party to it formally. It was neither socialism nor capitalism; it was the mixed economy, a term worth holding, because the mixed economy is the era's characteristic form of in-betweenness. The state would command the commanding heights — steel, heavy industry, defence, infrastructure — while leaving smaller enterprise to the private sector. The Planning Commission, established on 15 March 1950, would coordinate this through Five-Year Plans. The Second Plan of 1956-61, designed by the statistician P.C. Mahalanobis, was the one that mattered: it committed India to heavy public-sector industry as the engine of development. The steel plants at Bhilai (with Soviet help), Rourkela (German), and Durgapur (British) went up in this period. The Bhakra-Nangal dam, the Hirakud dam, the Damodar Valley Corporation — Nehru called these dams the "temples of modern India," and he meant it with the seriousness with which previous generations had built temples. The IITs, the CSIR, the BARC — Homi Bhabha's atomic energy establishment, which would become the foundation of India's three-stage nuclear programme — were the scientific infrastructure of this wager. The three akademis — Sangeet Natak, Sahitya, Lalit Kala — and the ICCR were the cultural infrastructure.

The Hindu Code Bills of 1955-56 — the Hindu Marriage Act, the Hindu Succession Act, the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act — were Ambedkar's last significant act in government before he resigned in 1951 in frustration at their dilution. They passed in partial form after his departure: they gave Hindu women rights of divorce, inheritance, and guardianship that they had never legally held before. They are among the most consequential social legislation of independent India's first decade.

The weakness of the Nehruvian wager was not in its ambition but in what it couldn't manage. The land reforms that should have redistributed the agrarian surplus to actually produce a rural economy capable of funding industrialisation were enacted patchily — zamindari abolition in UP and Bihar moved quickly, the ceiling laws almost everywhere moved not at all. The Licence-Permit-Quota Raj — the intricate system of approvals, quotas, and import controls that the mixed economy required to function — created a bureaucratic apparatus that would, within a decade, become the primary obstacle to the private investment it was meant to control. The Congress Party's hold on power produced what political scientists would later call the "Congress System" — a coalition of caste, class, and regional interests managed more through patronage than policy. And the "Hindu rate of growth" — the 3.5% annual GDP growth that the planning model produced, compared to the 8-10% that would have been needed to eliminate poverty within a generation — was not a Hindu failing but a structural one: the model could not do what it was asked to do, at the speed it was being asked to do it.

The mixed economy was the era's characteristic form of in-betweenness. Neither planned nor free, neither socialist nor capitalist, it sustained a republic that was too poor for either the welfare state or the laissez-faire. 1991 is when the in-betweenness becomes, finally, untenable.

The world Nehru imagined

The foreign policy of the Nehru years is one of the more interesting experiments in the diplomatic history of the twentieth century: the attempt by a newly decolonised country, weak in military and economic terms, to project itself as a moral force in international politics. Nehru called this non-alignment — not neutrality, he insisted, but active independence. In April 1954, in a joint declaration with Chinese Premier Zhou Enlai, he codified it as Panchsheel: the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence. Mutual respect for territorial integrity; mutual non-aggression; mutual non-interference; equality; peaceful coexistence. In April 1955, at the Bandung Conference in Indonesia, India was at the centre of the first great meeting of newly decolonised Asian and African nations — a moment that marks the zenith of what Nehru's foreign policy imagined. The Belgrade Conference of 1961, which formalised the Non-Aligned Movement, brought together Nehru, Tito of Yugoslavia, and Nasser of Egypt as co-founders of a third way in a world that was being asked to choose between Washington and Moscow.

The problem with Panchsheel was China. India had ceded its claim on Tibet in the 1954 agreement; when the Dalai Lama fled to India in 1959 and was granted asylum, Beijing took it as a provocation. The "Forward Policy" — Nehru's decision to establish posts in disputed territory along the Himalayan frontier — gave China a pretext. In October 1962, Chinese forces launched simultaneous offensives in Aksai Chin and the North-East Frontier Agency. The Indian Army, under-equipped, under-prepared, and deployed in terrain that the political leadership had never properly reconnoitered, collapsed in three weeks. The ceasefire left India having lost territory on both fronts. V.K. Krishna Menon, the Defence Minister whose negligence was a proximate cause, resigned. Nehru died in May 1964, diminished by the defeat and by the knowledge that the world he had imagined — of Afro-Asian solidarity and peaceful coexistence — had proved fragile against the hard calculus of territorial ambition.

Lal Bahadur Shastri, who became Prime Minister in June 1964, had eighteen months. They mattered. The 1965 Indo-Pak war — triggered by Pakistan's Operation Gibraltar, an infiltration of irregulars into Kashmir — was fought to a military stalemate, but it produced from Shastri something Nehru had never quite managed: a defiant popular politics. Jai Jawan Jai Kisan — honour the soldier, honour the farmer — was a slogan that reframed what the republic owed its working majority. Food Corporation of India, the Agricultural Prices Commission, the early infrastructure of what would become the Green Revolution — all of this was put in motion in these eighteen months, often by the insistence of Agriculture Minister C. Subramaniam working with agronomist M.S. Swaminathan and the American plant geneticist Norman Borlaug. Shastri signed the Tashkent Agreement in January 1966 and died, by cardiac arrest, that same night. The mystery of his death — it has never been fully investigated — is one of the republic's unresolved loose ends. Gulzarilal Nanda became acting Prime Minister for the second time; the Congress chose Indira Gandhi, Nehru's daughter, who was believed, by the Syndicate that ran the party, to be controllable.

She was not.

The high-tide and the rupture

Indira Gandhi arrived in the prime ministership in 1966 carrying the disadvantage of being underestimated. The devaluation of the rupee in June 1966 — forced by foreign aid conditions, necessary in retrospect, politically catastrophic — handed her enemies a weapon. The elections of 1967 returned Congress with a reduced majority; non-Congress governments were in office in nine states, including Tamil Nadu under the DMK's C.N. Annadurai, who had swept to power on the anti-Hindi agitation of 1965. The Congress System, which had held the first three elections through sheer organisational weight, was cracking.

Indira responded by moving left. The presidential election of 1969 — the Congress Syndicate's candidate V.V. Giri versus her own unofficial backing — became the occasion of the Congress split. The Indian National Congress (O) — for "organisation" — remained with the Syndicate; the Indian National Congress (R) — for "ruling," or "requisitioned," depending on who you ask — was Indira's, with most of the MPs. Within days she had announced the nationalisation of fourteen major banks. Bank nationalisation was not, in itself, novel — it had been on the Congress agenda for decades — but its timing, and the manner of it, was a political statement: this government would use the state against concentrations of private capital. The abolition of the privy purses of the former princely rulers followed in 1971. Both moves were constitutionally challenged; both would feed the jurisprudential battles that produced Kesavananda Bharati.

Garibi Hatao — abolish poverty — was the slogan of the 1971 election, the most consequential election of the post-independence era until 1977. Indira won 352 seats in the 545-seat Lok Sabha. The mandate was a phenomenon. Six months later, she used it.

The Bangladesh War of December 1971 was the republic's finest military hour and Indira Gandhi's political zenith. East Pakistan had been in revolt since March 1971, when the Pakistani army moved against the Awami League after Sheikh Mujibur Rahman's electoral victory; by August, ten million refugees had crossed into India. The Indo-Soviet Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation, signed in August 1971, gave India diplomatic cover against a potential Chinese intervention in support of Pakistan. The war itself lasted thirteen days; Pakistan's Eastern Command surrendered on 16 December 1971; Bangladesh was born. The Simla Agreement of 1972 established the Line of Control in Kashmir and the principle that all disputes would be resolved bilaterally. Indira Gandhi, in these months, was called Durga by Atal Bihari Vajpayee. The compliment contained, as many do, a warning.

On 18 May 1974, at Pokhran in the Rajasthan desert, India detonated its first nuclear device. The Atomic Energy Commission, operating under the three-stage nuclear programme Homi Bhabha had designed in the late 1940s — with the TIFR, the AEC, and the BARC as its institutional spine — had been building toward this for a decade. Raja Ramanna led the team. The code name was "Smiling Buddha." India called it a peaceful nuclear explosion. The NPT, which India had refused to sign in 1968 as discriminatory — only the five states that had tested before 1967 could keep their weapons — had predicted this. Pokhran-I changed the strategic calculus of South Asia permanently.

Jayaprakash Narayan — JP, the man who had been Gandhi's conscience and Nehru's peer, who had gone into voluntary political retirement for twenty years and devoted himself to Vinoba Bhave's Bhoodan movement — re-emerged in 1974 to lead what he called Total Revolution. The JP Movement was a student uprising in Bihar and Gujarat against corruption, price rise, and the general decay of the Congress system that became, improbably, the largest oppositional mobilisation since the freedom struggle. On 12 June 1975, Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha of the Allahabad High Court set aside Indira Gandhi's election from Rae Bareli on grounds of electoral malpractice — a corrupt official had used government resources for her campaign. She was barred from holding elected office for six years.

She declared a National Emergency on 25 June 1975.

The Emergency lasted twenty-one months and is the only rupture in Indian democracy. Article 352 — which permits Emergency proclamations — had been used before, in 1962 and 1971, in the context of wars. The Emergency of 1975 was the first use of Article 352 against the Indian polity itself. All fundamental rights were suspended. Over 110,000 people were detained under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act without trial — among them JP, Morarji Desai, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, L.K. Advani, George Fernandes. The press was censored. The ADM Jabalpur case of 1976 was the Supreme Court's darkest hour: the court, asked whether habeas corpus survived an Emergency, said no. Justice H.R. Khanna dissented alone. He was superseded for the Chief Justiceship. The 42nd Amendment — the "Mini-Constitution" of December 1976 — added "Socialist," "Secular," and "Integrity" to the Preamble, introduced Fundamental Duties, subordinated Fundamental Rights to Directive Principles, and curtailed judicial review. Sanjay Gandhi's informal authority produced forced sterilisations — 8.3 million in 1976-77 — and the demolition of slums in Turkman Gate, Delhi.

Indira Gandhi called elections in January 1977. The ballot boxes came back against her. The Congress lost; the Janata Party — a coalition of everything that was not Congress — formed the first non-Congress government under Morarji Desai. The Shah Commission investigated the Emergency's excesses. The 44th Amendment of 1978 reversed the worst of the 42nd: it restored judicial review, required that Cabinet advice for Emergency be in writing, changed the trigger from "internal disturbance" to "armed rebellion," and removed the right to property from Fundamental Rights (making it a legal right under Article 300A). Mandal Commission was appointed in December 1978 — its report, submitted in 1980, was shelved. The Janata coalition collapsed in July 1979 over the question of whether ministers could hold dual membership in the RSS. Charan Singh governed for five months without ever facing Parliament. Indira Gandhi returned in January 1980 with a larger majority than 1971.

What the court preserved

To understand the Emergency, you need to understand the legal battle that preceded it — and the judgment that, paradoxically, made the Emergency's constitutional excess eventually reversible.

The post-independence decades had seen a systematic contest between Parliament, which asserted the right to amend any part of the Constitution, and the Supreme Court, which increasingly asserted a limit to that right. Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965) had upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights. Golaknath (1967) had reversed this, holding that Fundamental Rights could not be amended at all. Parliament, led by Indira Gandhi's government after 1971, responded with the 24th and 25th Amendments reasserting Parliament's supremacy.

The Kesavananda Bharati case, argued before a thirteen-judge bench and decided on 24 April 1973, settled the question by a margin of seven to six — and, in settling it, produced the most consequential single judgment in Indian constitutional history. Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution, the court said, but could not destroy its basic structure. The basic structure — which the court declined to enumerate exhaustively, leaving itself room to identify features of it over time — included constitutional supremacy, the republican form of government, the separation of powers, judicial review, federalism, and fundamental rights. Amend as you will; you may not amend the skeleton.

Notice what this means. The court is saying that there is a constitutional core — something like a basic structure — that holds even against Parliament. It is, constitutionally, what I have been calling the era's metaphor: the republic has a skeleton that cannot be amended away. The Emergency was, in part, Indira Gandhi's attempt to amend that skeleton. The 42nd Amendment tried; the 39th Amendment tried to place her own election above judicial review. When the Janata government came to power, the Supreme Court used Minerva Mills (1980) — coincidentally decided after Indira's return — to strike down the portions of the 42nd Amendment that had curtailed judicial review, on the ground that judicial review was itself part of the basic structure.

Maneka Gandhi (1978) — a case brought by Indira Gandhi's own daughter-in-law — gave Article 21 (the right to life and personal liberty) a reading that the framers had not imagined: the procedure by which liberty could be taken away must be not merely legal but just, fair, and reasonable. The narrow, positivist Article 21 of the A.K. Gopalan case (1950) was dead; the expansive, substantive Article 21 that would eventually encompass the right to education, the right to health, and the right to a dignified livelihood was born.

The Shah Bano case of 1985 arrived at a different kind of constitutional moment. Shah Bano, a sixty-two-year-old Muslim woman divorced by her husband of forty-three years, sought maintenance under the Criminal Procedure Code. The Supreme Court granted it, going further to note that the Quran itself did not support the denial of women's maintenance rights. The judgment was correct in law and generous in spirit. What followed was the political capitulation that has never been fully forgiven: Rajiv Gandhi's government, facing Muslim conservative pressure and fearing electoral consequences, passed the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986, effectively reversing the judgment for Muslim women. The basic structure of equal citizenship — which the Constitution had promised — was bent here, not by the court, but by Parliament in deference to a constituency.

Blue Star, the accords, and the gathering polarisation

Indira Gandhi returned to power in January 1980 with no particular economic programme — the economy by this point was a tangle of the Plan and the Licence Raj and the oil shock adjustments and the BOP deficit beginning to build — and with her attention increasingly absorbed by regional crises she had, in some cases, helped create.

The Punjab crisis was the era's most catastrophic regional failure. The Akali Dal, the Sikh political party, had adopted the Anandpur Sahib Resolution in 1973, demanding greater autonomy for Punjab and a share of river waters. Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale — a militant preacher whom Congress had, in an act of spectacular short-sightedness, initially patronised to split the Akali vote — turned the demand into a violent campaign from his fortified position inside the Golden Temple complex in Amritsar. Operation Blue Star, launched on 1 June 1984, sent the Indian Army into the holiest site in Sikhism. The operation succeeded militarily. Its political consequences were irreversible: Indira Gandhi was assassinated on 31 October 1984 by two of her Sikh bodyguards. In the three days that followed, anti-Sikh mobs killed over two thousand seven hundred people in Delhi, with what evidence suggests was the connivance or at least the passivity of the Congress administration. The Bhopal Gas Tragedy occurred five weeks later, on 3 December.

Rajiv Gandhi won the December 1984 election with 415 seats — the largest majority in Indian parliamentary history — on a wave of sympathy and shock that was the republic's most troubling election. He was forty years old, a former pilot, and had been reluctantly pulled into politics after his brother Sanjay's death in 1980. He had the mandate; he did not have the political instincts to match it. The 52nd Amendment of 1985 — the Anti-Defection Law, which added the Tenth Schedule to the Constitution — was one of his early accomplishments: it barred elected members from switching parties post-election, addressing a pathology of Indian politics that had been destabilising governments for two decades.

Three regional accords followed in 1985-86. The Punjab Accord of July 1985 (Rajiv-Longowal) transferred Chandigarh to Punjab and set up a commission on river waters; Harchand Singh Longowal, who had signed it, was assassinated within a month. The Assam Accord of August 1985 set a citizenship cutoff date of 25 March 1971 for detection of illegal immigrants and produced the Asom Gana Parishad as a new political force. The Mizo Accord of June 1986 brought Laldenga and the Mizo National Front into democratic politics after twenty years of insurgency — the only accord of the three that held cleanly, and perhaps the republic's most successful negotiated peace.

Meanwhile, the Babri Masjid and the Ram Janmabhoomi agitation were gathering. The dispute over the site in Ayodhya — a mosque built in the sixteenth century on a site that Hindu nationalists claimed as the birthplace of Ram — had been in legal limbo since 1949, when idols were placed inside the mosque and it was locked. In 1986, the Rajiv government unlocked it for Hindu worship — a decision that appeared to be a balancing act for the Shah Bano reversal and that instead ignited the agitation that would define the next decade. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad launched its campaign for a temple on the site. The BJP — founded in 1980 from the old Jana Sangh — had two seats in 1984. It would have eighty-five in 1989.

In 1990, V.P. Singh — who had broken from Rajiv over Bofors, won the 1989 election with National Front support, and formed a minority government — implemented the Mandal Commission's recommendation of a 27% reservation for Other Backward Classes in central government services and educational institutions. The commission had submitted its report in 1980; it had been shelved for a decade. The upper-caste backlash — Rajiv Goswami's self-immolation in September 1990, followed by a hundred and fifty-nine more — was the republic's worst caste violence since the Poona Pact. L.K. Advani launched his Ram Rath Yatra in September, driving a decorated Toyota van toward Ayodhya; he was arrested by Lalu Prasad Yadav in Bihar in October. The BJP withdrew support from V.P. Singh's government. The Mandal-Mandir polarisation — caste rights versus Hindu nationalism — had set the terms of Indian politics for the next generation.

The social movements of the era ran in parallel to all of this, often unreported. Chipko — the forest-hugging movement of the Himalayan foothills, where Chandi Prasad Bhatt and Sunderlal Bahuguna and, crucially, the village women of Reni led by Gaura Devi had in 1973 wrapped their arms around the trees to prevent commercial felling — had produced the Forest Conservation Act of 1980. Medha Patkar's Narmada Bachao Andolan, from 1985, had turned the Sardar Sarovar dam into a national argument about development, displacement, and whose India the republic was being built for. The Dalit Panthers of Maharashtra (1972) and Kanshi Ram's BSP (1984) were building a political assertion out of the Ambedkarite tradition that the Congress had absorbed but never delivered.

In science, India had been building quietly. The ISRO, established on 15 August 1969 under Vikram Sarabhai's vision, launched Aryabhata in 1975 and put the Rohini satellite into orbit with an indigenous SLV-3 in 1980 — the first country in Asia to do both. INSAT-1A in 1982 began the satellite communications infrastructure that would eventually wire the country. The three Doordarshan channels brought the 1982 Asian Games and the 1983 Cricket World Cup into living rooms across India.

The gathering 1991

By 1990, the republic's fiscal position was in crisis. The BOP — balance of payments — deficit had been building since the mid-1980s, fuelled by rising imports, an unfavourable oil price, declining remittances from the Gulf after the Iraqi invasion of Kuwait, and a government that had borrowed its way through the decade. By early 1991, India's foreign exchange reserves had fallen to the equivalent of two weeks of imports. The Reserve Bank of India shipped gold to London as collateral for an emergency IMF loan. The gold was the republic's embarrassment made literal.

Chandra Shekhar's caretaker government was in office when the election was called in May 1991. On 21 May, in Tamil Nadu, Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated by an LTTE suicide bomber during a campaign rally. The election continued. P.V. Narasimha Rao formed a minority Congress government. On 24 July 1991, Manmohan Singh, the Finance Minister, stood up in the Lok Sabha to present a budget that would, in the months that followed, dismantle the Licence Raj, open the economy to foreign direct investment, devalue the rupee by forty percent, and begin the unwinding of the mixed economy that Nehru had built.

The era ends here, at the door of Era 11. The forty-four years between 1947 and 1991 had produced something that no political theorist of 1947 had confidently predicted: a functioning, if imperfect, democratic republic; eleven general elections that produced alternations of power; a judiciary that had, at its best moments, held the constitution's basic structure against the largest parliamentary majorities the country had ever seen; a nuclear capability; a space programme; a Green Revolution that had ended the threat of famine; a White Revolution under Verghese Kurien's Operation Flood that had made India the world's largest milk producer. They had also produced the Emergency, the anti-Sikh riots, the Bhopal Gas Tragedy, the Mandal-Mandir polarisation, the Babri Masjid unlocking, and a fiscal crisis that forced the most painful economic adjustment in independent India's history.

What the era leaves us

The era leaves us with an argument, not a settlement.

The argument is about the basic structure — not only of the Constitution, but of the republic itself. What, exactly, is the core of this experiment? The Preamble's language — Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity — has never been repealed. The basic structure doctrine has never been overturned. Universal adult franchise has never been suspended. The institutions — the Election Commission, the CAG, the Supreme Court, the Finance Commission — have survived everything thrown at them, including an Emergency that tried to make the Emergency unjusticiable. These are the load-bearing elements of the Indian republic, the bones that the Kesavananda Bharati judgment formalised into doctrine.

But around those bones, the flesh of the republic is contested. The linguistic states created by the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 — produced by Potti Sriramulu's fast-unto-death in 1952, the Fazal Ali Commission's report in 1955, and a political recognition that Hindi alone could not hold a multilingual country together — had, by the 1970s, produced the Dravidian politics of Tamil Nadu, the Telugu pride of N.T. Rama Rao's TDP, the Left Front's thirty-four-year democratic government in West Bengal, the Naga and Mizo negotiations, the Assam Accord. Federalism, in India, is not an arrangement that was designed and implemented; it is one that was argued into existence by regional assertion against central resistance, and has been renegotiated continuously ever since.

The planning architecture — seven Five-Year Plans, the Planning Commission, the PSU ecosystem, the bank nationalisation, the MRTP and FERA regulations — had produced the "Hindu rate of growth" and the mixed economy's distinctive inability to either plan effectively or compete freely. The Mahalanobis model had built an industrial base; it had not built a dynamic economy. The in-betweenness was running out of road.

The Cold War parallel — the Bandung world that Nehru helped build, the Belgrade non-alignment, the Indo-Soviet Treaty of 1971, the NAM summits, India's opposition to the NPT — is about to dissolve. The Berlin Wall fell in November 1989. The Soviet Union will dissolve in December 1991. The world in which non-alignment made strategic sense is ending. India will enter Era 11 without the external framing that had shaped its foreign policy for four decades.

What the era also leaves is the social debt it has deferred. The agrarian question — which land reforms partially addressed and Green Revolution partially resolved and which the rural poverty surveys have never stopped documenting — has been answered for food security but not for equity. The caste question — which Mandal has detonated into electoral politics but not resolved into social mobility — will define the next decade. The communal question — which the Constitution had tried to answer with secularism and which the Shah Bano reversal and Babri Masjid unlocking have put back into play — will produce the Babri Masjid demolition of December 1992 as Era 11's first crisis.

And it leaves us Ambedkar's Constitution — unamendable at its core, living at its edges, argued over in every election, in every courtroom, in every street where someone is told that the republic's promise does not apply to them. The bones hold. The question the next era will ask is whether the flesh can finally be made to match them.
