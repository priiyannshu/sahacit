4 — Where rivers come from and where they go

Every drop of water that falls on India today was, at some point in the recent past, hanging in the air over the Indian Ocean. Before that, it was being held in the ocean itself — in what the planet keeps as its enormous liquid reserve, the 96.5 per cent of all water on Earth that sits in the oceans and goes nowhere useful for land until the sun lifts it. The sun does lift it. About 502,800 cubic kilometres of water evaporate from the ocean surface every year. Most of that water returns to the ocean as rain over the ocean — but a small fraction, roughly 9 per cent, crosses from the ocean into the air over land and comes down as rain or snow over continents. That fraction is the hydrological cycle, and every river in the world is the runoff phase of it: rain falls, what doesn't soak in or evaporate runs downhill by gravity until it reaches the sea, and the whole thing starts again.

The first thing to notice is how small the land's share is. Of all the water on Earth, about 97 per cent is oceanic and saline. Of the remaining 3 per cent that is fresh, roughly 69 per cent is locked in polar ice sheets and mountain glaciers. About 30 per cent of fresh water is groundwater, underground and mostly inaccessible without drilling. What is left — the rivers, the lakes, the surface fresh water you can actually see and touch — is less than one per cent of that 3 per cent. India's rivers carry the substance that sustains more than a billion people, and they are drawing on a resource that is, in global terms, extraordinarily thin.

How that thin resource is distributed across continents depends, above everything, on where mountain ranges sit and which way slopes drain. The great rivers of the world run where the land falls. The Amazon — the planet's largest river by discharge, carrying roughly 20 per cent of all the fresh water that flows into the oceans — runs east because the Andes rise at South America's western edge and tip the entire continent toward the Atlantic. The Nile — the longest river by most measures, a title contested at its margins by the Amazon — runs north for 6,650 kilometres because the East African highlands push it toward the Mediterranean. The Mississippi drains the interior of North America east of the Rockies into the Gulf of Mexico. The Yangtze drains the Tibetan Plateau's eastern face into the Pacific. The Congo, second by discharge, collects the equatorial rain of central Africa in a vast basin and sends it west. Each system is the drainage consequence of a particular continental topology. India's rivers do not make the top tier by discharge or length, but they sustain the densest concentration of agricultural humanity in the world. The Ganga basin alone holds somewhere around 400 million people. The rivers are not larger; the use per drop is more intense — which is a form of inheritance all its own.

Before naming the rivers, it is worth thinking about the shapes they make — about what determines whether a river looks, from above, like a tree or a fishbone or a fan. Rivers follow slope and respond to the rock they cut through. On uniformly tilted land with no systematic fractures in the rock, tributaries join the main channel at all angles, like branches joining a trunk: this is dendritic drainage, the most common pattern, visible across most of the Ganga plains and much of the Indus system. Where the underlying rock is folded — where ridges and valleys alternate in parallel — tributaries are forced to run parallel to the ridges before they find a gap to cross: this is trellis drainage, and you see it in the Himalayan foothills where rivers cut across the fold system. Where a dome or cone rises in the centre, rivers radiate outward from the top like spokes: radial drainage, as around Amarkantak at the summit of the Maikal range, where the Narmada, the Sone, and the Mahanadi all begin and then run in completely different directions. Where rock is heavily jointed at right angles, rivers bend sharply at those joints: rectangular drainage. The pattern tells you about the physics of the land before the river arrived — a reading of deep history from above.

The divides between drainage basins are worth knowing precisely, because they tell you which way every drop of monsoon rain is going to travel. The single most important divide in peninsular India is the Western Ghats. Running almost the full length of India's western coast, the Ghats capture the full force of the southwest monsoon on their seaward face. Almost every drop that falls east of the Western Ghats eventually reaches the Bay of Bengal; almost every drop west reaches the Arabian Sea. The peninsular divide between the Indus basin and the Ganga basin runs along the Aravallis in the northwest — an ancient, heavily eroded range so old in geological terms that the Deccan would feel young by comparison. The Vindhyas and Satpuras, running east-west across central India, hold the Narmada and the Tapi in their rift grabens and direct both rivers west while most of the peninsula drains east. The Himalayan watershed is the master boundary in the north: rivers rising on the southern face run toward India, while those rising on the northern face run toward Central Asia — except for one extraordinary case we will come to shortly.

I want to slow down here on what is perhaps the most counterintuitive fact in this chapter. The three great Himalayan river systems — the Indus, the Ganga, and the Brahmaputra — are antecedent rivers. They predate the Himalayas. This runs against everything the eye expects. You look at the Himalayas — the world's tallest mountains — and naturally assume the rivers are young features, drainage patterns that formed after the mountains did. But the chronology runs the other way. Before the Himalayas rose, large river systems were already draining the margins of the ancient Tethys Sea. When the Indian plate began its collision with the Eurasian plate and the mountains started rising, those rivers kept cutting their channels downward as fast as the land rose around them. The result is the gorges. The Indus cuts through the Karakoram in a gorge at Nanga Parbat that is one of the deepest in the world. The Brahmaputra makes its famous hairpin turn around Namcha Barwa in a gorge deeper than the Grand Canyon. The rivers are not running through mountain gaps; they cut those gorges while the mountains were growing. The mountain is younger than the river. For a surface that has been building the case that geography is inheritance, this is a striking inversion — the oldest inheritance here is the river, not the range.

The Indus system is the westernmost. Its main stem rises near Mansarovar Lake in Tibet and enters India through Ladakh, where marine fossils in the surrounding hills confirm that this was once the floor of the Tethys. The five great tributaries of the Punjab plains — Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej — all join before the Indus reaches the sea in Pakistan. The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, brokered by the World Bank, drew a line across this hydrological family: the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) were allocated to India; the three western ones (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan. It is, in some sense, partition's hydrological translation. Every irrigation canal watering Punjab today has its roots in that 1960 arithmetic.

The Ganga is the longest river in India, 2,525 kilometres from source to sea. It begins as the Bhagirathi, emerging from the Gangotri Glacier in Uttarakhand, is joined by the Alaknanda at Devprayag, and then moves south and east across the plains gathering an enormous family of tributaries. From the left bank — from the Himalayas — come the Ramganga, the Ghaghara (draining much of Nepal), the Gandak, and the Kosi. The Kosi deserves a sentence of its own: it shifts its course dramatically during floods, sometimes by hundreds of kilometres over decades, earning it a reputation as one of Asia's most destructive rivers. From the right bank — from the Peninsular highlands — come the Chambal, the Betwa, the Son, and most importantly the Yamuna. The Yamuna is the longest tributary at 1,376 kilometres, rising from the Yamunotri Glacier and meeting the Ganga at Prayagraj at a confluence sacred for as long as there have been people along these rivers. At the coast, the Ganga splits into distributaries; the western one, the Hooghly, runs through Kolkata, while the rest enters Bangladesh as the Padma-Meghna system. The combined Ganga-Brahmaputra delta is the largest river delta in the world — slowly sinking, enormously fertile, storm-scoured, and covered in the world's largest mangrove forest, the Sundarbans.

The Brahmaputra is the strangest of the three. It rises in Tibet near Mansarovar and runs east for most of its Tibetan length as the Tsangpo, at an altitude above 4,000 metres. Then, at Namcha Barwa, it turns abruptly — a near-180-degree bend — cuts through the Himalayan front in a gorge of extraordinary depth, and emerges in Arunachal Pradesh as the Siang, later picking up the Dibang and the Lohit to become the Brahmaputra in Assam. In Assam it is a braided, wide, sediment-heavy river that floods catastrophically almost every year — it is carrying the erosional product of those gorge-cutting forces and dropping it on a flat valley floor with almost no gradient. Majuli, the world's largest river island, sits in its middle. The Brahmaputra's annual sediment load is so heavy that the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta is still actively growing seaward, even as sea-level rise threatens it from the other direction.

The Himalayan rivers are perennial because they have three sources running simultaneously: monsoon rain from July to September, snowmelt from the Himalayan glaciers in April to June, and groundwater base-flow all year. The combination means flow is continuous even in India's driest months. This is not true of the peninsular rivers, and the difference in livelihood it implies is enormous.

The peninsular river systems are much older than the Himalayan ones and much simpler in their hydrological physics. The Deccan's tilt is eastward, so most of them flow east into the Bay of Bengal. The Mahanadi rises in the Sihawa hills of Chhattisgarh and drains through Odisha, building a delta on its eastern coast; the Hirakud dam controls its floods and was, at its construction, one of the longest earthen dams in the world. The Godavari — called the Dakshin Ganga, the Ganga of the South, and the longest peninsular river — rises at Trimbakeshwar near Nashik and crosses the full width of the Deccan before reaching the Bay of Bengal in Andhra Pradesh, collecting the Pranhita, the Indravati, and the Manjira on the way. The Krishna rises near Mahabaleshwar, is joined by the Bhima and the Tungabhadra, and builds its own delta on the Andhra coast. The Cauvery rises at Talakaveri in the Brahmagiri hills of Coorg and runs through Karnataka and Tamil Nadu to a fertile delta. It has been at the centre of an inter-state water dispute that predates independence, an inheritance of arrangements between the princely state of Mysore and the Madras Presidency, never fully resolved, resurfacing regularly in agitation and court orders — another case of a geographical fact carrying an unfinished political argument inside it.

Two peninsular rivers break the eastward pattern entirely. The Narmada and the Tapi both flow westward into the Arabian Sea, and both do so for the same reason: they run through rift valleys — down-dropped blocks of crust between parallel faults — rather than across the open Deccan surface. The Narmada, rising at Amarkantak, flows westward through a graben between the Vindhyas and the Satpuras for more than 1,300 kilometres before entering the Gulf of Khambhat. Because the rift valley is narrow and the gradient steep, the river has no room to meander and deposit silt — so it builds not a delta but an estuary, a funnel-shaped mouth where tidal water mixes with river water. The Tapi, parallel and south of the Satpuras, does the same. Both are reminders that even the ancient peninsular crust has faulted and rifted — that deep time does not mean absolute stillness.

Peninsular rivers are seasonal because they have none of the Himalayan advantages: no glaciers, no permanent snowfields, relatively smaller catchments, and no continuous groundwater contribution of the scale the Himalayan rivers receive. In summer many of them are nearly dry. This is why the peninsula historically irrigated from tanks — small human-built reservoirs — and from river dams far more than the Himalayan plains did. Water on the peninsula has always had to be stored.

The lakes of India are a compressed catalogue of geological and geomorphic processes. Wular in Kashmir — the largest freshwater lake in India — sits in a tectonic depression in the Jhelum valley. Dal nearby, and Nainital and Bhimtal in the Kumaon hills, are glacial, left behind when Pleistocene ice retreated. Lonar in Maharashtra is unique: a saline crater lake in basalt, formed by a meteorite impact roughly 50,000 years ago — the only known hypervelocity impact crater in basalt on Earth. Cut-off meander loops form oxbow lakes all along the Ganga plains wherever the river has shifted course. In Rajasthan, Sambhar and Didwana are salt lakes, relicts of a more humid past when the Thar received considerably more rain than it does now. Along the coasts, lagoons form where sand bars cut off portions of the sea from river mouths: Chilika in Odisha is the largest brackish-water lagoon in India and one of the largest in Asia — a Ramsar site, winter home to migratory birds from Siberia, commercially central to the fisheries of coastal Odisha. Pulicat straddles the Andhra-Tamil Nadu border. Vembanad in Kerala is the long, narrow backwater system where fishing nets stand in the shallows and where the boundary between land and water feels more negotiated than drawn.

The dams came later — as intentional technology imposed on this natural system. After 1947, the new Indian state needed irrigation to feed a growing population, hydroelectric power to run industries, and flood control to protect the plains. The answer was the multipurpose river valley project: one large structure doing many things at once. Bhakra-Nangal on the Sutlej in Himachal Pradesh, one of the highest concrete gravity dams in the world, feeds the Indira Gandhi Canal and has irrigated Punjab and Haryana for decades. The Damodar Valley Corporation, set up in 1948 and modelled on the Tennessee Valley Authority, was the first Indian river-valley authority — the Damodar had been called the "sorrow of Bengal" for its catastrophic floods, and the DVC was meant to tame it. Sardar Sarovar on the Narmada — completed after decades of legal and political battle, including the Narmada Bachao Andolan led by Medha Patkar and the displacement of hundreds of thousands of tribal people — now feeds Gujarat, Rajasthan, and parts of Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra. Tehri on the Bhagirathi, built in a seismically active zone in the Garhwal Himalaya, is the tallest dam in India; its construction required submerging the old town of Tehri and relocating its inhabitants. Each of these projects carries both a benefit register — power, water, flood protection — and a cost register: displacement, downstream ecology disrupted, river sediment trapped behind walls, questions about dam safety in seismic terrain.

Before the dams, there were older technologies. The johads of Rajasthan — earthen check dams to capture and hold rainwater — were community-built and community-maintained, serving villages through centuries of monsoon variability. The kuls of Spiti and Ladakh were irrigation channels cut from glacial streams to mountain fields, allocated turn-by-turn by village custom. The zings of the cold desert captured snowmelt where no river ran. The stepped wells and vavs of Gujarat maintained groundwater access through the driest months. These systems were not primitive; they were precisely calibrated to their local hydrology. Many of them decayed after independence when state-run canal irrigation seemed to make them unnecessary. Some are being revived now, as groundwater depletion — particularly severe in Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh — makes the old small-water logic look prescient rather than quaint.

India has roughly 18 per cent of the world's population and about 4 per cent of its fresh water resources. Per-capita water availability has been falling steadily. The Ken-Betwa link, connecting the Ken in Madhya Pradesh to the Betwa in Uttar Pradesh, is the first inter-basin transfer project under actual construction — an attempt to move water from a surplus basin to a deficit one. The ecological objections are real: the Panna Tiger Reserve sits in the catchment. The inter-state negotiations for every subsequent link in the proposed national grid are expected to be harder. This is the inheritance the present generation holds — not a shortage of water so much as a misalignment between where the water falls and where the people are, an imbalance no amount of engineering can entirely correct.

The rivers exist because the rain falls. And the rain falls because of a single large atmospheric engine — a seasonal reversal of wind systems over the Indian Ocean that drives moisture over the western coast and releases it across the subcontinent for roughly four months each year. The next chapter is about that engine.
