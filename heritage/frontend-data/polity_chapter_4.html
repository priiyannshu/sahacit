4 — What does the citizen owe the state?

In closing the previous chapter, I noted that the next side of the bargain — what the population owes the state — would be much shorter, much more recent, and considerably more contested than the obligations the state owes the population. The work of this chapter is to make sense of why all three of those things are true.

Begin with a fact that should be more surprising than it is usually treated as. In 1950, when the document came into force, it contained no chapter on the duties of the citizen. The walls were there. The programmes were there. The procedural mechanism by which the walls would be enforced was there. But a chapter telling the citizen what the citizen owes — to the state, to other citizens, to the constitutive idea — was not. The document recorded a great deal about what the apparatus could not do and what the apparatus must work towards, and was almost silent about what the population, in its turn, was supposed to do.

This silence was not, as is sometimes thought, an oversight. The framers had considered the question and decided against it. To have a right, they reasoned, was already to bear the corresponding duty: my right to speak freely imposes on you the duty not to silence me, and on you the corresponding right against me. The obligations the state owes the population, set out in the previous chapter, were reciprocally obligations the citizen had a duty to engage with — to participate in elections, to obey lawful orders, to pay tax. To enumerate duties separately would be, on this view, either redundant — because the duties were already implicit in the structure — or dangerous, because such a list could be used by some future apparatus to extract obligations from citizens that would, over time, come to be traded against their rights. The framers, having watched the previous century carefully, were alert to the trade.

So the silence in 1950 was a considered silence. The chapter on duties was added to the document twenty-six years later, in 1976, by the same amendment that attempted, at the same moment, to place the directives above the rights and to enlarge the powers of the central executive in many other ways. The committee that recommended the addition was constituted under the conditions of the national emergency that had been declared the previous year — the same emergency we will treat properly in a later chapter. Notice the timing. A chapter on what the citizen owes the state was added to the document at precisely the moment when the apparatus had, by its own declaration, suspended a great deal of what the state owed the citizen. It was not added during a season of constitutional confidence. It was added during a season of crisis, by an apparatus that had found the rights-chapter limiting and had decided that the citizen, too, had a part to play in the constitutive bargain that the citizen had not been required to play before.

What does the chapter say? It says, in a single article with a list of clauses, that every citizen shall: abide by the constitution and respect its ideals, its institutions, the flag, the anthem; cherish the ideals of the freedom struggle; uphold the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of the country; defend it when called upon; promote harmony and brotherhood across religions, languages, and regions; renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women; preserve the composite cultural heritage; protect the natural environment, the forests, the rivers, the wildlife, and have compassion for living creatures; develop the scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry; safeguard public property and abjure violence; strive towards excellence in all spheres; and, in the case of those who are parents or guardians, provide opportunities for the education of their children. (The last of these was added later, in the early twenty-first century.)

Read the list slowly. What kind of obligation is being entered here? The duties are, on inspection, a portmanteau — a mix of high abstractions ("scientific temper", "excellence") with concrete civic acts ("safeguard public property", "abjure violence") with state-anchoring loyalties ("sovereignty, unity, integrity") with cultural commitments ("composite heritage", "renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women") with environmental obligations ("protect forests and wildlife"). No coherent theory of citizenship organises the list. The framers of the addition did not, I think, work out what a duty-chapter is for before they wrote it. They wrote it for several purposes at once.

These duties are, like the directives of the previous chapter, non-justiciable. No court will compel any citizen to develop a scientific temper, or to strive towards excellence, or to value the composite culture. There is no procedure by which the apparatus can summon a citizen and require performance of these obligations under penalty of law.

But this should not be mistaken for weightlessness. The duties have, over the decades since they were added, grown a kind of indirect teeth, through subordinate legislation that anchors itself in them. The duty to safeguard public property is enforced through ordinary criminal statutes against vandalism and the destruction of state assets. The duty to protect the environment is anchored in the legislation that protects forests and wildlife and that regulates pollution. The duty to respect the flag and the anthem is enforced through specific legislation against acts of insult to national honour. The duties themselves cannot be sued upon, but the legislation that operationalises them can. So the duties function as a constitutional foothold for laws that do have teeth — which is, in a quiet way, a route by which non-justiciable obligations become substantively operative.

There is, I think, a deeper question here, and it is worth holding open even though the chapter does not fully answer it. What is a duty-chapter doing in a document of consent? The opening chapter of our story argued that the authority of this state derives from the performative act by which the population gave the constitution to itself. On that account, the duties of the citizen are not separately imposed; they are part of what the citizen, by participating in the constitutive bargain, has already agreed to. To list duties separately is either to remind the citizen of what they have already consented to, or — more darkly — to draft a kind of citizen the apparatus needs, and to hold up that drafted figure as the standard against which actual citizens are measured.

There are, it seems to me, two coherent readings of this chapter, and a serious reader must hold both at once.

The first is defensive. Many of the duties protect the conditions on which a constitutional democracy can survive. The duty to promote harmony across religions, languages, and regions is the duty not to corrode the pluralism without which the rights-chapter cannot meaningfully apply. The duty to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women is the constitution speaking against the inherited cruelties of the social body. The duty to develop the scientific temper is the duty not to allow the population to be recaptured by the older epistemic authorities — the priest, the astrologer, the inherited dogma. Read this way, the duties are the constitution constraining the public itself from undoing the conditions that make rights meaningful. They are, on this reading, the herd's duty to itself — its duty not to revert.

The second reading is state-aggrandising. Several of the duties operationalise loyalty to the apparatus — to the constitution as a body of authority, to the institutions, the flag, the anthem, the sovereignty, the unity, the integrity, the defence of the country when called upon. Read this way, the duties are the apparatus drafting the kind of subject it needs in order to be obeyed — a citizen whose patriotism has the shape the apparatus wants it to have, whose dissent is bounded by a prior commitment to the institutions against which dissent might be raised. The shepherd, having consolidated the staff in the earlier chapters, here begins to specify the shape of the flock.

Both readings are correct. The duties chapter is doing both at once, and the work of constitutional citizenship — the work this story is, in part, asking you to do — is to know which reading is in play in any given invocation. When a citizen is ordered, by a local officer, to display reverence for the national symbols on pain of harassment, which reading is being mobilised? When the apparatus appeals to the duty of brotherhood to deflect attention from a failure of its own rights-protecting functions, which reading is being mobilised? The chapter does not answer this question. It leaves the answer to the political life of the country.

That is, I think, the most honest thing one can say about this chapter. It is the document's smallest chapter. It is its latest chapter. It was added in a season of crisis, by an apparatus that had reasons of its own to want it added, and it has since been used by every kind of speaker for every kind of purpose. It is invoked more often, in everyday public discourse, than the rights-chapter that long preceded it; and it is, also, the chapter that has had the least to say about what citizenship in this republic actually is.

The next chapter leaves the citizen behind, for a while, and turns to the apparatus itself — how it is organised, how its parts are arranged, how the staff is distributed across the limbs that hold it. The duties drawn here become, in that next chapter, the citizen's relationship to a particular set of institutions; and those institutions, between them, do most of the day-to-day work of governing the herd whose consent the document continues to claim.
