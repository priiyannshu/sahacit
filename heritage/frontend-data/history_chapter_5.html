5 — The Vernacular and the Dome: Three Centuries of the Delhi Sultanate

Consider Kabir. He is a weaver of Banaras, born in the fifteenth century into a Muslim julaha household, raised on Sufi devotional practice, trained in the bhakti lineage of Ramananda — a north Indian saint who taught across all four varnas. Kabir's verses address a formless God approached through no ritual that either Islam or Hinduism would recognise. He denounces pilgrimage, idol-worship, namaz, the Vedas, and the Quran with equal contempt, and he does it in the spoken dialect of the streets — not Sanskrit, not Persian, but the rough grain of vernacular Banaras. His followers, the Kabir Panthis, will argue for centuries over whether he was Hindu or Muslim. Both claim him; neither, by his own testimony, quite contains him.

I want you to begin with Kabir because this era — the Delhi Sultanate, roughly 1192 to 1526 — is the era that produces the conditions in which a Kabir becomes possible. It takes three hundred years of a foreign-origin warrior state, Sufi saints arriving from Central Asia and making their homes in Indian cities, Hindu devotional movements breaking free of Sanskrit and temple mediation, and the slow accumulation of a shared urban life — for this weaver's voice, in this language, to be heard at all.

Two images will hold the era together. The first is the two centres — Delhi in the north and Vijayanagara in the south, simultaneously the most powerful states in the subcontinent, mutually aware, rarely in direct collision. The second is the vernacular — the moment when devotion steps out of the languages of the elite and into the spoken tongues of ordinary people. The vernacular is this era's quiet revolution. And between them, a third image: the court and the dargah, political power and spiritual authority operating in the same cities but answering to entirely different rules — and the dargah outlasting the court.

The arc from conquest to collapse

The Sultanate begins with a defeat. In 1192, at the Second Battle of Tarain, Muhammad of Ghur overcomes Prithviraj Chauhan of Delhi and Ajmer and places a Central Asian Turkish ruler in control of the Gangetic heartland for the first time. Unlike his predecessor Mahmud of Ghazni, who raided and withdrew, Muhammad Ghori stays. When he is assassinated in 1206 he leaves behind a lieutenant, Qutbuddin Aibak, who makes the founding decision: he severs the new Indian state from its Central Asian origins, rules from Lahore then Delhi as a sovereign in his own right, and begins the Qutb Minar — the era's first self-portrait, its ground storey his, its upper storeys his successor's, its top added after a lightning strike by a Tughlaq sultan more than a century later. Continuity and reinvention in a single tower.

Iltutmish (1210–36) is the real consolidator: he earns Abbasid recognition, suppresses rival Turkish chiefs, and does the one extraordinary thing that defines the early Sultanate — he designates his daughter Raziyya as his successor. Raziyya Sultana rules 1236–40, acknowledged even by the court chronicler Minhaj-i-Siraj as more capable than her brothers. She rides elephants without a veil, holds open court, resists the Turkish nobility's attempts to make her a figurehead. She is deposed in 1240, but the fact that she ruled at all tells you that Turkish steppe customs — in which women exercised genuine authority — had not yet been fully displaced by the Persian courtly norms that will harden under Balban. Balban (1266–87) fixes that problem: he introduces prostration before the sultan, makes the monarch's person the source of all authority, and systematically dismantles the chahalgani, the Group of Forty Turkish nobles who had acted as kingmakers. The tone is set — formal, hierarchical, Persian.

The Khalji dynasty that replaces the Turkish line in 1290 marks the first great breach: Jalaluddin Khalji is not Turkish but Afghan, and his accession makes ethnic origin irrelevant to the throne. His nephew Alauddin (1296–1316) murders him for the throne and becomes the most capable administrator the Sultanate produces. He faces, at once, Mongols who will besiege Delhi itself in 1303 and the fiscal problem of maintaining the army to repel them. His solution is audacious: he abolishes iqtas in the doab, taxes grain directly at fifty percent of produce, fixes commodity prices in Delhi through three dedicated markets policed by spies, and brands cavalry horses (dagh) to prevent fraud at muster. Barani, writing a generation later, calls the price stability "a wonder of the age." The Mongols are repelled. Then Alauddin sends his slave-general Malik Kafur south — through Warangal in 1309, through the Hoysala country and to Madurai in 1310 — further south than any Delhi army had yet penetrated. The expedition finances the northern army; its precedent haunts the subcontinent for centuries.

After Alauddin comes the era's most brilliant and most catastrophic figure. Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1324–51) is the Sultanate's intellectual peak and its structural crisis: he reads philosophy, converses with Muslim mystics and Hindu yogis and Jain saints, gives offices on merit. He is also the century's most spectacular failure. He orders the officer class of Delhi to relocate sixteen hundred kilometres south to Daulatabad in 1327 to better govern both halves of the subcontinent — a logistically coherent idea, politically catastrophic. He experiments with a bronze token currency backed by state authority, like Qublai Khan's China — it is forged into worthlessness and withdrawn. He raises taxes in the doab at the wrong moment, triggering famine and rebellion. The two most consequential rebellions produce states we will come to: Vijayanagara and the Bahmani kingdom. Ibn Battuta, arriving in Delhi in 1333 and appointed its chief qazi, records in his Rihla a city of extraordinary grandeur alongside a ruler combining genius and obstinacy in impossible measure.

Firuz Shah Tughlaq (1351–88) inherits the chaos and manages a different kind of failure. He stabilises by conceding: he makes iqtas hereditary, restoring to the nobles the local power Alauddin had stripped. He digs canals — the longest, from the Sutlej to Hansi, two hundred kilometres — and builds towns, including the new city of Jaunpur, named after himself. But hereditary iqtas begin the slow undoing of central authority that will not be reversed. The storm arrives from the northwest in 1398. Timur of Samarqand sacks Delhi and departs with every artisan and mason he can find for his building projects at Samarqand. The Sayyid dynasty that rules from 1414 is, as contemporaries noted, a sultan whose writ ran "from Delhi to Palam." The Lodi dynasty — first non-Turkish rulers, Afghan in origin — attempts a revival. Bahlul Lodi (1451–89) fills Delhi with Afghan nobles and annexes the Sharqi kingdom of Jaunpur in 1484. Sikandar Lodi (1489–1517) founds Agra in 1506, introduces a standardised yard of measurement, and patronises Persian learning while re-imposing jizyah and demolishing temples. His son Ibrahim Lodi meets Babur at Panipat in 1526. Babur's artillery destroys the war elephants; the Lodi dynasty ends in an afternoon.

The two centres: Hampi and the Deccan

While Delhi moves through this turbulent arc, the south is writing a different story. In 1336, two brothers — Harihara and Bukka, former officers of the Kampili kingdom of Karnataka — establish a new state at Hampi, on the south bank of the Tungabhadra, at the site of the goddess Pampadevi's sacred ground. The founding story involves a sage named Vidyaranya and a dramatic reconversion from Islam; historians are divided on the conversion narrative. What is not in dispute is what it produces: the Vijayanagara empire, the most powerful Hindu kingdom in the subcontinent for two centuries.

Hampi is unlike anything being built elsewhere in India at this moment. The city is ringed by seven concentric lines of fortification — the outermost enclosing agricultural land so the city can feed itself under siege. The royal centre holds the Mahanavami Dibba, a platform of eleven thousand square feet rising forty feet, its base covered in relief carvings of processions and battles, from which the king presides over the Navaratri celebrations that are the empire's annual act of self-display. The Virupaksha temple in the sacred centre keeps its worship continuous — priests are still there, still serving, when the British surveyor Colin Mackenzie arrives in 1800 and learns about the ruins from them.

Krishnadeva Raya (1509–29) is the empire's peak: Tuluva dynasty, victorious over Bijapur, Orissa, and the Deccan sultanates in sequence, patron of Telugu letters (he composes Amuktamalyada himself). He maintains Muslim cavalry by granting them jagirs — a direct borrowing from the Sultanate administrative model. The Amara-Nayaka system, the empire's administrative spine, assigns territories to military commanders who collect taxes, maintain troops, and send tribute annually to the king. It is the iqta system adapted to peninsular geography — and like the iqta, it will eventually empower the periphery against the centre.

The Bahmani kingdom, Vijayanagara's great rival, is itself a product of Muhammad bin Tughlaq's overreach: Hasan Gangu rebels in 1347 at Gulbarga and founds a Deccan sultanate that will contest the Tungabhadra doab and the Krishna-Godavari delta for a century. The Bahmani's most remarkable moment is under its wazir Mahmud Gawan — an Iranian trader who reformed the provinces into eight tarafs with salaried officers, built a three-storey madrasa at Bidar in 1472 (free food and lodging for a thousand students and teachers), and extended the western coast from Dabhol to Goa. Executed in 1481 by factional rivals, he is the hinge on which the Bahmani fractures: five successor sultanates — Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Berar, Bidar — emerge by 1518.

The irony the era savours: the same Deccan sultanates that had been Vijayanagara's enemies become the instrument of its end. Rama Raja, the real power at Vijayanagara by the 1540s, plays the five against each other so effectively that he drives them to combine. At Bannihatti (Talikota) in 1565, the combined armies rout the Vijayanagara forces; Rama Raja is beheaded on the field. The city is sacked for months. When it is abandoned, the two centres that held the subcontinent in tension for two centuries reduce to one.

The provinces that outlived the centre

Timur's 1398 raid is not merely a military catastrophe — it is the moment when the political idea of a single north Indian sultanate is definitively broken. Five regional powers fill the space, and each develops a sophistication that the contracted Sultanate could not have produced.

Bengal, independent from Delhi since 1338, produces under the Ilyas Shahi and Hussain Shahi dynasties a culture of extraordinary depth. The Bengal architectural school develops the Bangla curved roof — borrowed from the thatched temples of the old Pala era — and introduces it into mosque and tomb construction. The Adina Mosque at Pandua (1375) is for a generation the largest mosque in the subcontinent. The zenith is Alauddin Hussain Shah (1493–1519): his highest officials are Hindu, his court patronises Bengali literature, and his respect for Chaitanya Mahaprabhu is famous. Kashmir under Zainul Abidin — "Bud Shah," the Great Sultan (1420–70) — inverts everything his predecessor Sikandar Butshikan had done: he cancels jizyah, rebuilds temples, brings paper-making craftsmen from Samarqand, has the Rajatarangini translated into Persian, and fosters the Rishi Sufi order that blends Hindu and Muslim devotional practice in a distinctly Kashmiri synthesis.

Gujarat under Mahmud Begarha (1459–1511) is fabulously rich from its ports — Cambay, Surat — and sufficiently strong to ally with Egypt against Portuguese naval pressure. Jaunpur, the "Shiraz of the East" under the Sharqi sultans, produces the distinctive Sharqi architectural style (towering propylon facades, no minarets) and a culture of musical refinement under which Malik Muhammad Jaisi writes Padmavat, the great Awadhi Sufi romance. Mewar under Rana Kumbha (1433–68) and Rana Sanga (1508–28) represents Rajput resistance at its most intellectually assertive: Kumbha builds the Kirti Stambha at Chittor to commemorate defeating Malwa, and Sanga — one-eyed, one-armed, scarred from seventeen wounds — is powerful enough that Babur regards him, not Ibrahim Lodi, as the real obstacle to Mughal rule in the north.

The era's political fragmentation is, paradoxically, its cultural richness. Each province carries a different inflection of the great synthesis unfolding simultaneously from Banaras to Hampi.

The court and the dargah

In every city the Sultanate builds or controls, two kinds of authority operate side by side. The court conducts power through taxation, military hierarchy, and Persian bureaucracy. The dargah — the shrine and hospice of the Sufi saint — operates entirely outside the sultan's gift, open to all comers regardless of caste or creed. The dargah outlasts the court. This is not accidental.

The Chishti silsila is the era's most consequential Sufi lineage. It arrives with Muinuddin Chishti settling at Ajmer in 1192 — a perfect simultaneity with Prithviraj's defeat that the tradition will make into a founding symbol. His disciple Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki establishes the lineage at Delhi; Baba Farid extends it to Ajodhan, composing in Punjabi (his verses will eventually enter the Sikh Adi Granth). Above all there is Nizamuddin Auliya (1238–1325), contemporary of seven sultans, who refuses to meet the last of them, uses local Hindawi dialect with ordinary people, and teaches that the basis of the relationship with God is love — not fear, not legal compliance, not birth. Amir Khusrau is his chief devotee and musical companion; they are buried in the same courtyard, as close in death as in life. The contrast with the Suhrawardis — the other major Sufi order, active in Punjab and Sindh, who accepted state patronage and court appointments — defines the Chishti choice: spiritual authority maintained by refusing, not seeking, the court's favour.

What meets the Sufi tradition from the other direction is the bhakti movement. Ramananda, active in Banaras around 1400, opens the Ramanuja devotional tradition across all four varnas: his disciples include a cobbler (Ravidas), a barber, a cultivator, and a weaver — Kabir. Kabir is the era's deepest synthesis: raised Muslim, trained in Ramananda's lineage, drawing from Nath yogis and Sufi poets alike, composing in the vernacular. His sakhis (couplets) are included in both the Kabir Panthi Bijak and the Sikh Adi Granth. Guru Nanak (1469–1539) begins at the same theological point — nirguna devotion, rejection of caste and gender distinctions — and builds something distinct: a centre at Kartarpur with the langar (common kitchen) as the social embodiment of his theology. His hymns in Punjabi, sung to the rabab, are the start of what will become Sikhism, though Nanak intended no new religion.

At the opposite pole from the nirguna saints is Chaitanya Mahaprabhu of Bengal (1486–1534), all form and devotional ecstasy: his kirtan — congregational worship through music — makes the name of Krishna itself the vehicle of union. The Maharashtra saints at Pandharpur — Jnaneshwar, Namdev, and the Varkari tradition composing in Marathi — reach across the same broad movement from yet another direction. What unites them all is this: they are composing in the languages people actually speak. The vernacular is not a side-effect of this era; it is its primary achievement.

The synthesis in stone, word, and coin

Amir Khusrau (1252–1325) is the cultural harvest of the encounter made audible. Born in UP of a Turkish father and an Indian mother, serving seven sultans, buried in Nizamuddin's courtyard, he calls himself the tuti-i-hind — the Parrot of India — and insists, in his historical mathnavis, that India is not a place of exile:

Hindustan is like heaven on earth... Brahmans here are as learned as Aristotle.

He writes in the sabaq-i-hindi style that blends Persianate form with Indian sensibility; he is credited with introducing new musical ragas into the Hindawi devotional tradition. His Persian-Hindavi synthesis does not have a name yet — but Hindavi, the spoken north Indian vernacular he uses alongside Persian, will eventually become Urdu and then modern Hindi.

The architectural synthesis has buildings. The era moves from the trabeate (pillar-and-beam) style of Hindu tradition to the arcuate (arch-and-dome) of Central Asian Islamic construction, and the transition generates new forms at every stage. The Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque at Delhi is built using the pillars of Hindu and Jain temples — carvings still visible — and the result is neither purely Hindu nor purely Islamic: it is a new thing. The Alai Darwaza (1311) is the first building in India with a correctly constructed true dome. The Tughlaqabad complex introduces massive sloped walls and the high-platform tomb. The Lodi tombs invent the double dome — a hollow inner dome beneath the outer — an innovation that passes directly into the Mughal canon and culminates, eventually, in the Taj Mahal.

The administrative synthesis is the iqta system, running through all five dynasties: Balban tightens it, Alauddin abolishes it in the doab for direct taxation, Firuz Tughlaq makes it hereditary. When the Mughals arrive, they rename it jagir and build the entire mansabdari system around it. The Sultanate solves neither the fiscal problem nor the loyalty problem; it passes both intact to its successor.

What the era leaves us

The Delhi Sultanate's end is not a collapse so much as a transformation. What Babur defeats at Panipat in 1526 is a Lodi state already exhausted by internal rebellion and Rajput pressure. What he inherits is not empty — it is the institutional, cultural, and architectural residue of three centuries of the most complex experiment in statecraft the subcontinent had seen.

The iqta becomes the jagir. Persian remains the court language. The Chishti silsilas, dispersed from Delhi after Nasiruddin Chiragh-i-Dehli's death, continue their work across eastern and southern India regardless of who sits on the throne. Khusrau's Persian-Hindavi synthesis will flower into the court poetry and music of Akbar's era. The bhakti voices — Kabir, Nanak, Chaitanya — will be the devotional air the Mughal Empire breathes whether or not its emperors notice. The Deccan will not be fully absorbed for another century and a half: the five Bahmani successors and the rump Vijayanagara require Aurangzeb's 1680s campaigns. Rana Sanga's Mewar, defeated by Babur at Khanwa in 1527, resists Mughal pressure for decades more.

What the era leaves, above all, is the shape of a question. How does a state of foreign origin govern a vast diverse population whose devotional, linguistic, and architectural traditions are not those of the ruler? The Sultanate's answer varies dynasty by dynasty, sultan by sultan — from Alauddin's controlled efficiency to Firuz's conservative patronage to Muhammad Tughlaq's restless experiment. But the fact that a Kabir can emerge from this world, and that his verses pass into both the Hindu and Sikh canonical collections before either community fully claims him, suggests that the answer is not only conquest and taxation. There is something else happening in the spaces between the court and the dargah, between the Persian chronicle and the Punjabi hymn, between the pillars of a dismantled temple and the dome rising over them — the vernacular pressing its way through every crack.

While all this was unfolding in the subcontinent, the world outside was in its own upheaval. The Black Death of the 1340s kills a third of Europe's population, empowering the peasants who survive and beginning the dissolution of medieval feudalism. The Renaissance that follows — Italian city-states, Gutenberg's printing press (c.1440), humanist scholarship, Leonardo and Michelangelo — is a civilisational transformation running in parallel to the Sultanate's own cultural synthesis. The Age of Exploration brings Portuguese ships to the Kerala coast in 1498 (Vasco da Gama), and Columbus to the Americas in 1492, inaugurating the Columbian Exchange and the Atlantic slave trade. Luther nails his theses in 1517, the year Sikandar Lodi dies and Ibrahim Lodi inherits a kingdom already crumbling. The world the Mughals will inherit is one in which European maritime power has begun to circle the globe — a fact the Delhi Sultanate never needed to reckon with, but one whose weight will fall, eventually, on everything it built.

When you open the timeline, you will see the era arranged as cards across its three centuries: dynasties, saints, monuments, battles, poets. They are the individual stones. The dome they hold up together is this.
