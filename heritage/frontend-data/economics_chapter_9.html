9 — What is the country doing with its land?

Begin with a number that doesn't announce itself loudly enough. Roughly 45 per cent of India's workforce earns a living from agriculture. Agriculture contributes roughly 17 per cent of the country's GDP. I want you to hold the gap between those two figures — not as a statistic but as a structural fact. Half the workforce produces less than a sixth of the output. The other half, in industry and services, produces the remaining five-sixths. Every other chapter in this surface is a chapter about an economy that takes this imbalance as its prior condition. The people with the thinnest safety nets, the most contingent livelihoods, the least access to formal credit and insurance, are disproportionately the people whose lives turn on the land. And the landholdings are small: over 86 per cent of India's operational holdings are smaller than two hectares. A third of farming families are effectively landless, working as agricultural labourers on others' land. This is not a problem to solve inside a single chapter. It is the constraint around which everything else arranges itself.

I want to start with the deep substrate, because agriculture is the sector in this economy where colonial history has not yet exhausted its consequences. The three land revenue systems that the British consolidated across the subcontinent were not merely administrative arrangements. They were the legal architecture of extraction. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 — imposed on Bengal, Bihar, and parts of Orissa — created a class of zamindars as hereditary and saleable landlords, assessed a fixed annual revenue to the colonial government, and left the actual cultivators as tenants with no legal claim to the land they worked. Whatever the zamindar extracted above his fixed tax was his to keep; the cultivator could be evicted at pleasure. The Ryotwari settlement, dominant in Madras and Bombay, dispensed with the intermediary and assessed revenue directly on individual cultivators — each assessed and taxed in annual instalments calibrated to the supposed yield of each plot. The Mahalwari settlement across the north and north-west assessed the village collectively, distributing the tax burden among the landed families of the community. Three arrangements, all orientated toward extracting revenue upward; none designed with the cultivator's security in mind. These systems reappear in the taxation chapter — the colonial land revenue was, for a long time, the primary source of government income — and they form the substrate that independent India's agrarian legislation had to reckon with. The inheritance was not just a broken economy but a legal structure of land relations built to serve extraction, which the new republic now had to convert into a structure that served production.

The post-1947 land reform programme had three pillars, and their fates were not equal. Zamindari abolition — the removal of the extractive intermediary — was the most successful. By the late 1950s, across most states, the zamindari systems had been legally wound up. Compensation was paid; the old intermediary class lost its revenue-collection rights. This was the inheritance India chose to break: the colonial layer closest to the surface was peeled back. But abolition of the zamindar did not, in most places, transfer ownership to the cultivators. The land reverted to the state, and many large landowners simply reclassified themselves as cultivators under the new law. Tenancy reform tried to extend the benefit downward — security of tenure, regulated rents, eventual ownership rights for long-standing tenants. The result was partial. Landlords evicted tenants before the notification date, registered their own cultivation, used the law's own procedures to protect themselves. By 1992, tenancy reforms had been realised on only about 4 per cent of the country's operated area — covering eleven million tenant-cultivators, real but far short of the ambition. Ceiling laws — the upper limit on individual landholding, with the surplus redistributed to the landless — came closest to failing entirely. The ceilings were set, but evasion was systematic: benami transfers in relatives' names, family partitions that fragmented holdings below the ceiling, political accommodation by state governments unwilling to move against their own landed support bases. West Bengal, Kerala, and parts of Andhra Pradesh were the exceptions that proved the rule. Land consolidation — merging scattered small plots into contiguous holdings — worked in Punjab and Haryana, and barely functioned elsewhere. Taken together, the land reform programme touched roughly 6 per cent of India's operated area. The small farm structure of Indian agriculture today is the lasting mark of ceiling laws that did not achieve redistribution.

I think it was the failure of the land reform route that made the government susceptible to an alternative. If redistribution could not increase agricultural production, perhaps productivity technology could. The state's third objective for land reform — increasing agricultural output — had received the least political attention precisely because it competed with the redistributive one. After the ceiling laws stalled, it became the only objective still available.

The Green Revolution arrived in India against a specific crisis, not as a planned programme. The monsoons of 1965 and 1966 failed in succession; Bihar faced conditions not far from famine. India's food situation was managed through PL-480, an American wheat shipment arrangement that gave Lyndon Johnson's administration real leverage over Indian foreign policy, and which the government in Delhi found genuinely humiliating — Johnson held shipments as leverage over India's position on Vietnam. Norman Borlaug, an American agronomist working on a Rockefeller Foundation programme in Mexico, had by the early 1960s developed dwarf varieties of wheat that redirected the plant's nutrient energy toward the grain rather than the stem, increasing yield per plant by more than 200 per cent. M. S. Swaminathan drove the initiative to bring Borlaug's seeds to India, ran field trials in Punjab from 1965, and pushed full adoption against considerable resistance. The connection is worth naming plainly: Borlaug's research was funded by the Rockefeller and Ford Foundations, institutions whose support for agricultural productivity work was part of a broader Cold War strategy to demonstrate that capitalist development could solve food insecurity without land redistribution. India accepted that inheritance because the domestic alternative had already failed, and because the urgency of escaping PL-480 dependency was real.

The package that made the Green Revolution work was not the seeds alone — it never works as seeds alone. High-Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds required reliable irrigation, chemical fertilisers at concentrations traditional compost could not supply, pesticides (the new varieties were not acclimatised to local pests), and a price guarantee at harvest. Without that guarantee, no farmer would invest in the costlier inputs the new seeds required. The Minimum Support Price (MSP) announced for wheat from 1966–67 was that guarantee: a floor price declared before the sowing season, with the government committing to purchase the crop through the Food Corporation of India (FCI). The MSP converted the harvest price from a variable into a certainty. Wheat yields in Punjab jumped from roughly 1,300 kilograms per hectare to over 3,200. Within a decade, India had ended its dependence on PL-480 shipments and was running grain surpluses. The stock of human beings who might otherwise have starved in the 1970s and 1980s did not starve. That is not a small thing to say.

What the Green Revolution did not do is as important as what it did. It was a wheat-and-rice revolution; other crops were not part of the package. It ran in Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh — regions that already had irrigation infrastructure, credit systems, and the administrative capacity to support the full package. It did not reach pulses, oilseeds, or coarse cereals. It did not reach eastern India, where rain-fed agriculture and smaller holdings did not suit the requirements. The regional concentration mattered: the inter-personal and inter-regional inequalities it generated were visible within years, as farmers in Punjab accumulated surpluses while the rest of Indian agriculture remained largely unchanged. The ecological costs compounded over decades: water table depletion from intensive tubewell irrigation — five tonnes of water are consumed per kilogram of rice produced — soil degradation from sustained chemical fertiliser use, and the loss of balanced cropping patterns as wheat and rice displaced pulses and oilseeds across the Green Revolution belt. The narrow crop base created its own vulnerability, which is now being compounded by climate variability the original package never had to price in.

The MSP-procurement system created by the Green Revolution is the clearest example in this surface of an emergency measure that became permanent infrastructure. Today, MSPs are announced for 23 crops — seven cereals, five pulses, eight oilseeds, and a few others. But actual large-scale government procurement — the physical act of buying grain at the announced price and storing it — happens overwhelmingly for two: wheat and rice. For the rest, the MSP is a price signal, not an operational guarantee. In Punjab and Haryana, wheat and rice farmers have built their entire production systems around the certainty of FCI procurement over fifty years. That certainty has accumulated into something that is no longer merely an economic expectation. It is a political compact, and it behaves like one.

This is the context in which the events of 2020–21 must be read. The three farm laws passed in September 2020 attempted, among other things, to dismantle the legal scaffolding that made public procurement the default arrangement for agricultural marketing — opening private markets, enabling direct contracts with corporations, removing trade restrictions between states. The stated logic was not unreasonable: concentrated procurement in a few states for two crops had distorted cropping patterns, depleted water tables in Punjab, and left the majority of India's farmers — in states without MSP procurement infrastructure — without comparable price guarantees. The protests that followed were sustained, large, and centred in Punjab and Haryana. The farmers who came to Delhi in the winter of 2020 were not arguing against agricultural reform in principle. They were defending a specific inheritance that had become a livelihood, and demonstrating that the inheritance could not be restructured without consultation. The laws were repealed in November 2021. The structural problem they were trying to address has not been.

The other major inheritance the Green Revolution left behind was the need to distribute surplus grain to populations that could not afford to buy it. This is a different problem — and here the inheritance goes back not to 1966 but to 1943. The Bengal Famine of that year killed roughly thirty lakh people. The food was not absent from Bengal; Amartya Sen showed, in his 1981 study of famines, that what killed them was not a shortage of grain in the aggregate but a collapse in the purchasing power of specific groups — rural labourers, artisans, transport workers — who could not afford grain at prices the war economy had driven up. The entitlement failure, not the availability failure, was what produced the famine.

Starvation is the characteristic of some people not having enough food to eat. It is not the characteristic of there not being enough food to eat. While the latter can be a cause of the former, it is but one of many possible causes.

This is the opening of Sen's Poverty and Famines, and it reframed the entire question. Availability of food in the aggregate is not the same as access to food for specific people at specific moments. The Public Distribution System (PDS) that evolved from the wartime rationing introduced in Bengal after 1943 was, in this light, not a grain management programme but an entitlement guarantee — a mechanism for extending purchasing power to those who lacked it. The network of roughly 5.5 lakh fair price shops, selling grain at subsidised issue prices to cardholders, is that guarantee made physical.

The system evolved through three phases. The universal PDS — available to all cardholders — gave way in 1992 to a revamped version that directed additional allocations to high-poverty districts. In 1997, the Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS) introduced differential pricing: below-poverty-line households received grain at distinctly lower prices, while above-poverty-line households paid rates closer to the market. The Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY), launched in December 2000, went further — directing 35 kilograms of grain per month to the poorest households at Rs 2 per kilogram for wheat and Rs 3 for rice. The National Food Security Act (NFSA) 2013 converted the entire architecture into a legal right: covering roughly 67 per cent of the population, each person entitled to 5 kilograms per month at subsidised rates. It is the largest food security programme in the world, and it is — to connect the thread back to Chapter 1 — Sen's capability approach rendered as statute. The conditions for the real freedom to live differently, written into law.

The PDS has improved substantially since the early 2000s. Leakage estimates, once in the range of 30–50 per cent by some measures, have fallen as digital reforms reduced the scope for diversion: Aadhaar-linked authentication at fair price shops, point-of-sale machines recording each transaction, the One Nation One Ration Card scheme allowing migrant workers to draw their entitlement from any shop in any state. The persistent policy question — whether in-kind grain distribution remains the better instrument, or whether direct cash transfer achieves the same nutritional outcome more efficiently — has not been settled, though the direction of reform across welfare programmes has generally leaned toward cash.

The questions agriculture leaves open are harder than the ones the Green Revolution answered. How to extend real price support to pulses and oilseeds — the crops the MSP-procurement system largely does not reach? How to make small-scale farming viable without permanent subsidy? Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs), aggregating small farmers into collective buying and selling entities, are the current cooperative experiment. e-NAM, the electronic National Agriculture Market launched in 2016, offers farmers a trading platform where they can access bids from multiple buyers without physical presence at the mandi. The current-affairs layer — the ongoing political aftermath of the farm law withdrawal, the operational state of e-NAM, the FPO sector's uneven performance, climate-resilience and natural farming programmes, the question of whether MSP will be legally guaranteed — belongs there and not here. This chapter has set the structural ground.

The deeper question this chapter cannot fully answer is why India's agricultural labour has not moved out of farming the way it moved in every prior sustained episode of Asian development. In Taiwan, South Korea, and China, labour-intensive manufacturing absorbed farm workers over decades, raising productivity in both sectors simultaneously. India's manufacturing sector did not build that absorption capacity — and the reasons belong to Chapter 10. Informal services absorbed some of the labour force, but at low productivity and without the wage gains that factory employment historically delivered. The missing decades of labour-intensive manufacturing have left half the workforce attached to agriculture at holdings too small to generate adequate income, in a sector whose productivity gains have been largely exhausted since the Green Revolution's initial impact was absorbed.

Agriculture is where India most visibly inherits every policy choice it has ever made. The zamindari is gone but the small farm structure the ceiling laws failed to redistribute remains. The Green Revolution ended import dependence but concentrated water depletion and monoculture in specific states. The MSP saved Punjab's farmers and made them politically immovable. The NFSA made food a right for two-thirds of the country. None of these is complete; all of them are still in motion. The posture to carry into the next chapter is specific: the country's inability to move its agricultural labour into productive manufacturing employment is not an accident of neglect. It is a consequence of choices made in other domains — choices the industry and services chapter will have to account for.
