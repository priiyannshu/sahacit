9 — Where humans cluster and why

There are, at the time of writing, roughly eight billion of us. That number has a distribution problem. Spread those eight billion evenly across the planet's land surface and you would get about 56 people per square kilometre — a medium-density town, everywhere, from the Sahara to the Amazon to the Tibetan Plateau. That is not what happened. What actually happened is that about 90 percent of humanity has compressed itself into roughly 30 percent of the land, while vast continental interiors remain nearly empty. The question this chapter is organising itself around — why people cluster where they do — turns out to have the same answer as many of the earlier chapters in this surface. People cluster where the substrate permits them to. They avoid the substrate that would kill or starve them. Population geography is, at its root, a record of what the planet made livable.

Three clusters account for most of that compression. South Asia — the subcontinent, Bangladesh, and Pakistan — is the densest of the three, sitting around the Indo-Gangetic plain and the river deltas of the Bay of Bengal. East Asia — China, Japan, and the Koreas — is the largest by number, with China's eastern plains and Japan's narrow coastal strips carrying populations that their physical area barely seems able to justify. Western Europe is the third, thinner and more evenly spread, the product of several centuries of industrial development and agricultural intensification. Smaller dense pockets appear in Northeast USA, along the Nile valley (where the world's most famous case of a river-versus-desert is still ongoing), and in parts of Southeast Asia. Beyond these zones: the Arctic, the Sahara, the Australian outback, the interior of Amazonia, the high Tibetan Plateau — almost empty. The contrast is not subtle. Some of these empty zones are enormous; the Sahara alone is larger than the continental USA.

What explains the clustering? The factors are the same ones that have come up repeatedly in this surface, which is not an accident — they are the same substrate conditions. Physical factors first. Flat, fertile terrain at low altitude is preferred; mountain slopes and high plateaus are not, because farming is hard, transport is hard, and the cold is unforgiving. This is why the Indo-Gangetic plain — the great alluvial trough we spent time on in chapter three — carries the density it does. Water availability shapes distribution directly: river valleys and coastlines attract settlement, while interiors without reliable water systems do not. Climate matters: the temperate and monsoonal belts support more people per square kilometre than the hyperarid or the polar. Fertile soils — recall the alluvial plains and the Deccan black soil from chapter seven — concentrate the agriculture that feeds density. Economic factors compound the physical ones. Where minerals concentrate industry, people follow: the Chotanagpur plateau, for instance, has density far higher than its difficult terrain would suggest, because coal and iron pulled mining towns into its hills. Transport corridors thicken population by reducing isolation and connecting markets. Social and political factors matter too: stable governance, religious centres, and kinship networks can sustain dense settlement even in marginal terrain, while conflict evacuates areas that the physical factors alone would have kept populated.

Now, how populations grow matters as much as where they are. Demographers describe the pattern of change in a concept called demographic transition — the observation that populations move, broadly, through a sequence of phases as societies industrialise. In the first stage, birth rates are high and death rates are high, so population stays roughly stable at a low level; this is the pre-industrial agrarian condition, everywhere, before the nineteenth century. In the second stage, death rates begin to fall — because vaccines work, because clean water arrives, because the harvest becomes more reliable — while birth rates remain high from cultural habit; this produces explosive growth. Sub-Saharan Africa has spent much of the late twentieth century in this stage. In the third stage, birth rates begin to fall too, as more women receive education, as child mortality drops (reducing the perceived need for many children), and as urban living raises the cost of large families; growth continues but slows. Most of Asia has been in this stage in the last two generations. In the fourth stage, both rates are low and stable; most of Europe and Japan are here, with population essentially flat. A fifth stage is now observed in several countries — births have fallen below the replacement level of roughly 2.1 per woman, and the population is actually shrinking; Japan, Germany, South Korea, and parts of Southern Europe are here. The trajectory is not a law — it can be interrupted, delayed, or bypassed — but it has proved a useful organising description of what happened in most industrialised societies.

India is in late Stage 3, pressing against Stage 4. Its growth rate, which was above two percent annually in the 1970s, has declined to roughly one percent now. The year 1921 is the demographic divide in India's official reckoning — before it, population was essentially stagnant; after it, steady growth began. The pattern has four phases: stagnant before 1921, steady between 1921 and 1951, explosive between 1951 and 1981 (vaccines, the Green Revolution, famine prevention all working at once), and declining rate since 1981. The demographic dividend — the window during which the working-age share of the population is at its peak relative to the young and old — is currently open for India and is estimated to remain open until roughly 2055. Whether that dividend is converted into actual economic development depends on whether the schools and the skills and the jobs follow the demography. The demography alone guarantees nothing.

India, as of 2023, holds approximately 1.41 billion people — having recently overtaken China to become the most populous country on Earth. Seventeen and a half percent of the world's population lives on 2.4 percent of its land area. The national average density was 382 persons per square kilometre in the 2011 census; current estimates suggest something around 470. But the national average obscures the continent-scale variation within it. Bihar reaches 1,106 persons per square kilometre; West Bengal 1,028; Kerala 860; Uttar Pradesh 829. At the other end: Arunachal Pradesh at 17, Mizoram at 52, Sikkim at 86. The physical substrate explanation runs directly beneath these numbers — the alluvial plains of the Ganga and its tributaries support dense settled agriculture; the forested hill states of the northeast do not.

The composition of India's population matters as much as its size. The age structure is still relatively young — roughly 29 percent under 14, 64 percent in the working age band of 15 to 64, and 7 percent above 65 — with a median age of about 28 years. This is the demographic dividend in numerical form. The sex ratio in 2011 was 940 females per 1,000 males — a figure that sits below the world average and has been persistently poor. The spatial pattern inside that figure is one of the more revealing maps in Indian social geography. The south and east are markedly better: Kerala at 1,084, Tamil Nadu at 996, Andhra Pradesh at 993. The northwest — the prosperous agricultural belt — is markedly worse: Haryana at 879, Punjab at 895, Delhi at 868. This is not a coincidence. The agrarian prosperity of the northwest is real, but it sits inside a patriarchal inheritance that has historically treated daughters as liabilities; the PCPNDT Act of 1994 exists precisely as a response to the sex-selective practices that this inheritance produces. The south's higher ratios reflect a different inheritance: a Dravidian cultural tradition with historically stronger female autonomy and, in the case of Kerala, a century-long investment in female literacy. Literacy, in turn, is another composition axis: the national rate was 74 percent in 2011, with males at 82 percent and females at 65.5 percent. Kerala reaches 93.9 percent; Bihar sits at 63.8 percent. The religious composition, from the same census: Hindu 79.8 percent, Muslim 14.2 percent, Christian 2.3 percent, Sikh 1.7 percent, Buddhist 0.7 percent, Jain 0.4 percent. The rural-urban split — 31 percent urban in 2011, currently estimated at around 36 percent — makes India still a majority-rural country, unusual given its GDP level; most countries at comparable income were already over fifty percent urban.

On human development I want to be brief, because the economics surface carries this properly. The Human Development Index, introduced in 1990 by Mahbub ul-Haq in collaboration with Amartya Sen's capability framework, combines life expectancy, education, and per capita income adjusted for purchasing power into a single number between zero and one. India sits in the medium-development tier, around 134 out of 193 countries in recent rankings — well below where its economic size alone would place it, which is itself a political fact. The gap between GDP rank and HDI rank is the country telling you something about how growth has been distributed and what it has, so far, failed to reach.

Migration is the slowest-moving of the population forces but not the least important. By place of birth, about 307 million people in India in 2001 were living somewhere other than where they were born — roughly 30 percent of the population at that point. Migration streams divide into four types: rural-to-rural, rural-to-urban, urban-to-urban, and urban-to-rural. Rural-to-rural is the largest by volume, and it is dominated by women — most of them moving on marriage, which is classified as migration when it crosses a district boundary. Rural-to-urban is dominated by men seeking employment. The push-pull framework organises the causes: push factors are the things that make origin places intolerable (poverty, landlessness, flood, drought, slow wages); pull factors are the things that make destinations attractive (jobs, schools, hospitals, the city's possibility of anonymity and reinvention). The inter-state corridors are a geographical fact in their own right. Uttar Pradesh and Bihar are the dominant net out-migration states — UP losing on net about 2.6 million people and Bihar about 1.7 million in the early 2000s. Maharashtra, Delhi, Gujarat, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu are the dominant net in-migration destinations. Maharashtra gains on net about 2.3 million; greater Mumbai receives the highest absolute urban in-migration of any city in the country. This flow — the poor east to the industrialising west and south — feeds the construction sites and textile mills and domestic-service economy of the receiving states. The seasonal migration underneath the census figures is even larger and structurally invisible: agricultural labourers from Bihar, Odisha, Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh move for four to eight months for harvest or construction and then move back, never appearing as migrants in any count. The 2020 lockdown made this population suddenly, painfully visible.

India's external diaspora is one of the largest in the world — roughly 32 million people across more than 110 countries — and the country receives the world's largest remittance flow, somewhere around $125 billion annually. The diaspora formed in three waves. The first, during the British colonial period, was involuntary: millions from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar were sent under the indentured labour system to Mauritius, Trinidad, Tobago, Guyana, Fiji, and South Africa as plantation workers. The second came from the 1970s oil boom in the Gulf: millions of relatively unskilled workers from Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, and increasingly other states took construction and service jobs in UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Qatar, and Kuwait. This stream did not transfer skills to the destination; it transferred remittances back to the origin, funding the Kerala house, the brick-and-mortar school fees, the debt repaid. The third wave, from the 1960s onwards, was skilled: professionals and students moving to USA, UK, Canada, Australia, Singapore, Malaysia. The forced migration streams — Tibetan refugees post-1959, Sri Lankan Tamil post-1983, Bangladeshi post-1971, Rohingya post-2017 — are a separate and more politically fraught category that I want to name without litigating.

Settlement geography asks where, within a landscape, people actually put their houses — and the answer turns out to be shaped directly by the terrain and social inheritance beneath them. In rural India, four settlement forms exist. Clustered or compact settlements — houses tightly packed, fields outside — dominate the fertile alluvial plains of the Indo-Gangetic plain, and the northeast; here, the abundance of water and flat farmland makes dense clustering rational, and historical needs of defence and irrigation management reinforced it. Semi-clustered settlements appear in Punjab, Haryana, and parts of Rajasthan, where a dominant castes' main village is surrounded by smaller hamlets of other communities — a partial dispersal that reflects social fracture rather than physical necessity. Hamleted settlements — where what appears on the map as a single village is actually several discrete sub-villages called panna, para, palli, nagla, or dhani in different regional dialects — are common in the lower Ganga plain, West Bengal, and Chhattisgarh, and tend to reflect ethnic and caste separation at a fine spatial grain. Dispersed settlements — isolated farmsteads or huts separated by fields and forest — appear where the terrain makes clustering impossible or where social organisation does not require it: the Himalayan slopes, Meghalaya, Uttarakhand, and Kerala's hilly laterite interior.

Urban settlement in India is defined by a triple census criterion: a minimum population of 5,000, a population density of at least 400 per square kilometre, and at least 75 percent of the male working population in non-agricultural activities. Plus all statutory towns — those declared under a state municipal act regardless of size or density. This third criterion is the one that actually distinguishes a genuine town from a large agricultural village, and it is the criterion that struggles most with India's semi-urbanised fringes. Towns are classified by population into six census classes, from Class I (above 100,000, of which there are roughly 500 in India) downward. Cities above one million — about 50 — are metropolitan; those above ten million are megacities: Delhi NCR, Mumbai MMR, Kolkata, Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad in roughly that order. Urban centres also have a functional character: administrative (Delhi, Bhubaneswar, Gandhinagar), industrial (Jamshedpur, Bhilai), transport and port (Vishakhapatnam, Mughalsarai, Itarsi), commercial (Mumbai, Kolkata), mining (Dhanbad, Singrauli), religious (Varanasi, Tirupati, Madurai, Amritsar, Ajmer), educational (Pilani, Roorkee), tourist (Shimla, Goa). Most large cities eventually become multifunctional as they grow — Mumbai is simultaneously commercial, industrial, educational, and administrative — but the functional origin shapes the urban morphology long after it ceases to dominate.

A word on world settlement archetypes, briefly. Rural settlement patterns follow terrain with almost predictable regularity: linear patterns along roads and rivers, nucleated patterns around water sources or defensive hills, dispersed patterns in frontier farming landscapes where land is abundant and neighbours are not needed. The world's great megalopolises are a twentieth-century invention. Patrick Geddes coined the term conurbation in 1915 to describe the merging of adjacent urban areas. Jean Gottmann coined megalopolis in 1957 for the largest of these mergings: the Boswash corridor of Northeast USA — Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Washington — a near-continuous urbanised zone of roughly 50 million people. The Tokaido megalopolis in Japan, running from Tokyo to Osaka through Nagoya and Kyoto, is its Pacific counterpart. India's equivalent corridors are still forming: the Delhi-Mumbai industrial corridor and the Chennai-Bengaluru corridor are the two being most actively planned and promoted.

This vast and uneven population — concentrated on fertile plains, thinned on mountains and deserts, flowing along corridors of migration, clustering into cities whose logic was partly the terrain and partly the colonial port — has to do something to stay alive. What it does, and what the landscape permits it to do, is the subject of the next chapter.
