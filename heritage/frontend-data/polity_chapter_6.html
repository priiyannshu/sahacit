6 — How is power split across levels?

I closed the previous chapter with the question this one is built around. The architecture set out there told you how power is organised within each level of the apparatus — within the Union, within a State, within a local body. It left open the question of how power is divided between these levels. This is the question of federalism, and I think it is the question on which more of the country's political life turns than is usually acknowledged.

Begin, as before, with the architectural fact, because the framers — having read the federal experiments of the previous century carefully — did something rather unusual. They did not say, in vague terms, that the Centre and the States each have their spheres and the boundary between them shall be worked out as it goes. They wrote out the spheres in enumerated lists. The document carries, in one of its later schedules, three lists. The first list contains the matters on which the central Parliament has exclusive power to legislate — defence, foreign affairs, currency, banking, atomic energy, railways, the postal service, citizenship, several dozen others. The second list contains the matters on which the State legislatures have exclusive power to legislate — public order within the State, police, public health, agriculture, land, fisheries, several dozen others. The third list — what the framers called the concurrent list — contains the matters on which both can legislate, with a tie-breaking rule that the Union prevails on conflict.

This much sounds even-handed: the Union has its sphere, the States have theirs, and there is a shared sphere where both can act. Federations, you might think, look something like this. But read the document one clause further, because the most decisive choice the framers made on this question is buried just past the lists. Residuary power — the power to legislate on any matter not enumerated in any of the three lists, on any matter the framers did not foresee or did not bother to specify — belongs to the central Parliament. Not to the States. The matters the framers wrote down are divided between the two; the matters they left out belong only to one.

This is not how most federal documents are written. The federations that have come together by the assembling of previously sovereign units — the United States, Australia, the original Canada — typically reserve the residuary power to the units, on the principle that the units, having pooled certain powers in a centre, retain everything they did not pool. Our document tilts the other way. It begins, as it were, from the Union as the original holder of all power, and then enumerates what is to be parcelled out to the States — leaving everything unenumerated still in the Centre's hands. The herd, on this account, was not constituted by the assembling of pre-existing flocks; it was constituted as a single herd, organised into administrative regions for the convenience of governance. The structure of the document records this view of where authority originally lies.

There are then a series of clauses that deepen the tilt. The State List is not, as it appears at first, a sanctuary into which the Union may not legislate. The central Parliament may legislate on a matter on the State List in the national interest, if the upper house — the house in which the States themselves are represented — passes a resolution by a two-thirds majority saying so. The central Parliament may legislate on a State List matter at the request of two or more States. The central Parliament may legislate on State List matters to give effect to international treaty obligations. The central Parliament may legislate on State List matters during an emergency. Look at this list of routes carefully. The State List, in formal terms, contains matters on which the States have exclusive power. In practical terms, the Union retains five different doors through which it can legislate on those matters when the political conditions are right. The exclusivity is more a presumption than a wall.

The financial architecture deepens the tilt further. The major sources of revenue — income tax on persons and corporations, customs duties, central excise, until recently a great many indirect taxes — are constitutionally allocated to the Union. The States have access to a much narrower set: stamp duties, alcohol excise, agricultural income tax, a few others. But the work the States are constitutionally required to do — public health, education, police, agriculture, social welfare — is enormously expensive. So the document arranges, every five years, for a body to be constituted whose task is to determine the formulae by which a portion of the centrally collected revenue is transferred to the States. The States cannot fully fund themselves from their own taxes; they are constitutionally fiscally dependent on transfers from the Union. The Union sets the tap, the States receive the water. This is a structural fact about the federation that has, I think, more political consequences than the legislative architecture above it.

Why did the framers tilt the federation this far towards the Centre? The honest answer requires us to remember the immediate genealogy of the document. The framers were writing in the years immediately after the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946, which had proposed a more decentralised arrangement — a weak Centre with substantial powers reserved to the regions and to the provinces. That proposal had failed, and its failure had been one of the proximate causes of the violent partition of the country. Most of the framers had lived through the riots, the displacements, the killings of 1947. They had concluded — and I think it is a defensible conclusion — that strong centripetal pressure was, under the conditions they faced, the only thing that would hold the new state together. The herd had only just narrowly avoided fragmenting; a constitution that gave more autonomy to its parts might invite the next fragmentation. So they built strong centralisation into the document, even while calling it a federation, and they were not embarrassed by the seeming inconsistency.

This produced a vocabulary problem that has not really been resolved. The document is technically federal but politically tilted, and constitutional scholars have spent decades trying to find the right adjective. Quasi-federal (the term the political scientist K. C. Wheare used to describe the Indian arrangement) captures the tilt but underplays the genuine federal architecture. Cooperative federalism captures the way the levels are required to work together but underplays the structural dependence of the lower on the higher. Holding-together federalism (a phrase from the comparative literature on multi-national states) captures the genealogy — that this is a federation built to prevent fragmentation, not one built by the assembling of separate units. None of these phrases is perfectly satisfactory. The system we have is what the framers built; the names we give it are approximations.

There is, however, one thing the framers did that is the opposite of what their centralising choices would have suggested, and it is among the most consequential things in the document. They could not, given the diversity of languages, scripts, and cultural traditions across the herd, impose any single uniformity. So they did something quietly radical: they redrew the map along linguistic lines. The territorial reorganisation of the country in 1956, on the recommendations of a commission set up to investigate the question, gave each major linguistic community a State of its own. The boundaries of the units of the federation were aligned, where possible, with the boundaries of the linguistic communities. The substantive power was kept central, but the form of the units accommodated plurality. The map was made plural so that the staff could remain central. I think this is one of the document's most underappreciated structural commitments, because it is the reason the linguistic dispute that nearly broke the country in the 1950s and 60s did not, in the end, break it.

The language question more broadly was settled by a similar compromise. The document declared Hindi to be the official language of the Union, but provided that English would continue to be used for official purposes for fifteen years, and made provision for Parliament to extend the use of English thereafter. Parliament has, repeatedly, exercised that provision. The schedule of recognised languages has grown over the decades to include twenty-two languages. No single language was finally imposed on the country. The framers' instinct, here, was to defer the question rather than answer it — to give Hindi the formal honour, give English the practical reality, and let the political process work out the rest. It has continued to work it out.

This brings us to the question of how the levels of the federation actually interact — how disagreements between the Union and the States are managed in practice. The document provides for a council in which the President may convene representatives of the Union and the States to discuss matters of common interest. There are, also, regional councils that bring together groups of neighbouring States. Outside the document, more recent arrangements — most notably the consolidated tax council that emerged in the second decade of this century, which now sets the rates for the major shared tax — have created a kind of operational federalism that the original framers did not anticipate but that has grown into the most intensive forum of Centre-State coordination we now have. The cooperative mechanisms are uneven, often sluggish, and politically contested, but they are the visible practice of a federation whose written architecture tilts so far towards the Centre that, without them, the States would have no real seat at the table.

There is, finally, the structural tension that the architecture cannot dissolve. The document permits the Union, under specified conditions, to dismiss a State government and place the State directly under central rule. The conditions are restrictive on paper: the State machinery must have failed, the constitutional government must be unable to function. The conditions have, in practice, been read more elastically than the framers can have intended; the power to centrally administer a State has been used, at various times, against State governments controlled by parties opposed to the one in power at the Centre. The highest court has, over the decades, narrowed the conditions under which this power can be deployed, but the deployment itself remains a recurring fault line in the federation.

Read all of this together. The federation is constitutional fact: there are levels, there are spheres, there are lists, there is judicial enforcement of the boundary. The federation is also tilted: residuary at the Centre, fiscal dependence of the States, multiple breach-routes for central legislation on State subjects, the latent power of central administration. And the federation has, despite this tilt, accommodated more linguistic and cultural plurality than perhaps any other state on earth, by the device of redrawing its internal map even as it kept its substantive staff concentrated. These three features, in tension with each other, constitute the answer to the chapter's question.

The architecture above tells you how power is split across levels. It does not tell you who, at each level, holds it — how the people who staff the political branches are chosen, by what means consent is renewed across electoral cycles, what stops those who win an election from converting their majority into a permanent capture of the apparatus. That is the question of the next chapter, and it is one of the most intricate problems any modern democracy has to solve.
