5 — Why India breathes the way it does

If you climbed straight up from where you are sitting right now, you would pass through 99% of all the air that exists on this planet within the first 32 kilometres. Thirty-two kilometres is roughly the distance from the centre of Mumbai to its northern suburbs — a distance you might drive in an hour. Above that, the air continues for another 10,000 kilometres before it fades completely into space, but that upper stretch is so vanishingly thin that it barely registers in the physics of weather. Everything that matters — every cloud, every rain, every wind, every cyclone, every monsoon — happens in the lowest 10 to 15 kilometres. The atmosphere is the thinnest and most consequential skin imaginable, and it came out of the rock itself. I said in the first chapter that the early Earth was outgassed by volcanic vents, that water vapour condensed into the first oceans, and that cyanobacteria released oxygen over two and a half billion years. That is the atmosphere's origin — not a primordial given but a geological and biological production. India breathes air that life made.

That atmosphere, if you read it vertically, is a stack of distinct layers, each with different physics. The lowest is the troposphere, extending from the surface up to about 18 kilometres at the equator and 8 kilometres at the poles — thicker at the equator because warm air expands and pushes the ceiling higher. This is where all weather happens; every cloud, raindrop, and windstorm you have ever experienced was inside the troposphere. Temperature drops with altitude through this layer at roughly 6.5°C per kilometre — the normal lapse rate — which is why high peaks are cold even in the tropics. Above the troposphere lies the stratosphere, from 18 to 50 kilometres, where something unusual happens: temperature rises with altitude. The reason is the ozone layer, concentrated between 20 and 35 kilometres, which absorbs ultraviolet radiation from the Sun and heats the surrounding air in the process. The ozone layer is the reason complex life exists on land at all; without it, UV flux at the surface would be enough to damage DNA and prevent most terrestrial biology. Above the stratosphere, the mesosphere cools again, reaching the coldest atmospheric temperatures — around -90°C at its top — where meteors burn up as they fall. The thermosphere above it absorbs intense solar energy, reaching temperatures of over 1,000°C on instruments, but the air there is so sparse that it carries negligible heat; it is also where auroras form and where radio waves are reflected by the ionised ionosphere within it. Beyond that, the exosphere bleeds gradually into vacuum. All of this above your head, every moment. The atmosphere is the most layered inheritance the planet gave us, and the one we depend on most completely.

The climatically decisive gases in this shell are not the most abundant ones. Nitrogen is 78%, oxygen 21%, argon about 1% — these are the structural gases, chemically inert or relatively so. What matters for climate is smaller in quantity: water vapour, which varies between 0 and 4% of the lower atmosphere and is the most powerful greenhouse gas, concentrated mainly in the tropics and near the surface; and carbon dioxide at just 0.04%, trace by volume but the dominant driver of the greenhouse effect — the process by which these gases absorb outgoing infrared radiation that the warmed surface radiates upward, and re-radiate it back, keeping the planet roughly 33°C warmer than it would otherwise be. Without the natural greenhouse effect, Earth's average surface temperature would sit around -18°C rather than the +15°C it actually is. The greenhouse effect, in its baseline form, is not a problem — it is what makes the planet habitable. The enhanced version from anthropogenic emissions is a different story, and belongs to chapter 12.

The engine of all of this — the thing that makes the air actually move — is the Sun, and the Sun heats the planet unevenly. Insolation, incoming solar radiation, varies enormously with latitude. At the equator, the Sun's rays arrive nearly vertical, concentrating energy on a small patch of ground. At higher latitudes, the same rays arrive obliquely, spreading the same quantity of energy over a much larger area and passing through more atmosphere on the way. The result is a persistent latitudinal heat gradient: the tropics are permanently warmer than the poles. In the long run, what comes in must equal what goes out — this is the heat budget — but the imbalance between latitudes is permanent, and it is this imbalance that sets the atmosphere in motion.

Think of the atmosphere as a heat engine trying to even out that gradient. The simplest version would be one large circulation cell per hemisphere: warm air rises at the equator, moves poleward at altitude, cools, sinks at the pole, and flows back along the surface. But the Earth rotates, and rotation disrupts this neat picture. What actually forms is three cells per hemisphere. The Hadley cell occupies the zone between the equator and about 30°: warm moist air rises at the equatorial zone — this is the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, the ITCZ — moves poleward at altitude, cools, and subsides around 25–30° latitude in the sub-tropical high pressure belt. The Ferrel cell sits between roughly 30° and 60°, an indirect thermal cell driven by the Hadley and Polar cells on either side. The Polar cell completes the picture from 60° to the poles. The resulting pressure pattern is alternating belts running around the globe: equatorial low (ITCZ), sub-tropical high, subpolar low, polar high.

These belts explain some of the most consequential facts about where things are on the planet. Where air rises — at the ITCZ and the subpolar lows — you get low pressure, cloud, and rainfall. Where air sinks — at the sub-tropical high and polar high — you get high pressure, clear skies, and aridity. This is why the world's great deserts cluster around 30° latitude in both hemispheres: the Sahara across North Africa, the Arabian desert, the Thar in Rajasthan, the Gobi in Central Asia, the Atacama along the South American Pacific coast, the Kalahari in southern Africa, the deserts of interior Australia. The Thar is not a geological accident. It is India's share of a planetary dryness belt that runs all the way around the Earth at 25–30° north. From these pressure belts flow the planetary winds: the Trade winds, blowing from the sub-tropical high down to the equatorial low — deflected by the Coriolis force (the apparent deflection of any moving object caused by Earth's rotation, rightward in the Northern Hemisphere, leftward in the Southern) into Northeast Trade winds in the north and Southeast Trade winds in the south; the Westerlies, blowing from the sub-tropical high toward the subpolar low, from the southwest in the Northern Hemisphere; and the Polar easterlies, from the polar highs toward the subpolar lows.

High above all of this — in the upper troposphere, near the tropopause — are the jet streams: narrow rivers of very fast air, sometimes 200–300 kilometres per hour, circling the globe in a loose, meandering path. The sub-tropical jet stream, found around 30°N, flows west to east. In the Indian context, it sits south of the Himalayas during winter and drags along with it the weather systems called western disturbances — extra-tropical storms originating over the Mediterranean Sea that travel eastward, bringing winter rain to Punjab, Haryana, and the northwestern Himalayas, and depositing snow on the high passes. Wheat in the Rabi season owes much of its moisture to western disturbances riding the sub-tropical jet. In late May or early June, this jet abruptly shifts north of the Himalayas, and that shift is one of the key triggers for the monsoon's onset. The polar-front jet, around 60°N, drives mid-latitude weather systems. In summer, a third jet matters enormously for India: the tropical easterly jet, or TEJ, which blows east to west over peninsular India at roughly 14 kilometres altitude. It is a summer-only structure, generated by the heating of the Tibetan Plateau, and its strength is closely correlated with the strength of the monsoon below it. These upper-atmosphere rivers act as steering currents for everything below them.

Monsoons — seasonally reversing wind systems — exist wherever large landmasses and adjacent oceans heat at different rates. West Africa has one; eastern China has one. But the Indian monsoon is the largest, most persistent, and most agriculturally consequential on the planet. About 75–80% of India's total annual rainfall arrives in four months. A single weak monsoon can cut agricultural output sharply enough to move food prices across a subcontinent. Understanding why the Indian monsoon is so strong and so seasonal requires holding several reinforcing mechanisms in mind simultaneously, because no single factor explains it.

The first and most basic is differential heating. In summer, the Indian subcontinent and the Tibetan Plateau heat far faster than the adjacent Indian Ocean. The hot land creates a thermal low. The cooler ocean is relatively high-pressure. Air flows from high to low, from ocean to land, loaded with moisture picked up over a long ocean track. In winter the gradient reverses: land cools faster, pressure over land rises, winds blow outward from land to sea — dry, carrying little rain to most of India. That basic see-saw is the monsoon's skeleton.

What amplifies it into the most dominant atmospheric event in Asia is a specific set of reinforcing mechanisms that compound on one another. The ITCZ, which in winter sits near the equator, shifts northward in summer to lie over the Ganga plain and the foothills of the Himalayas; in this position it is called the monsoon trough, and it provides a persistent low-pressure axis into which the moisture-laden winds pour from the south and southwest. The Tibetan Plateau, sitting at an average of 4,500 metres and covering an enormous area, heats intensely in summer and generates a powerful upper-air anticyclone — the Tibetan high — which pulls the low-level monsoon circulation in from the southwest. The sub-tropical jet stream has already been described as retreating north of the Himalayas in late May or early June; its withdrawal from south of the range removes a stabilising upper-level influence and allows the monsoon to burst northward. A low-level jet called the Somali Jet — named for the Somali coast off East Africa from which it originates, also called the Findlater Jet — flows from East Africa northeastward across the Arabian Sea, carrying enormous quantities of moisture directly into the monsoon. Once the monsoon is established over the peninsula, the tropical easterly jet reinforces the circulation from above. Finally, two large-scale ocean-atmosphere oscillations modulate monsoon strength year to year: El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) — in El Niño years the eastern Pacific warms, trade winds weaken, and the Indian monsoon tends toward deficit and drought; in La Niña years the pattern reverses and the monsoon tends to be stronger — and the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), which measures the sea-surface temperature difference between the western and eastern Indian Ocean; a positive IOD generally enhances monsoon rainfall. The compounding of all these factors — differential heating, ITCZ northward shift, Tibetan high, sub-tropical jet retreat, Somali Jet, tropical easterly jet, and interannual ocean states — is why the Indian monsoon is what it is: not a sea breeze scaled up, but a convergence of mechanisms all pointing in the same direction at the same time.

The onset arrives at the Kerala coast around June 1st — the monsoon burst — recognised by a sharp transition from the oppressive dry heat of May to continuous heavy rainfall. From there the monsoon advances in two distinct arms. The Arabian Sea arm moves northward along the west coast, strikes the Western Ghats, and deposits most of its moisture on the windward western slopes; the Konkan coast and Kerala receive several hundred millimetres in a single month. The leeward Deccan rain shadow behind the Western Ghats receives comparatively little — Pune sits in it, which is why Pune and Mumbai have almost identical latitudes but radically different annual rainfall totals. The Bay of Bengal arm strikes Assam and the northeastern hills first, deflects westward along the southern slope of the Himalayas, and feeds the Ganga plain from east to west. The Khasi hills of Meghalaya funnel the moisture-laden winds from the Bay into a concentrated upslope channel and produce the highest recorded rainfall on Earth: Mawsynram averages roughly 11,871 millimetres a year — and the nearby Cherrapunji has recorded single-month totals that no other recorded place has exceeded. Rainfall everywhere else in India measures itself, implicitly, as a position somewhere along the gradient between Mawsynram and the western Thar.

India does not have a simple summer-winter year. It has four meteorologically distinct seasons. The cold weather season runs December through February: the sub-tropical jet has returned south of the Himalayas, Northeast Trade winds blow dry and cool from the land, temperatures in the north fall to near zero and lower in exposed valleys, dense fog blankets the Indo-Gangetic plains, and the only significant rainfall — vital for wheat — comes from western disturbances carried in by the jet. The hot weather season, March through May, is characterised by rapid temperature rise, an intensifying thermal low over the Thar and northwest India, and local atmospheric events that are physics, not decoration: the loo, a desiccating hot dry wind that pushes temperatures above 45°C across Rajasthan, Punjab, and the UP plains in May and June; the Kalbaisakhi — also called Nor'westers — violent convective thunderstorms moving northwest to southeast across West Bengal and Assam, bringing destructive hail but also moisture critical for tea gardens and jute; and the mango showers of Kerala and Karnataka, convective pre-monsoon rains that accelerate mango ripening and assist coffee flowering. The advancing southwest monsoon, June through September, is the subject of everything above. The retreating northeast monsoon, October through November, is the reversal: as land pressure rises and the ITCZ retreats southward, winds blow from land toward ocean. For most of India this is a dry transition. But those winds cross the Bay of Bengal, pick up moisture, and deposit it on the Coromandel Coast — Tamil Nadu and southern Andhra Pradesh receive their main annual rainfall in these two months. Chennai's annual rainfall calendar is essentially the mirror image of Mumbai's.

The spatial range of India's climate is exceptional even by global standards. Rainfall varies from fewer than 100 millimetres a year in the Thar to over 11,000 millimetres at Mawsynram — a gradient of more than a hundredfold inside a single country. Temperature swings from around -45°C in winter at Drass in Ladakh to around 50°C in summer at Phalodi in Rajasthan. Coastal areas — Mumbai, Kochi, Chennai — have equable climates because the adjacent ocean moderates; interior locations — Delhi, Nagpur, Bikaner — swing widely between seasons. The Tropic of Cancer bisects India roughly from Gujarat to Mizoram, marking the line south of which the Sun stands directly overhead at noon twice a year; north of it, India is technically in the temperate zone, but the loo is a reminder that latitude is only one variable.

The most widely used system for naming world climate types was developed by Wladimir Köppen, who mapped climate zones onto five letter-groups based on temperature and precipitation thresholds: A (tropical), B (dry), C (warm temperate), D (cold temperate), E (polar), each subdivided further. India contains almost all of these within its borders, which is unusual for a country of its size. Most of the peninsular interior is Aw — tropical savanna, warm year-round with a distinct dry season. Kerala and the northeastern hill states approach Am — tropical monsoon, with heavier and longer rainfall. The Thar sits in BWh — hot desert. The Deccan rain-shadow areas are BSh — hot semi-arid steppe. The Ganga plain is broadly Cwa/Cwg — humid subtropical with a dry winter, the same regime as much of eastern China. The high Himalayas and Ladakh have ET — tundra. This internal diversity — from desert to tropical monsoon to tundra — is the inheritance of India's latitudinal range, its topographic extremity, and the monsoon's habit of treating windward and leeward sides of every range as different worlds.

One thing before we move on. The climate inherited from the monsoon is not a stable inheritance. Under global warming, monsoon variability is increasing: the same four months still produce most of India's rain, but those months are beginning to contain more intense wet spells and longer dry intervals within them, rather than the steadier rainfall that rain-fed agriculture works best with. Himalayan glaciers are retreating, which will in time affect dry-season flow in the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra river systems that carry glacial melt. Sea levels are rising along India's long coastline. These are the outlines of a new inheritance being laid down in this generation, one without precedent in the span of human civilisation. The mechanisms belong to chapter 12. What matters here is that the atmospheric physics we have worked through in this chapter — pressure gradients, circulation cells, jet streams, the compounding of ocean and land forcing — is also the physics by which that change is playing out.

The next chapter turns from the atmosphere to what lies beneath it. The atmosphere gave India its rain and its seasonal calendar. The next question is what the substrate itself contains — the rocks, the minerals, the energy stores that the planet laid down over deep time and that the Indian peninsula inherited.
