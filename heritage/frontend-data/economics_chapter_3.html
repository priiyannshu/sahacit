3 — Where does money come from, and who decides?

The paper note in your wallet is, if you stop to think about it, a fairly strange object. It is printed on cheap material. It has no intrinsic use — you cannot eat it, wear it, or build shelter with it. The government has given it a fine name, legal tender, and declared that no creditor may refuse it, but the declaration itself reveals the strangeness: why would anyone need a law to compel acceptance of something genuinely useful?

People have been asking this question, in one form or another, for as long as there have been markets, and the standard answer is that money works because everyone believes it will work. The shoe manufacturer sells shoes for rupees because she believes the wheat seller will accept rupees; the wheat seller accepts rupees because he believes the tailor will. The belief sustains itself in a loop, and the loop is money. This is not particularly reassuring. A thing whose value rests entirely on collective expectation is, in some sense, a shared hallucination — one that happens to be the most durable hallucination in human history, and on which a great deal of real activity depends.

Before there were central banks, economies tried to ground this belief in something solid. The oldest answer was commodity money — grains, cattle, precious metals, things that had uses beyond exchange so their value was, in principle, independent of anyone's belief. Barter, the system commodity money replaced, required a double coincidence of wants: you needed someone who simultaneously wanted what you had and had what you wanted. Money abolished that constraint. Gold and silver became the dominant commodity monies because they were durable, divisible, portable, and scarce. Then came the next problem: carrying gold is inconvenient, and verifying its weight and purity is slow. Paper notes backed by gold — claims on a specific quantity of metal held in a vault — were more practical, but they required trust in the issuing institution. And then came the rupee.

British India ran on a silver rupee, whose value the colonial government pegged first to silver and then, after the silver price collapsed in the 1870s, to sterling. A rupee pegged to sterling meant that India's monetary conditions were set in London, not in Calcutta. When Britain needed to tighten credit — when the Bank of England raised rates to defend the pound — Indian credit conditions tightened too, whether Indian conditions warranted it or not. The colonial monetary system was designed for sterling's convenience, not India's. The Reserve Bank of India was established by the RBI Act of 1934 and began operations on 1 April 1935, originally as a privately owned shareholders' bank. One of the first things the independent government did was nationalise it in 1949. That choice was not administrative housekeeping. It was monetary sovereignty: the country that issued the rupee would, for the first time, decide what the rupee was worth and how tight or loose credit conditions would be.

But before the RBI, there is a more fundamental question: why a central bank at all? The nineteenth century had an answer, sometimes called the Free Banking school: let competing banks issue their own notes, let depositors choose between them, and competition will discipline over-issuers. Scotland tried this system, and the American frontier briefly operated under it. The experience of the century repeatedly discredited the argument. Panics spread through correspondent relationships — bank A held deposits at bank B which held deposits at bank C, and when confidence in C collapsed, A and B collapsed with it. The carnage was not competition's self-correction. It was contagion, and it moved faster than any regulatory intervention could follow.

The answer the Victorian era worked out was the lender of last resort — a single institution, outside the competitive system, that would lend to banks in a panic and break the contagion before it became general. Walter Bagehot gave the principle its canonical statement in Lombard Street, published in 1873:

To avert panic, central banks should lend early and freely, to solvent firms, against good collateral, and at high rates.

Each element matters. Lend freely — do not ration credit in a crisis, because the whole point is to stop the panic from spreading. At high rates — to punish the imprudent bank and ensure the facility is not abused in normal times. Against good collateral — to protect the lender of last resort itself from assuming the losses of failed banks. This 150-year-old formula still defines what modern central banks do when markets seize. The RBI inherited the Bagehot architecture.

Now, here is something that surprises most people who have not looked closely at where money actually comes from. The RBI creates what economists call high-powered money, or the monetary base (M0) — the notes and coins in circulation, plus the reserves commercial banks hold with the RBI. This is the only money the RBI directly issues. Almost everything beyond it is created by banks, and by every person who has ever taken a loan.

When a commercial bank makes a loan, it does not go to its vault and count out notes. It creates a deposit. You borrow ten lakh rupees; the bank does not transfer notes from somewhere else. It types a number into its ledger, and ten lakh rupees of new money exists in the economy that did not exist an hour before. This new deposit is itself money — as liquid, as spendable, as transferable as the notes in your wallet. When you spend the loan and the seller deposits your payment at another bank, that bank keeps a fraction as reserves and lends the rest, creating another deposit. Each round creates more. The ratio between the total money supply and the original monetary base is called the money multiplier. If banks collectively keep 10 per cent as reserves and lend the other 90, one hundred rupees of new base money can generate close to a thousand rupees of deposits in the banking system.

The consequence is that most of what we call money — the balance in a savings account, the salary that appears as a bank credit — is bank money: entries in ledgers, created by lending and destroyed when loans are repaid. The RBI issues currency; the banks issue the economy. This distinction is not academic. It means that controlling "the money supply" is not simply a matter of printing more or fewer notes. It means influencing the lending behaviour of hundreds of commercial banks, each making its own continuous assessment of which borrowers are creditworthy and at what price.

Economists measure this money at two principal levels. M1 — narrow money — is currency in circulation plus demand deposits (accounts one can withdraw from immediately). M3 — broad money — adds time deposits to M1: the fixed deposits sitting in accounts for months or years. M3 is the standard measure of total money supply. The gap between M1 and M3 is, roughly, the money the economy has committed to saving rather than spending — less liquid, but present in the system.

The RBI's toolkit for influencing this system is an inheritance from two different eras, and seeing which tool came from which era explains why the toolkit looks the way it does.

CRR — the Cash Reserve Ratio — is the older, blunter instrument. Commercial banks must hold a specified percentage of their deposits as cash reserves with the RBI, earning nothing. Raise it, and banks have less to lend; lower it, and they have more. In the planning era, when the government wanted direct control over credit volumes and price signals were weak and unreliable, CRR was a primary lever. It is a quantitative tool: it does not distinguish between a bank in Bombay lending to a textile mill and a bank in Calcutta lending to a speculator. It squeezes or loosens the entire system simultaneously.

SLR — the Statutory Liquidity Ratio — has a related but distinct origin. Banks must hold a percentage of their net demand and time liabilities in liquid assets: cash, gold, or approved government securities. Notice the last item. SLR was, in the planning era, a mechanism for compelling bank financing of government borrowing. The government needed capital for its Five-Year Plans; banks sat on large deposit bases; SLR directed a portion of those deposits toward government bonds regardless of whether banks would have chosen to purchase them freely. The government was using monetary regulation as a fiscal instrument. Over the decades the SLR ceiling has been progressively reduced — from near 40 per cent in the mid-1990s — but the underlying structure remains.

The modern price tool is the repo rate, operating through the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF), introduced in 2000. Under LAF, the RBI stands ready daily to lend money to banks against government securities — repo, short for repurchase agreement — and to borrow from banks (reverse repo). The repo rate is what the RBI charges to lend; the reverse repo rate is what it pays to borrow surplus funds. Between them they create a corridor: a floor and a ceiling for overnight interest rates in the banking system. When the RBI wants to tighten credit conditions, it raises the repo rate; borrowing from RBI becomes more expensive, banks raise their own lending rates, credit contracts. Above the corridor sits the Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) rate — 100 basis points above the repo — at which banks can borrow a limited additional amount when they have exhausted other options. Open Market Operations (OMOs) complement the corridor: when the RBI buys government securities from banks, it injects rupees into the system; when it sells, it absorbs them. OMOs manage the overall pool of liquidity that the repo corridor operates within.

Why maintain both the old quantitative tools and the newer price tool? I think the inheritance frame makes it visible. In 1950, in a capital-scarce economy where markets were thin and price signals were unreliable, one could not steer credit by adjusting an interest rate that banks might or might not respond to. CRR and SLR forced allocation. They worked, after a fashion, but at a cost: forced allocation insulates the lender from the consequences of bad lending decisions, because the decision to lend was not freely made. When 1991 liberalised the economy and price signals began to matter again, the repo rate emerged as the instrument the system could actually respond to. The old tools were not abolished — they are still active and still adjusted — but the operating procedure shifted. The repo rate became the primary signal; CRR and SLR became background conditions rather than the policy instrument of first resort.

The most recent transformation is the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), created by an amendment to the RBI Act in 2016. Before that, the RBI Governor decided the policy rate essentially alone. The 2016 framework created a six-member statutory body: three from the RBI — including the Governor, who chairs it — and three external members appointed by the central government. Decisions are by majority vote; the Governor holds a casting vote in a tie. Publishing the minutes and the votes of individual members was part of the design. Accountability requires a named decision, not an anonymous one.

The intellectual architecture for this shift had been laid two years earlier. The Urjit Patel Committee report of 2014 recommended moving to an explicit inflation targeting framework — the central bank publicly commits to a specific inflation number, uses the repo rate as its primary instrument, and is held accountable when it misses. A formal agreement between the Government of India and the RBI was signed in February 2015, and the 2016 amendment gave it statutory force. The target: 4 per cent CPI, with a ±2 per cent tolerance band. Sustained breach — above 6 per cent or below 2 per cent for three consecutive quarters — triggers a mandatory written explanation to the government.

The choice of inflation targeting over its alternatives is itself a constraint-and-choice moment worth slowing down on. Why not target nominal GDP growth, which captures both output and prices and so avoids the problem of supply-shock inflation? Because nominal GDP data arrives late, is repeatedly revised, and is a poor operating target for decisions made at two-monthly meetings. Why not target the money supply — the old monetarist prescription? Because the money multiplier is unstable; financial innovation creates new quasi-money instruments that change the relationship between M3 and prices in ways no formula can reliably track. The Urjit Patel committee's reasoning for choosing CPI inflation was pointed: it is the variable households experience directly. A target people can check against their grocery bills is a target the central bank cannot quietly miss. The 4 per cent figure was calibrated for a developing economy with structural food inflation — not the 2 per cent standard appropriate for mature low-inflation economies, but not 6 per cent either, which would have normalised inflation rather than targeted it. New Zealand adopted the world's first explicit inflation target in 1989; the Bank of England followed in 1997. India came to the framework late, but the 2015 agreement reflects a specific negotiation: a government that wanted growth headroom and a central bank that wanted a clear and bounded mandate.

I should flag, in passing, three current-affairs territories this chapter does not enter. Specific MPC decisions, voting records, and dissents belong to the current-affairs layer and change with every meeting. Recent OMO operations and the RBI's current liquidity stance are outside this structural account. And the CBDC — the Reserve Bank's digital rupee pilot, launched in 2022 — opens questions about whether central bank money might one day reach households directly, bypassing commercial banks entirely; that territory is beyond what this chapter can hold.

What the chapter can say, in closing, is this. Monetary policy is the most responsive instrument the country has for managing aggregate conditions. It can be adjusted every two months, publicly, with reasons and minutes published. But the transmission mechanism — repo rate change to bank lending rates to borrowing decisions to spending to prices — takes six to twelve months to work through fully. By the time a cut made in October has finished affecting consumer prices, it is the following spring, and the conditions that motivated the cut may have already changed. The instrument is precise in its setting and imprecise in its landing. It can fight demand-pull inflation — the kind that comes from too much credit chasing too few goods. It cannot fight a bad monsoon.

And there is a further limit, which points directly to what comes next. Monetary policy travels through banks. If banks are reluctant to lend — sitting on bad loans, worried about their own solvency, waiting for borrowers to repair their balance sheets before extending new credit — then the signal from the RBI stops at the first relay. The policy rate falls and lending does not follow. This is not a theoretical failure mode; it is what stretches of Indian banking have experienced over the last decade. How that problem is structured, and what the country has tried to do about it, is the subject of the next chapter.
