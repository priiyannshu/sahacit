12 — What is the economy for?

In the first chapter of this surface, I recalled that Simon Kuznets warned Congress in 1934 that goals for more growth should specify of what and for what. The warning was ignored almost immediately, and I said so, and then proceeded across ten chapters to walk through the apparatus that had ignored it — the institutions of money creation and banking, the mechanics of fiscal management and taxation, the price signals of inflation, the productive machinery of agriculture and industry, the international ledgers of the external sector. All of it was held inside that single number Kuznets invented, which grew or didn't grow, which the analysts coloured red or green on a Tuesday morning, which governments treated as a verdict on themselves.

I want to hold the original question here, at the close. What is all of this — the measuring, the lending, the taxing, the trading, the producing — actually for? The question is not rhetorical. In eleven previous chapters it was implicit, present as a pressure beneath every discussion of deficits and reserves and minimum support prices. Here it is explicit. And the only honest answer is: it is for the lives people get to live.

That answer, once given, immediately raises a harder question. Which lives have improved, and by how much? Answering that question runs directly into the problem of how to count the people for whom growth has not been enough.

A poverty line is a number drawn in the data, and what the number means depends entirely on how it was drawn. India has drawn it several times.

The first official poverty line came from the Lakdawala Committee in 1993. Its method was calorie-based and blunt: it asked how much food a person needed — 2,400 calories per day in rural areas, 2,100 in urban areas — calculated what that food cost in the market, and called that cost the poverty line. Separate thresholds for rural and urban. Consumption below the line meant poverty. The method was operationally clear but philosophically narrow: it treated poverty as hunger, and only hunger, and made no allowance for the fact that a poor household in a city without public health services faces deprivations that the calorie count cannot see.

The Tendulkar Committee, reporting in 2009, moved to a different basis. Rather than anchoring to calories and back-calculating cost, it anchored to the cost of a basket of basic needs across rural and urban areas — food, clothing, healthcare, education — and revised the line upward from where Lakdawala had placed it. The revision revealed a larger poor population than the earlier method had shown. This was not a discovery that more people had become poor. It was a discovery that the previous lens had been narrow. The politics of this was awkward: governments that had been reporting declining poverty numbers found the new methodology reset their baseline, and whatever decline they could claim had to be recomputed.

The Rangarajan Committee in 2014 revised again, with higher thresholds than Tendulkar. It was producing an estimate at almost exactly the moment the government changed, and its findings were quietly shelved. The methodological debate had by then acquired a recursive quality: each committee's line was contested by those whose poverty numbers it made worse, and celebrated by those whose poverty numbers it made better. The lines were doing political work, not only statistical work.

The most significant shift came from a different direction entirely. In 2021, NITI Aayog released India's Multidimensional Poverty Index, adopting the UNDP-OPHI framework — a measure that asks not about income alone but about deprivation across three dimensions: health, education, and standard of living, decomposed into twelve indicators including nutrition, child mortality, years of schooling, cooking fuel, drinking water, sanitation, housing, and electricity. A household is counted as poor in this measure if it is deprived in enough indicators simultaneously — not because it has crossed a calorie threshold, but because its members cannot access the basic conditions for a functioning life. This is Sen's capability approach, translated from philosophy into an operational index. It measures not what people earn but what they are able to do.

I think the sequence Lakdawala → Tendulkar → Rangarajan → MPI is worth reading as an inheritance story, not merely a methodological one. Each revision reflects a changing answer to the question of what poverty is. The Lakdawala answer is: insufficient calories. The Tendulkar answer is: insufficient consumption basket. The MPI answer is: insufficient capability. These are not refinements of the same concept; they are different concepts with the same name. And the choice between them is the choice between asking whether people have enough food and asking whether people have enough freedom — which is, as I noted in Chapter 1, the core of what Sen spent a decade arguing.

India's poverty has declined by most of these measures in most periods. NITI Aayog's MPI data suggests the headcount fell from around 25 per cent to 15 per cent between 2015–16 and 2019–21. But the decline is uneven across states, castes, genders, and age groups in ways the headline number does not show. Bihar and Jharkhand remain poor by MPI even as Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu approach middle-income complexity. That national poverty fell does not mean the life of a Dalit agricultural labourer in rural Uttar Pradesh improved proportionally to the life of an urban software engineer in Bengaluru. The number hides the distribution it is drawn from. That is precisely what Sen had warned about, and what the HDI story from Chapter 1 confirmed. The current-affairs layer — recent NFHS findings, the latest MPI round — will update the figures; the structure of the argument does not change with each new survey.

The employment question is, in some ways, the sharper version of the same problem.

Unemployment in India, as measured by the Periodic Labour Force Survey — which NSSO launched in 2017 as the successor to the older Employment and Unemployment Surveys it had been running since the 1970s — runs at roughly three to four per cent in recent years. The number sounds like success. It is not, or not simply. The three-to-four per cent figure counts as employed anyone who worked even one hour in the reference week. Most Indians who need work cannot afford to not work in some form — so they are counted as employed whether they are a software engineer on salary or a street vendor making two hundred rupees a day. The relevant measure is not whether people are working but whether they are working in ways that give them dignity, security, and a share of the economy's growth.

On that question, the picture is harder. Only around ten per cent of India's workforce is in the formal organised sector — employed with written contracts, provident fund contributions, and coverage under labour legislation. The remaining ninety per cent works in conditions that vary from precarious to desperate: informal agriculture, informal construction, informal street trade, informal domestic work. These are where most Indians earn, and where protections are lightest and volatility is highest. Youth unemployment is the headline crisis inside this picture; the inversion is particularly sharp — graduates report higher unemployment than school-leavers in some survey rounds, which signals not that education failed but that the formal economy is not generating jobs that use educated labour fast enough. The gig-economy layer — delivery riders, platform workers, app-based contractors — has grown explosively in the last decade. These workers are large in number, visible in every city, and almost entirely without the social protections that formal employment would require their employers to provide.

The structural transformation that accompanied industrialisation everywhere it happened — agricultural workers moving into factory employment, absorbing into a formal wage economy — has not happened in India at the required scale. India's services boom after 1991 produced extraordinary wealth in organised technology and finance but did not pull agricultural workers into factory employment the way East Asia's manufacturing path had done in Taiwan, South Korea, and later China. Services-led growth absorbs skilled labour and capital well; it absorbs unskilled surplus agricultural labour badly. India grew at over seven per cent annually through much of the 2000s and still has a chronic employment problem. This is not a paradox. It is the consequence of the specific growth path that 1991 unlocked, and it is the inheritance the next decade of policy has to work against.

The demographic dimension of this problem is what makes it urgent.

India's median age today is roughly 28. The working-age population — 15 to 64 — is growing as a share of the total, projected to peak around 2040 before declining as fertility falls and the population ages. Economists Bloom and Williamson, writing in the 1990s about East Asia's growth miracle, coined the phrase demographic dividend: when a large cohort of young people moves through working age in an economy that can absorb them, the dependency ratio falls, savings rise, and growth compounding begins. India's demographers have applied this framework to the country's own projections since the early 2000s, and it has become standard language in economic policy documents.

The dividend is a contingent claim, not a guarantee. It converts to growth only under specific conditions: the working-age cohort must be educated and healthy enough to be productive, and the economy must generate jobs that absorb them. Both conditions are unevenly met. The states with the youngest populations and the highest workforce growth — Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan — are precisely the states that rank lowest in educational attainment, health infrastructure, and employment generation. The states that have built the strongest human capital — Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Himachal Pradesh — are already past their demographic peak, with fertility at or near replacement. The dividend is spatially concentrated where the absorptive capacity is weakest. If that mismatch is not addressed in the next fifteen years, the dividend inverts: large youth cohorts without productive employment become a source of political volatility, not a source of growth. Notice the constraint-and-choice moment here: why not simply raise the minimum wage sharply and lift earnings by fiat? Because a binding minimum wage raises the cost of formal employment, and employers facing higher costs may respond by contracting the formal sector further and pushing more workers into informality — precisely the direction the problem does not need to go. The instrument that seems most direct often has a blunted or reversed effect when ninety per cent of workers are already informal. The trade-off is between the welfare of those protected by the higher wage and the welfare of those pushed further from formality by it.

The sustainability question adds a different kind of constraint — not one that can be designed around, but one that bounds the entire project from outside.

India is the world's third-largest emitter of greenhouse gases in absolute terms. In per-capita terms, the number falls to roughly 1.9 tonnes of CO2 equivalent per person per year, against 14 for the United States and 8 for China. This gap is India's most consistent argument in international climate negotiations. The historical stock of emissions in the atmosphere was put there almost entirely by the industrial economies of Europe and North America across the 20th century. India is being asked to constrain its development to address a crisis it did not create. That argument is not simply diplomatic leverage — it reflects a real distributive question about who bears the cost of a transition that everyone must collectively make.

The international architecture in which India operates runs from the 1972 Stockholm Conference — which established that environmental protection was a legitimate international concern — through the 1992 Rio Earth Summit, which introduced the Brundtland definition of sustainable development: meeting present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs, to the 2015 Paris Agreement, where India submitted its Intended Nationally Determined Contribution: a reduction in the emissions intensity of GDP by 33 to 35 per cent from 2005 levels by 2030, alongside renewable capacity targets. At the 2021 Glasgow COP, India committed to net zero by 2070 — two decades after China's 2060 target and three after the European Union's 2050 commitment.

Why 2070 and not 2050? The negotiated answer is the per-capita and historical-emissions argument. The structural answer is that India's energy system remains coal-dependent in ways that cannot be quickly unwound without collapsing the grid and the livelihoods tied to it. The coal regions of Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, and Odisha support communities whose employment, revenue, and economic logic are built around extraction. A just transition in those regions is not a technical problem that can be solved by building solar panels in Rajasthan — it requires an equivalent employment base in an economy that has not yet demonstrated it can generate that base for agricultural surplus labour, let alone for displaced mining workers. India's renewable additions are real and the trajectory has moved faster than any 2010 projection anticipated — but the current-affairs layer of where India's targets and progress stand today is what this chapter can only signal and defer. The structural ground is what this chapter provides. The argument about carbon space is the inheritance India carries into every COP negotiation, derived from the same constraint-and-choice logic that has run through this surface since Chapter 2.

I want to say one thing, before the last paragraph, about what the closing frame still does not see.

Every measure I have discussed — the poverty line, the MPI score, the PLFS unemployment rate, the demographic dependency ratio, the emissions intensity — is a chosen lens. Each makes something visible and something else invisible. The MPI sees deprivation across twelve indicators; it does not see what it feels like to live under caste discrimination. The PLFS sees whether you worked; it does not see whether the work was coerced or chosen. The emissions intensity commitment keeps India's per-capita number inside the argument; it does not ask what the land, forests, and groundwater of the country will look like when 2070 arrives, if it arrives. These are not criticisms of the measures. They are the honest acknowledgment that the measures, like GDP itself, were built — by particular people, for particular purposes, with particular blindspots. Every chapter in this surface has had this moment. This chapter is no different.

The economy is for the lives people get to live. Numbers measure the stage. The lives are what is on it.

The reader who has walked through twelve chapters has built, I hope, two things simultaneously. The first is a working knowledge of the apparatus — what a fiscal deficit means, how the RBI controls the money supply, why the current account matters, what the MSP does to agricultural incentives. These are the mechanical facts the examiner tests, and you now have them. The second is an orientation toward that apparatus — the habit of asking which lens you are being shown, who built it, when, against what alternative, and what it does not see. That orientation is harder to build and harder to lose. The country measured in tomorrow's GDP release is the residue of every choice made under every constraint by every previous generation — the colonial extraction that built railways to ports and broke artisanal industry, the planning era that built steel mills and throttled competition, the post-1991 opening that unlocked services and left manufacturing depth unbuilt. Tomorrow's number arrives freighted with that history, whether the analyst's coloured box notes it or not.

Carry both forward — the apparatus and the skepticism. They do not cancel each other. Together, they are the thing the surface was trying to give you.
