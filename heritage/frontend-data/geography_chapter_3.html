3 — The faces of the inheritance

Before anything about rocks or rivers or soils, there is a simpler inheritance to name: India's location on the planet. The coordinates that matter most are the latitudinal ones. India extends from 8°4'N in the south — at Indira Point on Great Nicobar Island — to 37°6'N in the north, where Jammu and Kashmir approaches the Karakoram. The Tropic of Cancer, at 23.5°N, runs through the middle of the country, cutting through eight states: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura, and Mizoram. That single line divides India into a tropical south and a subtropical north, and that division, more than almost anything else in the syllabus, organises what India's climate can do, what India's agriculture can grow, and where India's population can cluster. The south of the Tropic of Cancer sits in the tropics; it receives the sun directly overhead twice a year, and its rainfall regime is shaped by the equatorial convergence zones. The north sits in the subtropical belt; it experiences continental cold in winter and the extraordinary anomaly of the monsoon in summer. I will return to that anomaly in Chapter 5 — but the physical fact to hold here is that a line drawn at 23.5°N is one of the most consequential lines in Indian geography because it is a planetary line that happens to bisect the country.

The east-west extent matters too, though differently. India spans from 68°7'E in the west, near Guhar Moti in Gujarat, to 97°25'E near Kibithu in Arunachal Pradesh. That is nearly 30 degrees of longitude — almost two hours of solar time. A person in eastern Arunachal Pradesh sees the sun rise close to two hours before someone in western Gujarat. A single time zone is used regardless: the Indian Standard Meridian at 82°30'E, passing through Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh, gives India IST = UTC+5:30. The country's land boundary totals about 15,200 km. Seven countries share it — Pakistan and Afghanistan to the northwest, China, Nepal, and Bhutan to the north, Myanmar and Bangladesh to the east. Maritime neighbours include Sri Lanka across the Palk Strait, the Maldives to the southwest, and Indonesia, whose territorial waters are touched near Indira Point. Total area: 3.28 million km², the seventh-largest country in the world. Coastline: about 7,517 km. That coastal length, and what the Indian Ocean does with it, is a second layer of inheritance — but the shape of the land the ocean frames is what this chapter is about.

The previous chapter stayed with the Himalayas — the three parallel ranges (Himadri, Himachal, Shiwaliks), the folded Tethys sediments, the still-rising wall of young mountains. That wall is the first of India's six physiographic faces. What follows are the other five, which together make up the country those mountains look down upon.

The most useful thing the Himalayas did — and are still doing — is drop silt. For tens of millions of years, Himalayan rivers carving their way down from the freshly risen ranges have been carrying enormous loads of rock flour and depositing it in the trough between the mountains and the ancient peninsular plateau to the south. What those deposits produced is the Indo-Gangetic-Brahmaputra plain, the world's largest alluvial plain — roughly 7 lakh km² of flat, deep, and densely fertile land, stretching 2,400 km from west to east, 240 to 320 km wide. Around 40% of India's population lives here; it is the most intensively farmed and most densely settled alluvial system on the planet. The plain is not geologically uniform — there are three sub-basins. The Punjab plain in the northwest is the basin of the Indus and its tributaries: Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej. Between consecutive rivers lie the doabs, the interfluve tracts — the Bist Doab between Beas and Sutlej, the Bari Doab between Ravi and Beas, the Rachna Doab between Chenab and Ravi. Most of the Punjab plain fell in what became Pakistan at Partition — one reason that Partition was not merely a political act but a physical severing of a farming system that had been functioning as a unit for centuries. The Ganga plain is the largest section, running from Delhi eastward to the Bangladesh border. The Brahmaputra plain is narrower and wetter, confined between the hills of Assam and the Purvanchal in the east.

Within this plain, the sediment follows a repeating sequence as you travel south from the Himalayan foothills. First is the Bhabar — a narrow belt, 8 to 16 km wide, of coarse pebble deposits laid down by fast-flowing mountain streams as they lose velocity fanning out onto the plains. The deposit is so porous that the streams themselves disappear underground, sinking into the gravel. The Bhabar is not good farmland. South of it, those underground streams re-emerge as springs and seeps, and the ground turns marshy and waterlogged: this is the Tarai, historically dense forest and still one of the better-preserved wildlife corridors in the subcontinent. South of the Tarai, the alluvium divides by age. The Bhangar is the older deposit, sitting slightly above the active floodplain on low terraces no longer renewed by annual floods; it contains calcareous nodules called kankar and is less fertile than what lies below it. The Khadar is the newer alluvium — the active floodplain, refreshed every monsoon by the rivers in spate — and it is the most productive surface in India. The distinction between Bhangar and Khadar is not academic; it shows up in land use, in settlement elevation, in which villages flood and which do not.

South of the plain, the land rises onto a different kind of ground. The Peninsular Plateau is not a product of the Himalayas; it is the Gondwana fragment itself, the old stable piece of southern supercontinent that made its way north over 140 million years of drift. Chapter 1 introduced this: the peninsula is ancient crystalline rock — igneous and metamorphic, none of the soft folded sedimentary material that makes the Himalayan ranges. Its northern margin is defined by two rift valleys: the Narmada graben and the Tapi graben roughly parallel to it, structural trenches where the land dropped along faults. Through these corridors the Narmada and Tapi rivers flow westward to the Arabian Sea in flat-bottomed valleys flanked by the Vindhyas to the north and the Satpuras to the south. These are block mountains formed by faulting, not by folding — a different geological character from the Himalayas entirely, and they have functioned historically as a cultural and ecological divide between northern and southern India.

North of the Narmada, the plateau becomes the Central Highlands. The Aravallis — among the oldest surviving fold mountains on Earth, Precambrian relicts now eroded to a long low ridge running northeast to southwest across Rajasthan — are its northwest edge. East of the Aravallis lies the Malwa Plateau, giving way further east to Bundelkhand, Baghelkhand, and then the Chotanagpur Plateau in the northeast. The Chotanagpur Plateau is, economically, among the most significant pieces of ground in India. It sits above the country's single largest mineral belt — coal, iron ore, copper, bauxite, mica — and the reason those minerals concentrated here is exactly the reason this terrain looks the way it does: billions of years of geological stability, deep metamorphic processes, and the hydrothermal activity that ancient crystalline Gondwana rock preserves. That is Chapter 6's territory; the physiographic fact for now is simply that the Chotanagpur block is the mineral heart of the country.

South of the Narmada-Tapi line is the Deccan Plateau proper — a triangular landmass occupying most of peninsular India, higher along its western edge and tilting gently eastward. That eastward tilt is why most peninsular rivers flow toward the Bay of Bengal rather than the Arabian Sea; the drainage follows the slope. The western edge of the plateau is the Western Ghats, known in Maharashtra as the Sahyadri — a nearly continuous range running 1,600 km from the northern margins of Goa southward to the tip of the peninsula. Average elevation here is 900 to 1,600 metres, significantly higher than the eastern margin. The highest point in peninsular India is Anaimudi at 2,695 metres, in the Anaimalai Hills of Kerala, within the convergence zone where the Western Ghats bend east and meet the Eastern Ghats at the Nilgiris. The Nilgiris are the junction knot — the tri-state meeting of Kerala, Karnataka, and Tamil Nadu — where Doddabetta at 2,637 metres is the second-highest peninsular peak. The Eastern Ghats by contrast are a broken series of hills running from Odisha to Tamil Nadu, repeatedly cut through by the eastward-flowing rivers. Mahendragiri in Odisha at 1,501 metres is the highest individual peak in the Eastern Ghats; further south lie the Javadi Hills and the Shevroy Hills in Tamil Nadu. The structural asymmetry between the two systems has a geological explanation: the western edge is a fault scarp — a block that was lifted relatively suddenly along fault lines — while the eastern side is the gentler depositional slope of the same tilt. The Western Ghats rise steeply from the coast and are a wall; the Eastern Ghats are a series of hills interrupted by river valleys, not a wall at all. Western rivers are short and steep and form estuaries. Eastern rivers are long, low-gradient, and build deltas.

Spread across the Deccan Plateau's northwestern surface is a younger skin, geologically speaking: the Deccan Trap. About 66 million years ago, right at the K-Pg boundary when the dinosaurs vanished, massive fissure eruptions poured basaltic lava across what is now Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, and parts of Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh, covering roughly 500,000 km². That basalt has been weathering since. The product is regur — black cotton soil, deep, self-ploughing, moisture-retentive, excellent for cotton, sorghum, and pulses. When a farmer in Vidarbha works the soil, they are working material slowly released from volcanic rock laid down at one of the most dramatic extinction events in the planet's last quarter-billion years. The inheritance here is 66 million years old.

The two coasts are different from each other, and the difference goes back to geology. The Western Coastal Plain is narrow — 50 to 100 km — squeezed between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea. It runs in three named sections: the Konkan in the north (Maharashtra and Goa), the Karnataka coast in the centre, and the Malabar coast in the south (Kerala). The Malabar coast is known for its kayals — backwater lagoons formed behind sand bars and spits — of which Vembanad is the largest. Because the Western Ghats rise steeply as a fault scarp from the narrow coastal strip, the western rivers are short, fast, and steep; they drop their load early and reach the sea as estuaries, not deltas. The Eastern Coastal Plain is wider — 100 to 200 km — and gentler in gradient, running in two sections: the Northern Circars (Odisha and northern Andhra Pradesh) and the Coromandel coast (southern Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu). The eastern rivers — Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery — flow across the plateau tilt and arrive at the Bay of Bengal with enough sediment and low enough gradient to build large, productive deltas. The lagoons here — Chilika on the Odisha coast, Pulicat straddling the Andhra-Tamil Nadu border — are tidal lagoons behind barrier bars, different in character from the Malabar kayals. Why ports distribute differently on the two coasts — natural harbours on the west, open roadsteads on the delta-fringed east — is Chapter 11's question.

At the western margin of the country lies the Thar, also called Marusthali: roughly 200,000 km² of arid western Rajasthan, extending into Pakistan. A reasonable question is why a desert sits exactly here and not somewhere else. Two forces converge. The Aravallis run northeast to southwest, roughly parallel to the direction from which the southwest monsoon arrives — so they offer no orographic barrier, no wall to force the monsoon upward and wring out its moisture. Additionally, Rajasthan sits squarely in the subtropical high-pressure belt — the same planetary band that produces the Sahara in Africa and the deserts of Australia — where descending, dry air suppresses rainfall almost regardless of local surface conditions. Annual rainfall drops below 150 mm; wind sculpts crescent sand dunes called barchans; the Luni is the only significant river, disappearing into the Rann of Kutch. The salt lakes — Sambhar and Didwana — are remnants of larger water bodies from a wetter geological past, their salinity a record of long cycles of evaporation and aridification.

India's islands complete the picture, and they deserve to be read as two distinct geological stories. The Andaman & Nicobar Islands in the Bay of Bengal — about 572 islands in a long arc — are structurally the submarine extension of the Arakan Yoma mountain chain of Myanmar, compressed upward by the tectonic collision along the eastern margin of the Indian Ocean. They are volcanic-tectonic in origin, and Barren Island — India's only confirmed active volcano — makes that plain. Indira Point on Great Nicobar Island, India's southernmost point at 6°45'N, was partially submerged by the 2004 tsunami, a direct consequence of the tectonic energy still latent along this same arc. The Lakshadweep Islands in the Arabian Sea — 36 coral atolls with a total land area of barely 32 km² — have an entirely different origin. They are reef structures grown on the crowns of old submerged volcanic peaks, their formation biological rather than tectonic: warm shallow water, long time, patient coral polyps. Their very low elevation makes them acutely vulnerable to sea-level rise, which Chapter 12 will address.

The Himalayan rivers built the plains; the peninsular rivers carved their channels across the old plateau on their way to two different coasts. The next chapter follows the water — where it comes from, what routes it takes, and what it does to the land it passes through.
