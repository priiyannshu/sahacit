2 — The Silent City and the Singing Voice: Ancient Indus and the Vedic World

Stand, for a moment, at the edge of what archaeologists call the Lower Town of Mohenjo-Daro. The year is approximately 2300 BCE. You are standing in a city of perhaps forty thousand people, in a street running north to south in an almost perfect grid. The houses on either side are built of standardised baked bricks — each brick maintaining the same ratio of length to breadth to height that you will find again in a warehouse at Harappa in Punjab, three hundred kilometres to the northeast, and again at Lothal in Gujarat, nearly a thousand kilometres to the south. Beneath your feet runs a covered drain. It connects to a larger drain along the street. That drain connects to the main sewer of the city. No Bronze Age urban culture in the world — not Egypt, not Mesopotamia — came close to matching this.

Walk up to the Citadel, the elevated western section. You pass the Great Bath: a rectangular tank of fired brick sealed with natural tar, eleven metres long, seven metres wide, two and a half metres deep, with steps on both sides and rooms arranged around it. You pass the granary. You look for a palace. You look for a royal tomb. You look for the monumental proclamation of authority that every other contemporary civilisation announces in stone.

There is none.

Hold this as the governing question of the first half of this era: a civilisation that planned cities with extraordinary precision, that maintained standards of brick and weight across a territory larger than contemporary Egypt or Mesopotamia, that traded with the known world — and that has left us, four thousand years later, with almost no way to read the mind behind it. The Harappan script has 375 to 400 signs. More than four thousand inscribed objects have been recovered. Not one has been decoded. The Indus made everything legible — the weight of a copper tool, the route of a drain, the dimensions of a wall — and wrapped its own language in silence.

The previous era was the world of early domestication: painted pottery in the Vindhyas, megalithic burials in the Deccan, the first Neolithic settlements. This era opens with the full fruit of that transformation: the Bronze Age urban civilisation of the Harappans, followed — in a second act that barely overlaps it — by the entirely different world of the Vedic people, pastoral, hymn-singing, horse-centred, arriving from the northwest and moving over seven centuries from the Punjab into the Gangetic plain. These are the two voices of this chapter. The Harappan is silent — cities, weights, and seals, but no word we can read. The Vedic is pure voice — the oldest compositions in Sanskrit, transmitted orally for centuries, but almost no archaeology of its early world. We are told, in effect, the same story twice: once in stone and brick, without words; once in words, without stone.

The cities we cannot read

The Harappan Civilisation — in its mature phase from roughly 2600 to 1900 BCE — is by any geographic measure the largest of the three great Bronze Age urban cultures. It extended from the Makran coast to the Himalayan foothills, from eastern Afghanistan to Gujarat; scholars have identified roughly 2,800 sites, over a thousand in the mature phase alone, and the total area covered exceeds a million square kilometres. At the centre were the two great cities: Harappa in Pakistani Punjab, and Mohenjo-Daro in Sindh, literally "the mound of the dead."

Every city was built in the same grammar. A Citadel to the west, slightly elevated and walled, containing the public structures. A Lower Town to the east, larger, where most people lived in courtyard houses with private bathing areas and their own wells — Mohenjo-Daro had approximately seven hundred. What no city had was ostentation. The Harappan attitude to death was notably modest: the dead were generally buried in pits, sometimes brick-lined, with pottery and the occasional ornament. At Ropar, a dog was buried with its human companion. The finds at Harappa — wheat and barley, dice, a copper mirror, a bronze figure of a dog chasing a deer — describe a life, not a court.

Notice what is absent. The gold death-mask, the royal chariot at Ur, the grand inscription of victories. The Harappan civilisation, alone among the ancient urban cultures, appears to have organised itself without the conspicuous display of concentrated power. The steatite bust that Marshall excavated from Mohenjo-Daro — the bearded figure in an ornamental robe, eyes half-closed in apparent meditation, that Marshall called the "priest-king" — is as close as we come to a face of authority, and even that identification is entirely speculative. There is no evidence of priest-rule. There is no identifiable palace.

Among the artefacts, two stand apart. From Mohenjo-Daro's lower levels came a bronze figurine, roughly ten centimetres tall, of a young woman — naked, weighted with bangles on her left arm, right hand on hip, head tilted back in the three-bend tribhanga posture. The Dancing Girl, cast by the lost-wax technique, is the oldest known bronze sculpture in the world. And then the seals: thousands of small steatite rectangles bearing images of animals — the unicorn most common, then the humped bull, tiger, rhinoceros, elephant — and above them, those unread signs. These seals sealed goods for trade: the Harappan handshake with the wider world, legible to us only in their image, never in their words.

What the cities actually did

The Harappans were, above all, traders. They were the people whom Mesopotamian cuneiform records, from around 2350 BCE onwards, called the people of Meluha — a land supplying carnelian beads, lapis lazuli, copper, gold, and fine timber. The route ran by sea to intermediate stations at Dilmun (modern Bahrain) and Magan (modern Oman), and from there into the river-cities of Mesopotamia. Copper from Oman in Harappan artefacts carries nickel traces matching Omani sources; Harappan jars have been found at Omani sites. For the rarest materials — lapis lazuli, whose only source was a single valley in Afghanistan — the Harappans established a trading colony at Shortughai, near the best lapis deposits, complete with canals and an unmistakably Harappan settlement far into Central Asia.

The craft workshops that fed this trade were organised with the same logic. At Chanhudaro, a small town almost entirely devoted to industry, artisans bored carnelian beads with specialised drills and worked copper, shell, steatite, and faience — the last a blue-green material fired from ground quartz, prized because it was difficult to make. At Lothal, on the Gujarat coast, a dockyard facility near the Gulf of Cambay made it the subcontinent's first known site of maritime trade, where instruments for measuring angles and a bead factory served the export economy simultaneously. No metal money was used; the system ran on barter, bullock carts with solid wheels, and the authority of the standardised weights.

Those weights — cubical chert stones running in a binary sequence for lower denominations and a decimal sequence for higher — are the clearest evidence of coordinated authority across the civilisation. The base unit of sixteen still echoed in the sixteen-anna rupee India used until 1957. To maintain such standards across a million-square-kilometre territory for seven centuries requires not just a norm but an enforcement of the norm, which is to say, some form of governance. What that governance looked like, the script has not told us. The Dholavira inscription board — ten large Harappan signs carved in white stone, resembling a public notice — is the closest thing to a public communication we have, and we cannot read it.

The surprise, when you look at Harappan urbanism whole, is not the planning or the trade — it is the sustained commitment to something that looks like public infrastructure over private display. Mohenjo-Daro's drainage system, connecting every house to covered street drains with inspection holes at intervals, is a civic investment of a kind that most ancient states never bothered with. The Great Bath — whatever ritual function it served — is a public facility, not a royal bath. The granaries are not private storehouses. The Harappan civilisation seems to have answered the question of authority differently from its contemporaries.

The silence of the Sarasvati

Around 1900 BCE, the great cities begin to empty. Seals and standardised weights stop appearing. The script vanishes. Long-distance trade collapses. This is not conquest — Wheeler's Aryan invasion theory, with the skeletal remains at Mohenjo-Daro as evidence of massacre, was challenged definitively by George Dales in 1964, and the physical record does not support mass violence. It is a slow dispersal: the Late Harappan phase, from roughly 1900 to 1300 BCE, in which Harappan elements survive in Gujarat at Rangpur and Rojdi, in Haryana at Bhagwanpura, and in the east as far as Alamgirpur in western UP — the easternmost known Harappan site, where an impression of cotton cloth on a trough marks the civilisation's final reach.

Why did the cities empty? Several pressures converged. The Yamuna and Sutlej, which had fed into the Sarasvati — the river the Rigveda calls naditama, "best of rivers," identified with the Hakra-Ghaggar system — shifted their courses around 1700 BCE, depriving the settlements along the drying Sarasvati of their water. The rise of Elam on Mesopotamia's eastern flank around 2000 BCE had already disrupted the sea trade, cutting the tin supply for Harappan bronze-workers and collapsing the export market for luxury goods. Rainfall declined. Soils exhausted. The Late Harappan levels at Bhagwanpura overlap with early Painted Grey Ware — the pottery of the later Vedic period. Two worlds touch in the ground. But by the time that touch registers, the script has already gone silent.

The Vedic dawn

The second act of this era opens in a different register entirely. Between roughly 1500 and 1200 BCE, groups of pastoral, cattle-herding, Sanskrit-speaking people began moving into the subcontinent from the northwest. They came, in all probability, from the Andronovo cultural zone of the Central Asian steppe — that Bronze Age world of spoked-wheel chariots, horse sacrifice, cremation practices, and a ritual drink called soma. They passed through the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, which shows spoked-wheel chariots and partial cremation by 1500 BCE. They entered through the Northwest Frontier passes and settled first in what the Rigveda calls the Sapta Sindhu — the Land of Seven Rivers, centring on the Indus and its five Punjab tributaries, with the Sarasvati as the revered seventh.

The evidence for this Indo-Aryan migration is not inscriptional — no text from India survives from the period — but it is convergent and substantial. Genetic studies show that the male-line marker M17, present in over forty per cent of Central Asian steppe populations, appears in roughly thirty-five per cent of Hindi-speaking Indian men and only ten per cent of Dravidian-speaking ones. Linguistic links between Sanskrit and ancient Iranian, Russian, and Greek are too systematic for coincidence. The archaeological trail through Andronovo and BMAC is consistent. The migration framework is now the working consensus of scholarship.

The world these people brought with them was organised around cattle. The word for war in the Rigveda is gavishthi: literally, "the search for cows." Their primary deity was Indra — Purandara, "destroyer of fortifications," warlord and rain-bringer, praised in two hundred and fifty hymns — alongside Agni, the fire-god carrying oblations to heaven as smoke, and Soma, the ritual plant-drink. Their society was divided into five tribal groupings, of which the dominant were the Bharatas, supported by the conservative sage Vasishtha, and the Tritsu. The rivalry of these priestly lineages runs through the Rigveda's earliest books: Vishvamitra, the liberal sage of mandala III, composed the Gayatri mantra as a universal admission formula into the Aryan fold — though later priests would restrict it to the three upper varnas alone.

The decisive political event of the Rigvedic period was the Battle of Ten Kings — fought on the banks of the Ravi, called the Parushni — in which Sudas of the Bharatas defeated a coalition of ten chiefs (five Aryan, five non-Aryan). The Bharatas absorbed the defeated Purus. From this merger the Kurus would eventually form. The country's name — Bharatavarsha — descends from this tribe and this battle.

Their assemblies were three: the sabha, a smaller council; the samiti, the larger deliberative and military assembly; and the vidatha, which we hear of less and less as the era progresses. Women attended the assemblies in early Rigvedic times. This will change.

From the Sindhu to the Ganga

The Later Vedic period — from roughly 1000 to 600 BCE — is the period of eastward movement and settlement. The Kurus and Panchalas, now the dominant tribes of the upper Ganga-Yamuna doab, are the cultural core of the age. Their capital was Hastinapur, in what is now Meerut district. Their enabling technology was iron — shyama ayas, "black metal," as the later Vedic texts call it — which allowed the clearance of the Gangetic forests for settled agriculture and the forging of weapons bronze could not match. Their characteristic pottery is Painted Grey Ware: grey clay with black geometric designs, found at roughly seven hundred sites across the upper doab, used for special occasions.

Three later Vedas were composed in these centuries: the Sama Veda, which took Rigvedic prayers and set them to melody for ritual recitation; the Yajur Veda, which added prose formulae for sacrifice; and the Atharva Veda, which contains charms, spells, and a strong folk tradition the Brahmanas themselves acknowledged as non-Aryan in origin. The Brahmanas — prose texts attached to each Veda explaining ritual symbolism — were compiled in the upper Gangetic basin between 1000 and 500 BCE. The word "janapada" appears first in these texts, around 800 BCE, marking the transition from tribal identity to territorial polity: the land where a people (jana) set its foot and settled.

What was also happening in these centuries is the formalisation of social hierarchy. The Rigveda's tenth mandala — its latest addition — contains the Purusha Sukta, in which the four varnas (brahmana, kshatriya, vaishya, shudra) are described as arising from the cosmic sacrifice of the Primeval Man. By the later Vedic period, these categories carry institutional weight: the upanayana, the sacred thread ceremony, marks entry into formal Vedic education, and it is the privilege of the three upper varnas — the dvija, the twice-born — alone. Women are excluded. Shudras are excluded. The Gayatri mantra that Vishvamitra had composed as a universal formula is restricted.

Royal power is reinforced by three great rituals. The rajasuya, the coronation sacrifice, whose dakshina could theoretically run to 240,000 cows. The ashvamedha, in which a horse is set loose for a year, its unopposed path defining the territory of the king's sovereignty. The vajapeya, the chariot race in which the king's chariot is ceremonially made to win. All three are about the same thing: the translation of force into legitimacy through the priestly apparatus. Indra and Agni, the warrior-gods of the Rigvedic period, yield their pre-eminence to Prajapati, the creator-god who rises to the top of the later Vedic pantheon.

The Mahabharata war, traditionally dated around 950 BCE, was the fratricidal conclusion of the Kuru experiment. But the stories the epic tells — the obligations of kinship, the cost of dharma, the weight of pledges — became the myths through which this entire period would understand itself, and which the subcontinent has never stopped reading.

The reaction to ritual excess was already forming. In Panchala and Videha, philosopher-kings and forest-dwelling thinkers were beginning to ask whether the sacrifice, with its priestly monopoly and endless elaboration, had any connection to the question the mind cannot stop asking. The Upanishads — composed around 600 BCE, the philosophical revolt that will open the next era — are the answer to a question this era raises but cannot resolve.

What the era leaves us

This era gives us two inheritances, and they are in tension with each other. The first is the fact of the Harappan world: a Bronze Age urban culture of extraordinary planning capacity and long-distance commercial reach, which built some of the most sophisticated public infrastructure of the ancient world, and which, for reasons still debated, could not sustain itself past the second millennium BCE. It leaves no readable text, no identifiable king, no clear theology. What it leaves is the question — one of the most productive in Indian historiography: what form of authority organises a million-square-kilometre civilisation without advertising itself?

The second inheritance is the Vedic corpus: the four Vedas composed across nearly a thousand years, and the social and ritual order they codify. The Rigveda is the oldest text in any Indo-European language; its oral transmission, across three and a half millennia to the present, is one of the great acts of cultural memory. But by the time this era closes — around 600 BCE — the order the Vedas have established is already generating the revolt that the next era will name. The sixteen Mahajanapadas, crystallising from the janapada transitions of the later Vedic period in the Gangetic basin, are the political formations from which Magadha will rise. Buddhism and Jainism, the heterodoxies that emerge against the Vedic ritual order, are the children of the dissatisfaction this era creates.

In parallel, to the west, the world that the Harappans had traded with was running its own sequence. The Sumerians built Uruk and Ur and invented cuneiform; the Akkadians built the first empire; Hammurabi codified 282 laws and inscribed them on a stele for all to read. That Indus-Mesopotamia commerce — the carnelian beads going west, the tin coming east — had already collapsed before this era ends. The two worlds had connected, briefly, across the sea-lanes of the Persian Gulf, and then separated.

The script we cannot read is the era's fitting image. When you open the timeline that follows, you will see the cities and the hymns laid out side by side — the mute grid of the Harappan world and the singing world of the Vedas, almost never speaking to each other across the seventeen centuries they share. One voice in silence; one silence with a voice: that is what this era passes to the next.
