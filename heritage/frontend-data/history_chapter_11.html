11 — From Liberalisation to the Present: The Era of the Unbundling

Open on Manmohan Singh. It is 24 July 1991. He is Finance Minister of a minority government that has been in office for five weeks, a government led by a Prime Minister who was not elected party leader but chosen in a crisis, governing a country that twenty-three days earlier had pledged forty-seven tons of gold to the Bank of England to stave off a balance-of-payments default. The Parliament chamber does not yet know what is about to be said. Manmohan Singh opens his budget speech — the most consequential budget speech in India's post-independence history — with a line from Victor Hugo.

No power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come.

The idea he is announcing is that the Nehruvian settlement — the web of licences and controls and public-sector monopolies and import barriers that has governed the Indian economy for four decades — is about to be systematically unmade.

I want you to hold this image: a mild-mannered economist, in horn-rimmed spectacles and a blue turban, quoting a French novelist to announce that India is about to dismantle the institutional scaffolding that Nehru spent his life building. The previous era had reached its limit. The licence raj had thickened into a system of coordinated rent-seeking; the public-sector undertakings that were supposed to build the commanding heights of the economy were running losses underwritten by the treasury; the foreign exchange reserves had sunk, by June 1991, to less than three weeks' worth of imports. India did not transform in 1991 because reformers finally won an internal argument. It transformed because the crisis left no room for postponement.

What 1991 began was, in my view, best understood as an unbundling. The Nehruvian state had bundled together a particular economic model, a particular welfare architecture, a particular foreign policy posture, and a particular understanding of who the republic was for. The era we are now entering — the last and, in the literal sense, most contemporary of our eleven eras — is the story of that unbundling, and of what was re-bundled in its place. Not everything unbundled. The republic's constitutional commitments survived; the Supreme Court in these decades will do more to give them practical meaning than any parliament. A new welfare state emerged — different in architecture, delivered differently — alongside the liberalised economy, not instead of it. The political landscape, freed from Congress hegemony, sorted itself into a two-pole system that the era of planning had suppressed. The unbundling is the era's central move; but what it produced, thirty-five years on, is more layered than a simple story of liberalisation permits.

1991: the year that could not be refused

The immediate mechanics of the 1991 transformation were austere. The rupee was devalued by 18-19 percent in two tranches in July. Industrial licensing was abolished for most sectors — in the Industrial Policy Statement of 24 July 1991, the exempt list shrank from hundreds of industries to eighteen, and later to six. The MRTP Act, which had restrained large industrial houses from expanding beyond an asset threshold, lost its restrictive provision; foreign direct investment was permitted at 51 percent in thirty-four priority industries without prior approval. The public sector's exclusive domain was reduced to a handful of strategic sectors. SEBI was given statutory powers under the SEBI Act 1992. The IMF's loan of approximately seven billion dollars came with conditionalities; India met them, and within two years had stabilised its foreign exchange position and returned to growth. The crisis that seemed, in the spring of 1991, to threaten the republic's economic foundations turned out to be the occasion on which the republic's economy was refounded.

But 1991 is not only an economic story. Two other events of the early Rao years press equally on the era's meaning. The first is Mandal. V.P. Singh's government had, in August 1990, announced implementation of the Mandal Commission's recommendations — a 27 percent reservation for Other Backward Classes in central government jobs. The announcement had triggered a wave of upper-caste protests and, alongside the rath yatra led by L.K. Advani from Somnath toward Ayodhya, brought down the V.P. Singh government. The 1992 Indra Sawhney judgment by a nine-judge Supreme Court bench settled the legal question — it upheld the 27 percent OBC reservation, capped total reservations at fifty percent, and introduced the creamy layer exclusion — but it could not settle the political one. Caste arithmetic would reorganise Indian electoral politics through the 1990s, producing the BSP and the SP as forces that the old Congress social coalition could not contain.

The second event is the Babri Masjid demolition of 6 December 1992. Kar sevaks of the VHP and Bajrang Dal pulled down the sixteenth-century mosque in Ayodhya — built under Babur's commander Mir Baqi, disputed since the late nineteenth century — in five hours. The BJP Chief Minister of Uttar Pradesh had given assurances to the Supreme Court that the mosque would be protected; he resigned the moment the demolition was complete. Riots swept India; approximately two thousand people were killed in the weeks that followed. In Bombay, in March 1993, thirteen bombs in a single day killed over two hundred and fifty people — retaliation organised by Dawood Ibrahim's network. The demolition ended one political era and opened another. The Congress model of managing religious communities through elite accommodation died in Ayodhya that afternoon. Hindu nationalist politics, previously confined to the margins, entered the mainstream as a governing project.

The Rao government also passed, in April 1993, the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments — giving constitutional status to panchayats and municipalities, mandating regular elections to local bodies, reserving one-third of seats for women, and creating State Election Commissions to run local-body polls. The amendments did not, by themselves, transfer meaningful financial powers to local bodies — that remained a deferred project across every subsequent government — but they laid the legal floor on which a genuine third tier of government could eventually stand.

The 1990s that followed were years of coalition arithmetic. The 1996 general election produced a fractured mandate — no single party close to majority. The BJP, the single largest party, formed a government under Vajpayee that lasted thirteen days before proving it lacked the numbers. The United Front coalition — an improbable assembly of regional parties — then governed for two years across two prime ministers, with Congress supporting from outside. WTO accession in January 1995, under GATT's Uruguay Round, tied India into the rules-based global trading system in ways that would compound over the following decades. The decade's instability produced, by its end, a clarification: the two poles of Indian politics — Congress and BJP — were becoming the anchors around which the coalition era would be organised.

The nuclear hinge and the BJP's arrival

In March 1998, after a third general election in three years, the BJP formed the National Democratic Alliance — a coalition of twenty-four parties. Atal Bihari Vajpayee became Prime Minister. Six weeks into the government, in May 1998, India conducted five nuclear tests at Pokhran — Operation Shakti. The tests declared India a nuclear weapons state; the world responded with sanctions; Dr A.P.J. Abdul Kalam and R. Chidambaram had led the scientific team. Pakistan tested within weeks. The sanctions and diplomatic costs were real; so was the strategic signal — that the republic, fifty years old, intended to claim its place in the first tier of the world's power architecture.

The year 1999 contained both a war and a diplomacy. The Lahore Declaration of February — Vajpayee's bus journey across the Wagah border to meet Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif — was the most ambitious peace gesture between India and Pakistan since Shimla. Three months later, Pakistani forces and militants had occupied heights across the Line of Control in the Kargil sector. Operation Vijay ran from May to July; India recaptured Tololing and Tiger Hill. Five hundred and twenty-seven Indian soldiers were killed. The war was fought, for the first time since 1971, between two nuclear-armed states; it ended with Pakistan's troops withdrawn, Nawaz Sharif's government soon toppled by a military coup, and India's argument vindicated. Kargil is the hinge on which the nuclear era's first strategic test turned; it also made clear that nuclear deterrence does not preclude limited war.

The Vajpayee years from 1999 to 2004 produced genuine infrastructure in the literal sense. The Golden Quadrilateral — the highway network connecting Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata — was Vajpayee's most visible legacy; so was the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana, which extended rural road connectivity at a scale no previous government had attempted. The New Telecom Policy of 1999 opened the mobile telephony market; the IT-services wave that would make Bangalore and Hyderabad globally synonymous with software had begun in the Rao years but accelerated sharply through the decade. This was the era's first great wave of growth, running above the agrarian and industrial India that remained below. Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan (2001) made universalisation of elementary education a time-bound national mission; the 86th Constitutional Amendment of 2002 made free and compulsory education a fundamental right — the provision that would eventually become the RTE Act of 2009. Three new states — Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand — were created from existing ones in November 2000.

The Parliament attack of 13 December 2001 — five gunmen breaching the perimeter of the Lok Sabha complex in a suicide attack that killed nine security personnel — triggered Operation Parakram, the largest mobilisation of Indian forces along the international border since 1971. Troops were massed at the border for nearly a year; the two countries came as close to full-scale war as they had since Kargil. The diplomatic and military standoff was eventually stood down without a military strike, but the episode accelerated the reorientation of Indian counterterrorism law and intelligence architecture, leading among other things to the strengthening of legislative provisions under POTA and, after 26/11, to the establishment of the National Investigation Agency in 2008.

Then Gujarat, in February and March 2002. The burning of a coach of the Sabarmati Express at Godhra — fifty-eight killed — triggered retaliatory communal violence across Gujarat that continued for weeks; the official toll was over a thousand dead. The Chief Minister was Narendra Modi, in office for five months. Gujarat 2002 became the single most contested political fact of the era — the SIT investigation, the Zakia Jafri petition, the eventual judicial proceedings — and I will not pretend to you that the legal argument settled the political one. What I can tell you is that this event's long shadow extends over every subsequent political calculation in the era.

The 2004 election — the India Shining campaign, the NDA's confident expectation of re-election — produced one of the era's most durable surprises. The Congress-led UPA won; Sonia Gandhi declined the prime ministership; Manmohan Singh was sworn in on 22 May 2004. The architect of 1991 returned, now as Prime Minister.

The UPA decade: rights as legislation

The UPA era from 2004 to 2014 is the era in which the welfare state was rebuilt — not the Nehruvian model, not public-sector delivery of goods, but rights-based entitlements backed by statute. The National Advisory Council, chaired by Sonia Gandhi, served as the intellectual engine of this legislative surge. The Right to Information Act, in force from July 2005, gave any citizen the right to demand, from any public authority, information about the exercise of public power within thirty days. The RTI was not a technocratic reform; it was a structural change in the relationship between citizen and state — the largest such change since the fundamental rights chapter of the Constitution itself.

Simultaneously, the National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (NREGA, 2005) guaranteed one hundred days of paid work per year to rural households on demand, with an unemployment allowance payable if work was not provided within fifteen days. The Forest Rights Act of 2006 recognised the rights of forest-dwelling communities over land they had occupied for generations but never held legal title to. The Right to Education Act of 2009 made schooling compulsory and free for children aged six to fourteen. Each statute was a legal floor: the distance between the floor and the ceiling was filled, unevenly, by implementation. Together they constitute the most ambitious rights-based legislative programme in any single decade of the republic.

The Indo-US Civilian Nuclear Agreement of 2008 was the other transforming move of UPA-I. India's 1974 test had placed it outside the Non-Proliferation Treaty; the subsequent decades of technology denial had restricted access to civilian nuclear materials and equipment. The 123 Agreement provided for full civilian nuclear cooperation while keeping India's military programme outside safeguards. The political price was severe: the Left parties withdrew support in July 2008, triggering a trust vote the government won narrowly. Manmohan Singh told Parliament he was staking his government on it; the NSG waiver in September 2008 was its international ratification.

Then, on 26 November 2008, ten LeT gunmen arrived by sea at Mumbai's shores and spread across twelve locations across the city for three days — the Taj Hotel, the Trident-Oberoi, the Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus, Nariman House. One hundred and sixty-six people were killed. The 26/11 attacks were the most sophisticated terrorist operation India had ever faced; the composite dialogue with Pakistan was suspended and never fully restored.

UPA-II (2009-14) began with a larger mandate and ended in a legitimacy crisis. The Comptroller and Auditor General's reports on 2G spectrum allocation and coal block allocations — the presumptive loss figures, disputed in their methodology but not in the underlying governance failure they described — triggered a parliamentary crisis and a Supreme Court that cancelled 122 telecom licences. The anti-corruption agitation of 2011, centred on Anna Hazare's hunger strikes at Jantar Mantar and Ramlila Maidan, was the era's largest urban mobilisation since the Emergency opposition of 1975-77. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act of 2013 was its legislative product; Arvind Kejriwal's departure from the movement to found the Aam Aadmi Party was its unintended one.

In December 2012, a young woman was gang-raped on a Delhi bus and died of her injuries thirteen days later. The Nirbhaya protests — tens of thousands in the streets, water cannons at India Gate — produced the Justice Verma Committee and, from its report of January 2013, the Criminal Law Amendment Act that widened the definition of rape, added new offences, and tightened sentencing. The Nirbhaya case is the era's clearest demonstration of the mechanism by which mass public outrage translates into legislative change. The National Food Security Act 2013 — covering two-thirds of India's population with a legal entitlement to subsidised grain — and the Direct Benefit Transfer system (2013), routing welfare payments directly into beneficiaries' bank accounts, completed the legislative arc of the UPA decade. Both the rights-based surge and the delivery architecture it built were legacies that the subsequent government would inherit, contest, and in some respects extend.

The court that never stopped arguing

I want to give one section to the constitutional jurisprudence of these decades, because it is the era's least narrated achievement. The Supreme Court in the post-1991 period produced, case by case, the most consequential body of rights jurisprudence in Indian history since the constitution was adopted — doing so against the grain of legislative inertia, sometimes against the express preference of the executive, in a form that compounds on itself. The constitutional present continuous is the era's defining metaphor for a reason: the cases keep arriving, the jurisprudence never settles, and the republic's arguments about what it is and who it is for are conducted, in the first instance, in the form of litigation.

Begin with Indra Sawhney (1992): a nine-judge bench settled the basic framework of reservations — the fifty percent cap, the creamy layer exclusion, no reservations in promotions. Move to S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): another nine-judge bench, this time on Article 356. The court held that President's Rule is subject to judicial review, that the floor test in the legislature is the proper mechanism for determining majority, and that secularism and federalism are basic features of the constitution that the power to impose President's Rule cannot override. The judgment ended the weaponisation of Article 356 as a political instrument.

Then Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): the court, in the absence of any legislation against workplace sexual harassment, laid down binding guidelines derived directly from Articles 14, 19 and 21 and from international conventions. The Vishaka Guidelines functioned as statute for sixteen years before Parliament passed the POSH Act in 2013. In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), a nine-judge bench reaffirmed that any law placed in the Ninth Schedule — however old the Schedule — remains open to constitutional challenge if it violates fundamental rights that are part of the basic structure. NALSA v. Union of India (2014) recognised transgender persons as a third gender entitled to constitutional protection and directed the state to create welfare measures. Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) struck down Section 66A of the IT Act — the provision used to arrest citizens for social media posts — as unconstitutional on the ground that its chilling effect on free expression was fatal.

Then, in August 2017, a nine-judge bench delivered Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, the Right to Privacy judgment. The court unanimously held that privacy is a fundamental right inherent in Articles 14, 19 and 21, overruling two earlier judgments from 1954 and 1962. It established a three-part test — legality, legitimate state aim, proportionality — for any state intrusion into privacy. The judgment immediately became the foundation on which three subsequent landmarks were built.

Privacy includes at its core the preservation of personal intimacies, the sanctity of family life, marriage, procreation, the home and sexual orientation. Privacy also connotes a right to be left alone. — Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)

In Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), the court unanimously decriminalised consensual same-sex relations between adults, overruling the retrograde 2013 Suresh Kumar Koushal judgment that had upheld Section 377. In Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018), the court unanimously struck down adultery as a criminal offence, holding that a provision treating a married woman's sexual autonomy as property of her husband violated Articles 14, 15 and 21. The Sabarimala judgment the same year opened the temple to women of all ages, though its implementation remained legally contested.

The NJAC case — Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015) — struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment that had replaced the collegium system with a National Judicial Appointments Commission. The court held that the NJAC would have impermissibly inserted the executive into the judicial appointments process, violating judicial independence as a basic feature. The judgment was contested as the court appearing to protect its own prerogative; the collegium's opacity and its own accountability deficit remained unresolved. But the NJAC case is, in constitutional terms, one of the era's clearest statements of what the basic structure doctrine actually does: it limits Parliament's power to amend even institutions of constitutional governance.

The Ayodhya verdict of 9 November 2019 — a unanimous five-judge bench — awarded the disputed 2.77-acre site to the Ram Lalla deity for construction of a temple and directed the government to provide an alternate five-acre plot for a mosque. The court held that the demolition of 1992 was illegal, that the Hindu side's possession after 1949 was also unlawful, and yet awarded the site on the balance of possessory title — a judgment carefully written to separate legal from historical questions, and equally carefully read by those on every side of the dispute. In December 2023, a five-judge bench upheld the abrogation of Article 370. In February 2024, the court struck down the Electoral Bonds scheme unanimously — the scheme that had allowed anonymous corporate donations to political parties totalling Rs 16,518 crore — holding that it violated the voter's right to know the sources of political funding. The cases keep coming.

Modi-I: the great re-bundling

Narendra Modi's election in May 2014 — the BJP's first single-party majority since Rajiv Gandhi's landslide of 1984, 282 seats alone — marked a decisive shift in who held the republic's centre of gravity. Modi-I (2014-19) is characterised by a cluster of flagship schemes whose institutional architecture is worth naming precisely, because they constitute a new model of state-to-citizen delivery built on the digital infrastructure that the Aadhaar project had been assembling since 2009.

Jan Dhan Yojana, launched August 2014, aimed at universal banking access for the unbanked: over four hundred million zero-balance accounts opened, linked to direct benefit transfer. The JAM trinity — Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, Mobile — was the technical spine of a welfare model that bypassed intermediaries and deposited entitlements directly into the beneficiary's account. This was the second great wave of the unbundling: where the first wave (1991-2004) had dismantled the licence raj, the second rebuilt the state's delivery mechanism on digital rails. Swachh Bharat Mission (October 2014) built over one hundred million toilets; Ujjwala Yojana (2016) provided LPG connections to below-poverty-line households; Ayushman Bharat (2018) created the world's largest health insurance scheme by coverage, offering up to five lakh rupees per family per year for hospitalisation to the bottom forty percent of the population.

The monetary architecture also shifted. The Monetary Policy Committee, established by amendment to the RBI Act in 2016, transferred the primary mandate of inflation targeting — a Consumer Price Index target of four percent within a two-percentage-point band — from the Governor's discretion to a statutory six-member committee. The Asset Quality Review of 2015 forced banks to declare the NPAs they had been evergreening; the resulting crisis — the largest non-performing asset crisis in Indian banking history — required the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) as the resolution mechanism and, eventually, a series of PSB mergers. The IBC created a time-bound insolvency resolution process that India had never had; its operational track record through the following years was uneven but its institutional significance was undisputed.

In September 2016, India conducted surgical strikes across the Line of Control in the aftermath of the Uri terrorist attack. In February 2019, following the Pulwama bombing that killed forty CRPF personnel, India struck across the LoC again at Balakot; the aerial confrontation the following day resulted in Wing Commander Abhinandan Varthaman's capture and return. Both episodes were, in their domestic political context, assertions of a willingness to use kinetic force beyond the LoC that no previous government had publicly claimed.

Then November 2016: the demonetisation announcement. At eight in the evening of 8 November, Modi addressed the nation and declared that five-hundred and one-thousand-rupee notes — eighty-six percent of currency by value — would cease to be legal tender from midnight. The ostensible objectives were to curb black money, counterfeit currency, and terror financing, and to push the economy toward digital transactions. The immediate aftermath was queues at bank ATMs, a reported cash crisis, a GDP contraction in the quarter of implementation. Ninety-nine point three percent of demonetised notes returned to the banking system, a figure that became the centre of the debate over whether black money had been caught. The Supreme Court upheld the decision 4-1 in January 2023. The academic debate over its macroeconomic effects has not settled; I note this without judgement and trust you to read the evidence with the same care.

GST — the Goods and Services Tax, launched at a midnight Parliament session on 1 July 2017 — was the era's most durably consequential institutional reform after the 1991 restructuring. The 101st Constitutional Amendment subsumed seventeen central and state indirect taxes into a single structure (four slabs: 0, 5, 12, 18, 28 percent plus cess) creating a unified national market. The GST Council — a federal body in which every state and the Centre have a seat, decisions by consensus or weighted majority — became, in this same period, the most consequential practising instance of cooperative federalism in the republic's institutional history. The political achievement of bringing every state to the table, including opposition-governed states, and holding them there, was genuine.

370, CAA, and the year the world stopped

Modi's re-election in May 2019 — 303 seats for the BJP alone, an enlarged majority — set the scene for the most compressed sequence of structural changes in modern Indian political history. On 5 August 2019, the President issued orders under Article 370 revoking the special status of Jammu and Kashmir; the J&K Reorganisation Act 2019 bifurcated the state into two Union Territories — Jammu and Kashmir with a legislature, and Ladakh without one. The Valley was placed under a communications blackout for weeks; political leaders across parties were detained. The legal challenge reached the Supreme Court; a five-judge bench upheld the abrogation in December 2023. The political argument — about federal procedure, about the power to bifurcate a state without its own legislature's consent, about what the move means for the republic's constitutional architecture — was not settled by the court's verdict; it was relocated from the legal arena back to the political one.

Four months later, Parliament passed the Citizenship Amendment Act, which created a fast-track to Indian citizenship for persecuted religious minorities — Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, Christians — from Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan, specifically excluding Muslims. The protests that followed were the era's most sustained act of public civil disobedience: centred on Shaheen Bagh in Delhi, where a women-led sit-in ran for over one hundred days, and spreading across university campuses and city streets. The protests were dispersed by the COVID lockdown of March 2020.

The COVID-19 lockdown of 25 March 2020 — announced with four hours' notice to 1.4 billion people — was the most extreme exercise of state power over everyday life in the republic's history. For twenty-one days (extended in phases to the end of May), India was under a complete lockdown; GDP contracted 6.6 percent in FY21, the first annual contraction since 1979. The migrant worker crisis that followed the announcement — hundreds of thousands of workers walking hundreds of kilometres home, stranded at state borders, carrying their children — became the era's most searing image of the gap between policy and its human consequences. The Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana, announced days into the lockdown, provided free food grains to eighty crore beneficiaries; the vaccination drive, begun on 16 January 2021 with Covaxin and Covishield, would eventually deliver over 2.2 billion doses — the world's largest vaccination programme in absolute terms. The second wave of April-May 2021, driven by the Delta variant, produced an oxygen-shortage crisis in hospitals across the country whose excess mortality toll — disputed in methodology but not in its scale — was the sharpest domestic public health failure of the Modi years.

The Galwan Valley clash of 15-16 June 2020 — the first India-China border deaths since 1975, twenty Indian soldiers killed in a confrontation in eastern Ladakh — permanently altered the India-China relationship. The disengagement negotiations, running across twenty-one rounds of Corps Commander talks, continued for years. The strategic pivot that Galwan accelerated — toward the Quad, toward greater self-reliance in defence technology, toward a sharper separation of economic from security relationships with China — is the foreign policy thread that runs most decisively into the present.

The three farm laws of September 2020 aimed to liberalise agricultural markets, allow contract farming, and remove storage limits on agricultural produce. The year-long protest by farmers camped on Delhi's borders from November 2020 was the longest sustained protest movement of the era, and one of the most extraordinary in the republic's history: disciplined, peacefully maintained, operating its own infrastructure of langar kitchens and legal cells and media units.

Today I want to tell the nation that we have decided to repeal all three farm laws... To the farmers who went on the path of agitation, I want to appeal to them: go home. — Prime Minister Narendra Modi, 19 November 2021

The movement had won. Notice what the farm laws episode demonstrates: that a government holding a legislative supermajority, with control of both Houses of Parliament, can still be turned back by a disciplined, sustained, non-violent protest. The republic's capacity for popular sovereignty is not exhausted by the electoral cycle. The Ram Mandir consecration on 22 January 2024 — the prana pratishtha ceremony on the Ayodhya site, attended by Modi, marking the culmination of a legal and political process that had run since the demolition of 1992 — was, four months before a general election, a political moment whose significance readers across the spectrum interpreted differently. In September 2023, Parliament passed the 106th Constitutional Amendment — the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, reserving one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies for women. Implementation was tied to the delimitation exercise following the next census, deferring its actual effect by a decade or more. In the same session, Parliament also passed the three new criminal codes — the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam — replacing the Indian Penal Code of 1860, the Code of Criminal Procedure of 1973, and the Indian Evidence Act of 1872. The renaming and recodification were, in their proponents' argument, a decolonisation of the criminal law; in their critics', a reorganisation that preserved most of the colonial content while introducing procedural changes whose implementation remained to be tested.

The 2024 general election returned the NDA to power but with the BJP at 240 seats — thirteen below the single-party majority threshold — making the coalition once again structurally necessary. Modi was sworn in for a third consecutive term.

What the era leaves us — and what is still being written

Two structural threads run through everything else in this era that I want to name before we close, because they operate below the political chronology and outlast any government.

The first is the digital-public-infrastructure wave — the era's second great wave of the unbundling, running from roughly 2014 to the present. Aadhaar, seeded from 2009 and made statutory by the Aadhaar Act 2016, enrolled over 1.3 billion Indians in a biometric identity database — and was restricted by the Supreme Court in 2018 to welfare-entitlement use, preventing its weaponisation into a universal surveillance instrument. UPI, launched by the National Payments Corporation of India in April 2016, became by 2024 the world's largest real-time payment system: fourteen billion transactions per month, a volume exceeding Visa and Mastercard globally. India Stack — Aadhaar, UPI, DigiLocker, the Account Aggregator framework — is the era's most globally observed architectural innovation: a new bundling of identity and financial access inside digital infrastructure, replacing the old bundling of economic activity inside government licences. Whether its benefits reach the last mile, and on what terms, is the implementation question that remains open.

The second thread is the space and science decade. Chandrayaan-1 (2008) confirmed the presence of water-ice on the lunar surface. Mangalyaan (2013-14) made India the first country to succeed in a Mars mission on its first attempt, and the first Asian nation to reach Mars. Chandrayaan-3's Vikram lander soft-landed near the Moon's south pole on 23 August 2023 — the first country to achieve a south-pole landing, and the fourth to soft-land on the Moon at all; National Space Day is now observed on that date. Aditya-L1, launched weeks later, reached the L1 Lagrange point for solar observation. These achievements represent decades of institutional investment in ISRO — a scientific public-sector model that the liberalisation era did not dismantle, even as it dismantled many others. India's G20 presidency in 2023 — the Delhi Declaration signed by consensus including Russia and China — placed the republic at the centre of global multilateral diplomacy in a way it had not been since the Non-Aligned era.

In the world running parallel to India's internal story, the end of the Cold War — the Berlin Wall fell in November 1989, the USSR dissolved in December 1991 — reset the strategic environment into which the 1991 reforms were born. India's foreign policy recalibrated from Non-Alignment toward what came to be called strategic autonomy: relationships with multiple power centres — Russia, the United States, China, the Gulf — without binding to any. The 9/11 attacks of 2001 and the Global War on Terror reshaped South Asian geopolitics and put Pakistan at the centre of American strategy in ways that complicated India's security calculations. The 2008 Global Financial Crisis hit India at the moment of the nuclear deal's ratification; India's economy, less integrated into global shadow-banking, weathered it better than most and deployed a fiscal stimulus that kept growth above six percent. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, and India's studied neutrality in the resulting international alignment — abstaining at the United Nations, continuing to buy Russian oil at discounted rates, maintaining diplomatic channels with both Moscow and Kyiv — demonstrated strategic autonomy in its most stress-tested form. The Israel-Hamas war of October 2023 placed India in a similar position, and its diplomatic calibration was watched as a test of whether strategic autonomy could be maintained across simultaneous crises. On climate, the trajectory is worth naming: the Earth Summit at Rio in 1992 and the UNFCCC established the framework; the Kyoto Protocol committed developed nations to binding cuts that India was exempted from; the Paris Agreement of 2015 — to which India submitted an INDC committing to 40 percent renewable capacity and a 33-35 percent reduction in GDP emissions intensity by 2030 — marked India's first binding climate commitment under the nationally-determined framework. At COP26 (Glasgow, 2021), Modi announced the Panchamrit: 500 GW of non-fossil energy by 2030, 50 percent power from renewables, net zero by 2070. The International Solar Alliance, launched in 2015 at Paris, was India's own diplomatic initiative in the climate multilateral — a coalition of solar-rich nations that India seeded and leads.

What does this era leave us? It leaves a liberalised economy that is the world's fifth largest and growing, but whose gains have been distributed with profound inequality. It leaves a digital public infrastructure without measurable parallel. It leaves a constitutional jurisprudence — from Bommai to Puttaswamy to Navtej Johar to the Electoral Bonds verdict — that has expanded the practical ambit of fundamental rights more than any legislature of the period. It leaves a BJP that has displaced Congress as the natural party of national governance, and a question about the republic's federal and minority-rights architecture that the political and legal arguments of the 2020s have sharpened but not resolved. It leaves a welfare state rebuilt on rights-based entitlements and digital delivery — different in architecture from Nehru's, but larger in reach. It leaves a space programme that has landed on the Moon's south pole. And it leaves the farm laws episode as a reminder that a government with a legislative supermajority can still be turned back by organised, disciplined, non-violent dissent.

The threads that remain in motion are not small ones. The constitutional balance between centre and states — tested by the Article 370 abrogation, by the controversies around Governors in opposition-governed states, by the disputes over GST revenue sharing — is genuinely unsettled. The relationship between the republic's formally secular constitution and the Hindu nationalist cultural politics that now governs from the centre is the deepest political question of the era; I will not pretend to resolve it here, because neither the legal nor the political argument has reached a terminus. The economic questions — of employment, of agrarian distress, of the digital divide, of climate adaptation and India's Panchamrit commitments under the Paris Agreement — are structural, not cyclical.

This era is, uniquely among the eleven, still being written. No future chapter will put it in context, because we are living it. The Constitution's debates are not over; the court's docket is full; the election cycle has not paused. When you open the timeline that follows — the contemporary spread laid out card by card across thirty stages, from the LPG architecture of 1991 to the present — you will see how densely the cards cluster in the last decade, how many threads run simultaneously in the final stretch of the x-axis. That density is not an artifact of the database. It is the character of the present: more moving at once, in more domains, with fewer settled outcomes than any earlier era. The cards show the trees. The era itself is still in the forest, and the forest is still growing.
