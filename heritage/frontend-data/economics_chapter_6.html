6 — Where does the government's money come from and where does it go?

Every February, something happens in Parliament that the country watches with partial attention. The Finance Minister stands before the Lok Sabha and reads, for two hours or sometimes longer, a speech that mixes economic philosophy with specific figures — how much the government expects to collect, how much it plans to spend, and by how much it anticipates missing the gap. Television will carry every word; the individual provisions will be dissected for a week and then replaced by other news. What very few people find strange is not what is in the budget. It is that this document — the government's annual account of its income and outgoings — must be placed before Parliament for approval at all.

I want to linger on that strangeness, because it contains the oldest inheritance this chapter is about. The idea that a government cannot tax or spend without legislative consent is not a neutral administrative arrangement. It was fought for, in England, over most of the seventeenth century, and settled definitively in the Bill of Rights of 1689: the Crown could not levy taxes without Parliament's consent, and the supply of money to the Crown required annual renewal. The constitutional principle was simple and radical. The executive proposes; the legislature approves. No money can leave the public treasury without Parliament's sanction. This single principle — the legislative control of the purse — was the idea the Indian framers inherited most directly, through the Government of India Act 1935 and then formalised in Article 112 of the Constitution, which requires the President to lay before Parliament what it calls the Annual Financial Statement. The Union Budget is, in its deepest layer, a constitutional document — a proof that the executive is accountable for what it does with money that is not its own.

The Indians who built the budget's architecture were building on an older apparatus than most people realise. India's audit tradition goes back to 1860, when the Indian Audit Act created the office that would become the Comptroller and Auditor General — the officer who tells Parliament, after each year is done, whether the government spent what it was voted to spend. The tradition of the Finance Minister reading the budget speech in Parliament goes back to John Mathai, who delivered the first Union Budget of independent India in 1948. The format of the accounts — the specific categories that show up in every budget document, receipts and expenditure, revenue and capital — descends directly from British accounting conventions that the colonial government had standardised across the subcontinent. The budget is older than the republic; what the republic did was make it answerable to elected representatives rather than to a colonial administration answerable to London.

All the money the government collects flows into three constitutional pools. The Consolidated Fund of India, established under Article 266, is the main account — all revenues, all loans raised, and all repayments of loans flow into it, and nothing can be withdrawn from it without Parliament's authorisation. This is the fund over which Parliament's power of the purse is most absolute. Alongside it sits the Contingency Fund, held at the disposal of the President for genuinely unforeseen emergencies — Parliament replenishes it after the fact. And there is the Public Account, which holds money the government handles on behalf of others: provident fund deposits, small savings, court deposits. The Public Account is not the government's money in any meaningful sense; it flows in and must flow back out, and Parliament does not vote on it. These three pools matter less as facts to memorise than as a reflection of a principle: the government acts as trustee, and Parliament is the mechanism by which the trust is enforced.

Now the receipts — where the money actually comes from.

Revenue receipts are the recurring inflows that neither create any liability nor reduce any asset. They split into two kinds. Tax revenue covers what the government collects in direct and indirect taxes — income tax, corporation tax, customs, and the Centre's share of the Goods and Services Tax. Non-tax revenue covers the government's earnings from its other activities: interest on loans extended to states, dividends from public sector undertakings, fees, fines, and grants. The defining characteristic of revenue receipts is that they arrive every year without the government giving up something permanent or promising to return anything.

Capital receipts are different in structure. They either create a liability — meaning the government will owe something back — or they reduce the government's asset base. The largest source by far is borrowings: money raised from the market through government securities, from small savings instruments, and from external sources. A second source is the recovery of loans the government has previously extended to states and public enterprises. And a third is disinvestment: the proceeds from selling or diluting the government's stake in public sector undertakings. Disinvestment proceeds are capital receipts because they reduce a government asset, the equity stake, rather than generating fresh income.

Here is the structural fact that shapes everything downstream. India's tax-to-GDP ratio is roughly 17 per cent — well below the OECD average of around 34 per cent and low even by comparison with peer emerging economies. That 17 per cent is not just a figure for an examiner. It is the constraint within which every government since 1947 has operated. Every large spending ambition — on health, on infrastructure, on poverty transfers — runs into the same arithmetic: the formal tax base is narrow, the informal sector is large, and raising revenue means taxing an economy that is still overwhelmingly composed of enterprises and individuals below any practical taxation threshold. The reasons are structural: a large informal sector, agricultural income exempted by historical convention, and a corporate tax rate that was cut sharply in 2019 to attract investment. The revenue constraint is the government's most persistent problem, and the chapters that follow — on taxation, on agriculture — each circle back to it.

On the expenditure side, the same inherited distinction applies. Revenue expenditure is what the government spends in a way that neither creates an asset nor reduces a liability. Salaries, pensions, interest payments, subsidies, maintenance grants to states — these are all revenue expenditure. They are the consumption of the present. Capital expenditure creates assets or reduces liabilities: spending on roads, bridges, hospitals, defence equipment, or the repayment of a loan. The revenue-capital distinction in Indian government accounting traces directly to British practice — the same logic a business uses when it separates running costs from investment spending. The political significance is large. Revenue expenditure tends to grow because of commitments already made — the salary bill grows with recruitment, the pension bill with an ageing civil service, the interest bill with accumulated borrowing. Capital expenditure is the more discretionary of the two, and under fiscal pressure it is historically the first to be cut. This produces a specific pathology: governments under stress protect revenue commitments and trim the investment that builds future capacity.

The single largest item of revenue expenditure is interest payments on past debt. Interest consumes roughly 20 to 24 per cent of the Centre's total revenue receipts in any recent year — meaning that before a single school is built or a single subsidy is paid, one rupee in every four or five that comes in has already been committed to servicing the legacy of past borrowing. This is what economists mean by fiscal space: the room between what comes in and what is already committed. A government with a large interest burden and a narrow tax base has almost no room to make new choices.

From these four categories — revenue receipts, capital receipts, revenue expenditure, capital expenditure — three deficit measures are computed, and they answer three different questions.

Revenue deficit is the gap between revenue receipts and revenue expenditure. It asks: is the government's regular income sufficient to meet its regular commitments, or is it borrowing to pay for consumption? A revenue deficit means the government is financing current spending with future obligations — borrowing to pay salaries, interest, and subsidies rather than to build anything. The British accounting tradition considered this fiscally imprudent, and the intuition is sound: a household that borrows each month to cover groceries is in a different condition from one that borrows to buy a house.

Fiscal deficit is total expenditure minus total receipts excluding borrowings. It measures the total borrowing requirement of the government — how much it must borrow this year to meet all its commitments. Fiscal deficit is the most-cited of the three because it captures the total addition to government debt in a single year and is the number that credit-rating agencies, bond markets, and international institutions use to assess fiscal sustainability.

Primary deficit is the fiscal deficit minus interest payments on past debt. It asks: setting aside the burden we inherited from earlier decisions, are we currently managing our finances? A zero primary deficit means the government is borrowing only to service what it already owes — it is not accumulating new commitments on top of old ones. A positive primary deficit means that even the current year's operations, stripped of historical debt service, require fresh borrowing. Primary deficit is the cleanest signal of the government's current fiscal behaviour, once the past has been quarantined.

Each deficit tells a story the others miss. A government could have a large fiscal deficit but a small revenue deficit — meaning it is borrowing heavily to build infrastructure rather than to pay salaries, which is considered more defensible. A government could have a manageable fiscal deficit but a large revenue deficit — meaning the total borrowing looks contained only because disinvestment proceeds are masking consumption-financed by debt. A government with a declining primary deficit is at least making structural progress even if the fiscal deficit remains elevated. The reader who holds all three in mind simultaneously is reading the budget more carefully than most commentators do.

The central government shall take appropriate measures to reduce the fiscal deficit, to achieve sufficient revenue surplus and to remove impediments to effective discharge of its fiscal management objectives.

That is the stated intent of the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act of 2003 — the FRBM. The logic of the Act is a specific kind of constitutional constraint applied to a different problem. Article 112 binds the executive to Parliament's approval. The FRBM tries to bind the legislature itself — and the governments it produces — to arithmetic limits. The original targets were a fiscal deficit of 3 per cent of GDP and a revenue deficit of zero. The political pressure for this kind of self-imposed discipline came partly from the IMF-era fiscal consolidation that the 1991 crisis had forced on India, and partly from the observation that states had accumulated unsustainable debt through the 1990s — several enacted their own fiscal responsibility laws in parallel.

The gap between the rule and the practice is, I think, the most instructive fact in this chapter. In the two decades since 2003, the 3 per cent target has been met in full only briefly, in years of exceptional revenue buoyancy. The FRBM contains escape clauses — national security, natural calamity, structural reform — and governments have used them with some creativity. The COVID pandemic produced a formal suspension in 2020, with the fiscal deficit reaching above 9 per cent of GDP. The N. K. Singh Committee, set up in 2017 to review the FRBM framework, recommended replacing the single fiscal deficit target with a debt-to-GDP anchor — the principle being that what ultimately matters is not the year's borrowing but the accumulated stock of debt relative to the economy's size. Those recommendations were partially absorbed. The rule still stands, the target still recurs, and the breaches still accumulate. This is not hypocrisy. It is something more structural: fiscal discipline is the first thing to give when the alternative is cutting services that voters depend on or raising taxes that producers resist. A law can set a target; it cannot create the political conditions in which meeting the target is easier than missing it.

Fiscal policy — the use of government spending and taxation to steer the economy — is in theory counter-cyclical: spend more in downturns, when private demand has collapsed, and restrain spending in booms, when it is not needed. This is the Keynesian logic that every Finance Minister invokes. In practice, India's fiscal policy has often been pro-cyclical: in boom years, rising revenues trigger spending expansion rather than deficit reduction; in downturns, falling revenues trigger spending cuts precisely when expansion would most help. The narrow fiscal space is the cause — a government with little room cannot sustain counter-cyclical spending across an entire downturn without alarming the bond market that funds it. The interaction with monetary policy runs in one clear direction: when the government borrows heavily, it competes with private borrowers for the pool of available savings, keeping interest rates higher than they would otherwise be and making the Reserve Bank's task of easing credit conditions harder. Fiscal expansion crowds out private investment, and monetary easing struggles to fill the gap. The two instruments are in constant negotiation, and the direction of pressure usually runs from the fiscal constraint to the monetary response.

The capex push of recent years — a deliberate shift toward raising capital expenditure's share of total spending — represents an attempt to address the historical over-weighting of revenue spending. Whether the structural fiscal constraint has been genuinely eased, and whether the investment multiplier justifies the borrowing it requires, belongs to the current-affairs layer.

Here is the stance I want to carry into what follows. The budget is not, at bottom, an accounting exercise. It is a distribution document — it shows, in aggregate, who pays and who receives, expressed in numbers so numerous that the distribution can hide inside the arithmetic. Every line is a political choice that has been converted into a figure. The revenue deficit measures whether the government is living within its means; but what counts as living within one's means for a government that owes simultaneous commitments to pensioners, to the poor, to infrastructure, to debt-holders — that is not a technical question. It is a question about priorities, and about who has the power to set them. Fiscal policy is the most political of all economic instruments, not because politicians argue about it (they argue about everything), but because the choices it encodes are directly legible in whose conditions improve and whose do not. The next chapter asks where the revenue that fills this picture actually comes from — which taxes, at what rates, collected from whom, and at what distributional cost.
