9 — How does the system rewrite itself?

The previous chapter ended on a question. Can the document, by its own amendment process, be amended out of recognition? I want this chapter to answer that question carefully, because the answer this country has worked out — through a series of judicial encounters between the apex court and successive Parliaments, across the decades from the 1950s to the present — is, I think, one of the most remarkable inventions of any modern constitutional order. It is not a textual answer. It is not what the framers wrote. But it is now, in operative fact, how the country reads its document. And I think the legal-philosophical interest of how the answer was arrived at is worth attending to slowly.

Begin, as before, with what the text of the document actually says. The document carries, in its later parts, an article that sets out the procedure by which it can be amended. The procedure has two tracks. For most matters, Parliament alone can amend the document, by passing a bill in both houses, with a majority of the total membership of each house and a majority of two-thirds of the members present and voting. For a smaller class of matters — those that affect the federal structure, the judiciary, the representation of States in Parliament, the article on amendment itself — the same parliamentary majority is required and the amendment must be ratified by at least half the State legislatures. That is the procedural architecture. On its face, it sounds straightforward. The amending power is parliamentary, supermajoritarian, occasionally federal. There is, on the text, no other limit. The amendment article does not, in 1950, say that the amending power is restricted in subject matter. It does not say that there are features of the document that cannot be touched. It does not say that the courts have any role in policing what amendments are valid. It seems to give Parliament, acting by special majority, an unlimited constituent power to remake the document.

The question that this chapter is the story of is whether that appearance is correct.

In the first decades of the document's life, Parliament used the article freely. There were sixteen amendments by 1962, twenty-four by 1971. Most of them were administrative — adjusting the lists of recognised languages, reorganising States, correcting drafting errors. A few were politically substantive. The first and the fourth of them, in particular, addressed the conflict between the rights chapter and the directive chapter that we treated in chapter three: they immunised certain land-reform legislation from challenge on the ground that it violated the right to property, by adding it to a schedule of the document — a list of laws specially placed beyond rights-based judicial review — and by carving out, on the face of the rights chapter itself, exceptions for state acquisition of estates.

This produced a question that the framers, I think, had not exactly anticipated. Can the rights chapter be amended? If the apparatus, by parliamentary special majority, can remove or restrict any of the freedoms that the rights chapter guarantees, then the rights are guarantees only against the executive and against ordinary legislation, not against constitutional amendment. They are protected against the apparatus on most days but unprotected on the days when the apparatus has a two-thirds majority. The question went to the apex court for the first time in 1951 and again in 1965. On each occasion, the court answered: yes, the rights chapter can be amended. The amending power is plenary; the rights chapter is not exempt from it.

In 1967 the court reversed itself. By a majority of six judges to five, in a judgment that stands in any honest history of this country's constitutional law as a turning point, the court held that the rights chapter cannot be amended. It reasoned, on technical grounds, that an amendment to the constitution is a law within the meaning of the clause that voids any law inconsistent with the rights chapter, and that an amendment which abridges a right must therefore itself be void. The reasoning was contested then and has been contested since, but the result was clear: the rights chapter was, by this judgment, placed beyond the reach of any parliamentary majority short of a fresh constituent act.

Parliament's response was direct. In 1971, by an amendment to the very article we are discussing, Parliament inserted a new clause stating, in effect, that the voiding-clause of the rights chapter does not apply to amendments. Nothing in that voiding clause shall apply to any amendment made under this article. Parliament had asserted, in writing, what the court had denied: that the amending power was plenary, that it reached the rights chapter, that no clause within the document could be used to immunise itself from amendment.

This was the constitutional crisis the apex court had to resolve, and the manner in which it resolved it is, I think, the most important thing I will tell you in any of these chapters.

The court took up the question in 1973, in a case brought by a religious mendicant in Kerala against legislation that had affected the lands of the religious mathematics he headed. The case was heard by a bench of thirteen judges — the largest bench ever assembled in the apex court of this country, before or since. The judgment, when it came, ran to over seven hundred pages and contained eleven separate opinions. The thirteen judges divided seven against six. But it is the seven-judge majority position that has become the law of the country, and that position is what concerns us now.

The seven held the following. They accepted the 1971 amendment that Parliament had passed in response to the 1967 judgment. They agreed that the amending power is, in substance, plenary — it reaches the rights chapter; it reaches every part of the document, including, in principle, the amendment article itself. They restored to Parliament the wide constituent authority that the 1967 judgment had attempted to deny it.

But they added one limitation. The amending power, they said, is plenary within the constitution. It cannot be used to destroy or abridge the basic structure of the constitution. There are features of this document — they did not exhaustively list them, and the various opinions enumerate slightly different lists — that constitute its identity as this constitution, the constitution given to ourselves in 1949. An amendment that altered those features would not be an amendment to the constitution at all. It would be a replacement of the constitution by a different one. And the article that authorises amendment does not, on a proper reading, authorise replacement. The constituent power Parliament holds under the article is creative, but it is creative within limits set by the constitutional identity itself.

Read this carefully, because the doctrinal move is subtle and it is the move the rest of this chapter rests on. The court did not say that the amending power is limited by what the text of the document says. The text, as I noted, sets no subject-matter limit on amendment. The court said that the amending power is limited by what the document is — by its identity as a constitution of a particular kind, with particular structural commitments. That identity is prior to the text in the sense that the text is one expression of it, but does not exhaustively contain it. The text could, in principle, be amended in many ways without changing the identity; the text could also be amended in particular ways that would change the identity, in which case the amendment is not authorised by the document at all.

What features make up this basic structure? The judges, in 1973, gave examples but refused to provide an exhaustive list. Different opinions named different things. Cumulatively, across the seven opinions and across the decades of judgments that have followed, the components that have been recognised as part of the basic structure include: the supremacy of the constitution itself; the sovereign, democratic and republican character of the polity; the secular character of the state; the separation of powers between the three branches we treated in chapter five; the federal character we treated in chapter six; the unity and integrity of the nation; the rule of law; the judicial review of legislation and of executive action; the principle of free and fair elections; the independence of the judiciary; the structural balance between the rights of the second chapter and the directives of the third. And a few others that the courts have, from time to time, added. The list is open-ended. It has not been closed. The doctrine is, in this respect, deliberately under-specified — a working indication of constitutional identity that the courts elaborate case by case rather than declare in advance.

Two years later, in 1975, the doctrine had its first practical application, and it had it under the conditions of the previous chapter. Parliament, during the emergency, passed an amendment that retrospectively validated the Prime Minister's election from 1971 — the same election that the High Court of her constituency had set aside for corrupt practices, and that the apex court was hearing on appeal. The amendment immunised the validation from judicial review. It said, in effect: this election is valid as a matter of constitutional law, and no court can review whether it was. The apex court, hearing the appeal, struck the amendment down. Free and fair elections, the court held, are part of the basic structure. An amendment that placed a particular election above the law of free and fair elections destroyed that structural commitment. Therefore the amendment was beyond the constituent power that the article on amendment grants to Parliament. The doctrine, two years after its invention, performed the function it had been invented for: it preserved a constitutional core against an amendment which a parliamentary majority of the day genuinely wished to enact.

Parliament, during the emergency, then passed the largest single amendment in the document's history. It restructured the constitution along multiple dimensions. It placed directives above rights. It expanded the Centre's powers in several directions. And — most directly addressed to the basic structure doctrine itself — it added two new clauses to the amendment article. The first stated that no amendment to the constitution shall be called in question in any court on any ground. The second stated that there shall be no limitation whatever on the constituent power of Parliament to amend the document. These two clauses were Parliament's attempt to remove, by amendment, the doctrine that the amending power was limited by the basic structure.

The 1977 election, as we saw in the previous chapter, removed the government that had passed those clauses. The successor government, in 1978, repealed many of the changes. The clauses limiting judicial review of amendments, however, were not formally repealed. They remained on the books until 1980, when a different bench of the apex court — hearing a challenge by certain textile mills against an order of nationalisation that had, indirectly, pulled in the question of these clauses — struck them both down. Held: the clauses themselves destroyed the basic structure, because they removed judicial review of constitutional amendments, and judicial review is itself part of the basic structure. The amending power cannot be used to remove the only mechanism by which the basic structure is policed. The doctrine had been used to defend itself against an attempted amendment-out-of-existence.

The doctrine has, since 1980, been applied with measure. It has not been used promiscuously. The court has been careful, in subsequent decades, not to invoke it for matters that ordinary statutory or constitutional review can handle. But it has been invoked. In 2007, a nine-judge bench held that any law placed in the special schedule that immunises listed legislation from rights-based challenge is itself reviewable for basic-structure violation, if added to the schedule after the date of the 1973 judgment. In 2015, the court struck down an amendment that had restructured the manner of judicial appointments, on the ground that judicial independence was part of the basic structure and the new arrangement compromised it. The doctrine continues to evolve.

It is worth, before closing, sitting with the philosophical move that the doctrine makes, because I think it is more interesting than its routine constitutional treatment generally allows.

Recall the foundational claim of the first chapter. The authority of this state derives from a performative, a single sentence in which the people gave the constitution to themselves. This sentence brought into existence the constituent power — the original authority to constitute a state. That power was exercised once, in 1949, by three hundred persons in a hall in New Delhi. The amendment article we have been discussing creates a derived constituent power — a parliamentary capacity to remake the document, not by the same actors as the original, but by a procedure the original document set out. The basic structure doctrine says: the derived power is creative but is limited by the identity of the original product. You can rewrite the document, but only to the extent that what you produce is recognisably a continuation of the document that was originally given. Beyond that, you are not amending; you are constituting a new state. And no clause in the amendment article authorises the constitution of a new state by parliamentary majority. To do that, the constitutive performative would have to be done again, fresh — a new founding, a new "we, the people", a new act of self-giving.

This is, I think, philosophically deep. It locates constitutional authority in two layers. The deeper layer is the constituent moment of 1949, frozen in time, beyond the reach of any subsequent parliamentary majority. The surface layer is the day-to-day amending power, plenary within limits, derived from the deeper. Most of the amendments to this document — over a hundred since enactment — operate at the surface layer. They reorganise States, recognise new languages, adjust reservations, add or modify directives. The surface is busy. The deeper layer has been touched twice — in 1973, when the court located it; in 1975-77, when an amendment attempted to reach it; in 1980, when the court reached it again to strike down those clauses; and a few times since. The constitution survives because the surface and the deeper are kept distinct.

The doctrine has been criticised, and the criticisms are not foolish. From one direction: what gives the court the right to identify what is and is not basic? The text doesn't give it that authority. The court invented it. This is true. The doctrine has no textual basis. It is a judicial creation. The court's defence is that, without it, the architecture is defenceless against the kind of amendment that destroys the architecture by lawful procedure — the 1976 attempt to do exactly this is the strongest example. From the other direction: the doctrine sets the court above the elected legislature, which is the only body holding democratic legitimacy. This is also true. The court, on basic-structure questions, can defeat a parliamentary majority. The defence is that the constitution itself, as a document of consent given by an earlier generation to all subsequent generations, is what the parliamentary majority is operating under, not above. A majority does not have authority to give itself the constitution it would prefer.

Whether the criticisms or the defences are correct is a question for political philosophy more than constitutional law, and I will not pretend to resolve it here. What I will note is that the doctrine, since 1973, has held. It has held through emergencies, through majority governments of every persuasion, through several attempts to constrain or remove it. The court has applied it sparingly enough to retain political legitimacy and consistently enough to constitute, in effect, a substantive constitutional limit not in the text. This is itself a remarkable thing.

So: how does the system rewrite itself?

By a parliamentary special majority, on most matters; by parliamentary special majority plus state ratification on a smaller class of federal-and-judicial matters; and never, on any matter, in a way that destroys the structural identity of the document as the constitution given to ourselves in 1949. The amending power is plenary within those limits. Beyond them, it is not an amending power at all. It is the power to make a new state, which the document does not grant.

That answers the question of how. It does not answer the question of who guards this answer. The basic structure doctrine, like the rights chapter and like every other limit drawn against the apparatus, depends on an institution willing to hold the line. Which institution holds the line? Why is its standing to do so accepted? What other bodies, formal and informal, share in the work of policing the document against capture? That is the question of the next chapter.
