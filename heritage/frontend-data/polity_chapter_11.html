11 — What about communities the system inherited as excluded?

I noted, in closing the previous chapter, that this story has, throughout, addressed the population the document governs as a whole. The herd has been treated as one body. The citizen has been treated as one figure. The rights and obligations and structures we have surveyed have been described as universal — as applying, equally, to every member of the population the document took as its subject in 1949. This was a useful simplification. It is also, I think, an inadequate one. The population the document inherited was not, in 1949, a single homogenous body of equally placed persons. It was a population that had been hierarchically organised for centuries by inherited social systems; that had been administratively divided for nearly two centuries by a colonial apparatus; that contained communities living, in some cases, in geographic and cultural isolation from the political centre; that had, at the moment of the document's enactment, just been violently divided by partition, and that was carrying, across its body, the unhealed wounds of that event.

The document, which had committed itself in its first chapter to the universal we-the-people, knew this. Its framers knew this — Ambedkar most acutely, having lived inside the inherited exclusion he then helped write a document against. The framers had a choice. They could declare formal equality and treat the asymmetric historical exclusions as social problems the law would not address, leaving the substantive work to politics, to time, to whatever would come. Or they could write the asymmetric treatment of the asymmetrically excluded into the document itself, accepting that legal universality alone could not produce social universality, and that, until the underlying exclusion was overcome, the document would have to make specific provisions for specific situations.

They chose the second. And the closing parts of the document — the chapters this chapter is about — are the place where the document makes that choice visible.

This is, I think, the document's most honest moment. Having spent its central architecture on a universal frame — citizens, rights, duties, branches, levels, elections, courts — it acknowledges, at its closing, that the universal is, in 1949, incomplete. The architecture cannot, by itself, integrate populations that the inherited exclusions had placed outside the conditions of meaningful citizenship. The document writes, into itself, exceptions that the universal cannot reach. It does not pretend the exceptions are unnecessary. It does not pretend the exclusions will be quickly overcome. It simply records that they exist, and provides instruments for their gradual correction.

Two registers of asymmetric provision run through these closing chapters. The first addresses communities — groups of people whose exclusion is inherited from social, religious, or caste hierarchies, or from geographic and cultural distinctness, regardless of where they live within the country. The second addresses regions — particular States or territories whose populations have specific historical or political claims that the standard federal template, treated in our chapter on federalism, would not honour. Take them in order.

The most contested register, and the one that has produced more political heat than any other in the document's history, is the provision for the historically excluded castes. The framers had, by the 1940s, an entire colonial apparatus of recognition to draw on. The British had, in 1909 and 1919 and 1932, attempted various forms of separate political representation for what were then called the Depressed Classes — a category designating those communities, mostly within Hinduism, who had been subjected to the ritual exclusion known as untouchability. In 1932 the colonial government had announced separate electorates — an arrangement under which only members of the depressed castes would vote for their own representatives, in a separate constituency. This was opposed by Gandhi, who undertook a fast unto death from prison; it was reluctantly negotiated by Ambedkar, who saw separate electorates as the only structural protection his community would have. The agreement that emerged from those negotiations — usually called the Poona Pact — preserved the principle of guaranteed representation but converted separate electorates into reserved seats within general constituencies: the seat would be filled only by a candidate from the excluded castes, but the voters would include the general population. This was a political compromise whose effects are still being argued. The Indian framers, when they came to write the document, broadly inherited this device. The list of historically excluded castes — which the document calls the Scheduled Castes — and a parallel list of geographically and culturally distinct tribal communities — the Scheduled Tribes — were carried forward into the document as categories deserving particular constitutional protection.

The protections take three forms. Reserved seats in the lower house of Parliament and in the State legislatures, in proportion to the population of the relevant communities — meaning that a fixed number of constituencies are designated as reserved, and only members of the relevant community can stand from them. Reserved positions in employment under the apparatus, again in specified proportions. Reserved seats in publicly-funded educational institutions, on similar proportions.

A philosophically interesting feature of the original design was that the electoral reservations were explicitly time-bound. The framers wrote, into the document, that the electoral reservation would last for ten years from enactment. The thinking was: this is a transitional provision. Once the historical exclusion has been substantively overcome, the reservation will be unnecessary, and the provision will quietly expire. The ten years passed. The exclusion had not been substantively overcome. The provision was extended, by amendment, for another ten years. That decade passed too. The provision was extended again. It has, since enactment, been repeatedly extended; the most recent extension carries it into the next decade. The other reservations — in employment and education — were not time-bound in the original text and remain in operation today.

This pattern — the temporal mechanism that has not, in fact, been temporal — is, I think, the most honest thing one can say about reservations. The reservations have not failed. The underlying social work they were intended to be transitional has failed. The historical exclusions of caste have, in the parts of the country where they were sharpest, eased; in many other parts, they have not. As long as the underlying inequality persists, the constitutional supplements that address it persist with it. The provision will continue to be extended for as long as the work it was meant to be transitional continues to be undone.

Through the late twentieth century, the framework was extended beyond the originally listed castes and tribes. A commission was constituted, under the document's authority, to identify those communities — outside the formal list of scheduled castes and tribes — whose social and educational backwardness made them comparable in deprivation, even if not identical in inheritance. This commission, known after its chair, reported in 1980 with a list of communities to be added to the framework of reservation; its recommendations were, after substantial political contention, implemented in 1990 by extending employment and educational reservations to a new category — the backward classes. The apex court, on subsequent challenge, upheld the extension in principle but added a doctrinal limit: those persons within the backward classes who had, by virtue of family wealth, government office, or other indicators, moved beyond the substantive condition of backwardness, would not be eligible to claim reservation. This doctrine — usually called the creamy layer test — refined the framework so that reservations would, in principle, reach those for whom they were intended.

A second class of community-asymmetric provisions addresses populations that are culturally distinct in ways the universal civic framework would, if uncorrected, override. The tribal populations of the country — communities living, often, in forest and hill regions, with their own languages, customary laws, agrarian arrangements, and social structures — would, under a uniform application of the standard architecture, be subject to land laws, marriage laws, inheritance laws, money-lending laws that had been designed for very different social conditions. The likely consequence would be the rapid alienation of tribal land to non-tribal interests, the dissolution of customary social arrangements, and the absorption of these populations into a uniform legal regime that would not, in fact, treat them justly. The document anticipates this. Two of its closing schedules address tribal populations directly. One sets out a special administrative regime for tribal areas across the country, with restrictions on the alienation of land to non-tribals, with the State's Governor empowered to make regulations specifically for tribal welfare, with money-lending controlled, with a council of tribal advisors institutionally located near the executive. A separate schedule deals with the tribal areas of the four States in the country's North-East, providing for autonomous district councils with the power to make laws on village administration, on social custom, on marriage and inheritance, on the management of forests and water — in effect, a sub-federal arrangement within the standard federal arrangement, calibrated to the specific cultural conditions of the populations covered. These are not the reserved seats of the caste-based provisions. They are something different — a recognition that some communities cannot be governed, justly, by the universal architecture, and require their own institutional space within it.

The second register — regional asymmetry — runs through the closing parts of the document along similar lines. The framers, and their successors, wrote into the document specific arrangements for particular States whose populations had specific claims that the standard federal arrangement would not adequately honour. Several of the States of the country's North-East — formed mostly in the decades after the document's enactment, as the framers' original arrangement was rearranged to accommodate populations the original arrangement had not adequately included — carry constitutional provisions specific to themselves. Customary law on land, marriage, and inheritance is protected against displacement by ordinary central legislation. Local self-governance is given particular procedural recognition. The transfer of land to outsiders is, in some cases, prohibited absolutely. These are States that exist, on the document's face, on terms different from the standard federal template — and the difference is an acknowledgment that the standard template, applied uniformly, would have produced, for these populations, exactly the kind of cultural and material dispossession that the document is, in other places, supposed to prevent.

Two regional cases deserve specific mention. One is the State formed in 1975 from a former protectorate, on terms that preserved its pre-existing legal protections; the integration was negotiated rather than imposed, and the document records the negotiated terms. The other is, by far, the most contested asymmetric provision in the document's history. From 1949 until 2019, the document carried a special provision under which the State of Jammu and Kashmir was integrated with the Indian Union on terms much narrower than those that applied to other States. The State had its own constitution. The provisions of the Indian constitution applied to it only in modified form, and only with the concurrence of the State's legislature for many extensions. The arrangement reflected the specific political conditions of the State's accession in 1947. In August 2019, the central Parliament passed legislation that, in operative effect, removed the special provisions, applied the Indian constitution to the State in unmodified form, and reorganised the State into two Union Territories. The matter is contested — politically, constitutionally, and in litigation that the apex court has so far heard partially. I will not adjudicate the question here. I note only that the abrogation, whatever its ultimate constitutional validity, was the most consequential alteration of the document's asymmetric architecture in its history.

The third register I want to note briefly is religious and linguistic asymmetry, although it is, in some sense, distinct from the others because it is addressed not in the document's closing parts but in the rights chapter we treated long ago. There we noted the protection of minority cultures, the right of minorities to establish and run their own educational institutions, the right of any community with a distinct language or culture to conserve it. These are protections that operate, in effect, against the universalising tendency of any single State that absorbs many such communities. They were, with the asymmetric provisions of the closing parts, part of the same cluster of recognition: that the universal frame of citizenship, however carefully drawn, cannot replace the specific protections that specific communities require.

What is the document doing, in writing all of this into itself?

I want to suggest, at the close of this chapter and of this story, that the document is doing a single coherent thing across its eleven chapters, and that the asymmetric provisions of the closing parts are not an exception to the universal architecture but a completion of it. The first chapter argued that the authority of this state derives from a constitutive performative — we, the people, give to ourselves. That performative claimed a we that did not, at the moment of utterance, fully exist; it produced, by its utterance, the people whose self-rule it claimed to inaugurate. The chapters that followed described the architecture by which the people thus constituted are governed: the walls drawn against the apparatus, the programmes the apparatus must work towards, the duties owed in return, the institutional limbs through which the apparatus acts, the federal balance, the consent renewal, the emergency exceptions, the amendment limits, the guardian institutions. All of these chapters operated on the assumption of a we that the constitutive performative had brought into existence.

The closing chapter the document writes, and this chapter records, acknowledges that the we the constitutive performative claimed was, in 1949, not finished. There were members of it who had been formally included by the constitutive sentence but who had not been substantively included by the social order on which the constitutive sentence had laid itself. The asymmetric provisions are the document's recognition that those members would, without specific correction, remain formally inside and substantively outside. The herd was a herd of many flocks; some of those flocks the constitutive sentence had absorbed by name but not yet, in social fact, integrated. The closing parts of the document write in the slower work that the constitutive performative could not, by itself, complete.

Ambedkar, in the closing speech to the Constituent Assembly that I have quoted in earlier chapters, warned that political democracy without social democracy would be unstable. The first chapter of this story described the political democracy he meant — the formal equality of vote, the formal equality before law, the formal equality of citizenship. The third chapter described the programmatic commitment to the social democracy he warned was unfinished — the welfare state in directive form. This eleventh chapter describes the specific corrections the document makes, for specific historically-excluded communities, to bring the formal political into closer alignment with the absent social. The three chapters together — the foundational performative, the programmatic ambition, the asymmetric correction — define the substantive scope of what the document is trying to do.

It is not, despite its hundred-plus amendments, a finished text. It will continue to be amended. The interpretive layer the previous chapter described will continue to read it. The reservations and asymmetric provisions will continue to be extended, narrowed, contested, defended. The herd will continue to discover new forms of exclusion within itself, and the document will continue to attempt — sometimes well, sometimes poorly, often after the underlying problem has been visible for years — to address them. The eleven governance problems this story has been organised around are not, on any of them, problems the document has finally solved. They are problems the document has provided frameworks within which to keep working.

The herd metaphor, returned for the last time. The shepherd is held by the flock. The staff is distributed across many hands within the flock. The lines protecting individual sheep from the staff are drawn and policed by other parts of the flock. The flock contains many smaller flocks, each with its own integrity, that the document recognises and tries, with imperfect instruments, to keep distinct. None of this is finished. The document is the working set of arrangements by which it can continue.

We are at the end of the story.

There is no ending, in the sense that the older stories of nations had endings. The document does not conclude. It does not announce a triumph. The twenty-sixth of November 1949, on which the framers signed it, and the twenty-sixth of January 1950, on which it came into force, were not endings; they were beginnings of a working arrangement that the country has been operating, contesting, amending, and reading ever since. We, the people, give to ourselves. The performative was uttered once, in a small hall in New Delhi. But the giving has not stopped. Each election the population holds, each judgment the apex court delivers, each amendment Parliament passes, each watch the constitutional bodies keep on the apparatus, each act of dissent the population mounts when the apparatus oversteps — each of these is a quiet re-utterance of the constitutive sentence. We, the people, continue to give to ourselves. As long as that giving continues, the document remains. When it stops — if it stops — no clause within it can save it.

That is, I think, the most honest answer one can give to the eleven questions this story has been built around. Each of them has a textual answer in the document. None of them has an answer that is secured by the text alone. Each of them depends, in the end, on the ongoing work of a population that has, for seventy-six years now, agreed to act as though a sentence written in 1949 binds it. Whether that agreement continues is the question that hangs over every chapter you have read.

The story is at an end. The work the story describes is not.
