# 3 — From the Wheel to the Word: The Long Classical Era

Stand in the National Museum in Delhi before the Lion Capital of Ashoka. Four lions face the four directions, carved from a single block of buff Chunar sandstone, polished to a mirror finish that two thousand years have not dimmed. Below them, an abacus of animals — bull, horse, elephant — separated by four spinning wheels. Above them, originally, a great wheel crowning the whole. Underneath, in Pali script, runs the phrase Devanampiya Piyadasi — "the Beloved of the Gods." The wheel and the lion: India took both as its national emblem in 1950, the Ashoka Chakra spinning at the centre of its flag. The question this chapter asks is simple: how did this object come to be? The answer is the story of a thousand years.

The previous era had ended in the dissolution of the Indus cities and the long Vedic centuries that followed — a world of sacrifices and hymns, of nomadic clans slowly settling the Gangetic plain. By 600 BCE, the world this era opens into is transformed. Iron has arrived: iron axes clearing the dense eastern forests, iron-tipped ploughshares turning the heavy alluvial soil of the Ganga valley, making production possible at a scale no earlier Indian civilisation had attempted. Surplus grain drives a second urbanisation — not the planned Harappan grid-cities, but organic trading towns, capitals of territories, seats of argument and commerce. Rajgir, Vaishali, Varanasi, Pataliputra, Ujjain: these are cities of the sixteen mahajanapadas, the great territorial states of the Gangetic plain from about 600 BCE. Punch-marked silver coins circulate. Trade networks reach south and north and west. The wheel — not yet the dharma-chakra but the wooden wheel of the cart — is what moves this world.

This era runs from approximately 600 BCE to 650 CE — one of the longest in the curriculum. It contains the first historical kings and the first empire, the first global religion exported from the subcontinent, a four-hundred-year interlude of post-Maurya fragmentation and plurality, a classical synthesis of astonishing density, and ends with a figure who is simultaneously the last great emperor of classical India and the first sign that the classical centre cannot hold. The wheel will turn many times before we are done.

## The sixteen and the rise of Magadha

The sixteen mahajanapadas of early Buddhist and Jain texts form the political landscape into which both the Buddha and Mahavira were born. Some are monarchies — Koshala, Magadha, Avanti, Vatsa. Some are gana-sanghas, oligarchic republics where a council of rajas governed by assembly. Among these, the Vajji confederacy at Vaishali stands out: seven thousand seven hundred and seven rajas deliberating in a great assembly hall. Buddha and Mahavira both came from such republican clans — Buddha from the Shakyas of Kapilavastu, Mahavira from the Jnatrika kshatriyas near Vaishali. The very political forms that would eventually produce the world's first empire first produced a world of structured debate.

The religious ferment of this period belongs to the same generation as the state-formation. Siddhartha Gautama, born around 563 BCE in Lumbini, attained enlightenment under a pipal tree at Bodh Gaya around 528 BCE and delivered his first sermon at Sarnath near Varanasi. The Four Noble Truths — dukkha, its cause, its cessation, the Eightfold Path — were spoken in Pali, not Sanskrit. This was deliberate. Buddhism opened its doors to all castes, admitted women to the sangha through Ananda's mediation, and held that status by birth was neither fixed nor spiritually decisive. The Tripitaka, compiled at the early Buddhist councils and finalised at the Third Council under Ashoka, would become the doctrinal foundation from which a world religion grew. In the same decades, Vardhamana Mahavira — preceded by the twenty-third tirthankara Parshvanatha but giving the tradition its effective philosophical form — codified Jainism around five great vows (ahimsa, truth, non-stealing, non-possession, celibacy) and the insistence that every living being possesses a soul whose liberation requires radical non-violence. Both traditions challenged the brahmanical ritual order that the Dharmashastras were simultaneously codifying — the Manusmriti, compiled across several centuries, would eventually become the most authoritative Hindu law text, prescribing graded duties and privileges by varna in exhaustive detail. The epics, too, were being composed and expanded in this long creative period: the *Mahabharata*, with its hundred thousand verses on the Kaurava-Pandava war and its embedded *Bhagavad Gita*; the *Ramayana* of Valmiki, with its ideal of the dharma-observing king and the devoted wife. These texts would define the cultural grammar of the next two millennia.

Magadha is the political story of this era's first six centuries. Its advantages were structural: rich iron deposits near Rajgir, fertile alluvial soil, river-fortress capitals that geography itself defended (Rajgir surrounded by five hills, Pataliputra at the confluence of the Ganga, Son, and Ghaghra), and access to war elephants from the eastern forests. The Haryanka dynasty — Bimbisara (~544 BCE), who used marriage diplomacy to build three royal alliances and commanded the allegiance of 86,000 village headmen, and his son Ajatasattu, who smashed the Lichchhavi republic of Vaishali with siege engines over sixteen years — gave Magadha the habit of expansion. The Shishunagas absorbed the rival Avanti kingdom. And then the Nandas — founded by Mahapadma Nanda, of non-kshatriya origin, who bore the title "destroyer of all kshatriyas" — amassed a military machine so formidable that when Alexander of Macedonia crossed the Hydaspes in 326 BCE, defeated Porus, and pressed east, his own army mutinied at the Beas. The soldiers refused to march against an empire they could not see but had heard described.

Alexander's nineteen months in India — the Achaemenid conquest of the northwest by Darius I a century earlier had already introduced the Kharoshthi script and Persian bell-capital forms — left structural traces: three Greek governors in the northwest, firm chronological anchors for Indian history, and a vacuum into which Chandragupta Maurya stepped within five years of Alexander's departure. How Chandragupta — of disputed origin, his brahmana strategist Chanakya (Kautilya) orchestrating the campaign — overthrew the Nandas is dramatised in Vishakhadatta's ninth-century play *Mudrarakshasa*. What is historical is the outcome: by 321 BCE, Magadha had its first pan-Indian empire. Within fifteen years, Chandragupta had fought Seleucus Nicator to a treaty that gave him eastern Afghanistan, Baluchistan, and 500 war elephants in exchange for those elephants, while Seleucus sent an ambassador named Megasthenes to Pataliputra. The wheel was rolling.

## The empire that learned to teach

The *Arthashastra*, attributed to Chanakya and rediscovered in 1905, is one of the most astonishing documents of ancient political thought. Fifteen books covering statecraft, economics, espionage, irrigation management, and the mandala theory of inter-state relations — every neighbour an enemy, every neighbour's neighbour a potential friend. It prescribes 27 adhyakshas (superintendents) over specific economic domains and mandates a state apparatus so comprehensive that Megasthenes, describing Chandragupta's Pataliputra in his *Indika*, catalogued a city of 270,000 people governed by six committees of five, its commerce and births and deaths all registered. The Mauryan state at its height extended four provincial governors at Taxila, Ujjain, Tosali, and Suvarnagiri, connected by roads along which pillars, resthouses, and wells marked the emperor's reach.

The Mauryan empire was the first state in Indian history to attempt to govern a subcontinent. Its army made it impossible for any external power to dislodge. But it is not the army that this era remembers. It is Ashoka.

Bindusara's son Ashoka (reigned 268-232 BCE) fought one major war. It was his eighth year, around 261 BCE, and the target was Kalinga, the coastal kingdom controlling the eastern sea routes. The war succeeded. Kalinga was annexed. And then Ashoka did something no emperor before him had done: he recorded his own remorse. The Thirteenth Major Rock Edict — notably omitted from the copies of the edicts found in Kalinga itself — declared:

> *One hundred thousand were killed, one hundred and fifty thousand deported, and many times that number perished. Now the Beloved of Gods feels deep remorse on account of the conquest of Kalinga. For the conquest of a country previously unconquered involves the slaughter, death, and deportation of the people. What is even more grievous to the Beloved of Gods is that brahmanas, shramanas, and householders — those who practice obedience to elders, who behave properly and have strong loyalty to friends and relatives — these too suffer violence, murder, and separation from their loved ones.*

The word Ashoka uses for what comes after this remorse is *dhammaghosha* — the drum of dhamma — in place of *bherighosha*, the drum of war. He did not disband his army. He did not surrender Kalinga. The apparatus remained. But in the thirty-odd years that followed, Ashoka bent that apparatus toward a different purpose: the propagation of an ethical code emphasising ahimsa, respect for parents and elders, mercy to servants, and amity between sects. He appointed dhamma-mahamattas — officials who rode through the provinces teaching rather than taxing — and rajukas who administered justice with a new injunction toward uniformity regardless of varna, a direct challenge to the Dharmashastra principle of graded punishment. His thirty-nine inscriptions, in Prakrit and Brahmi across most of the subcontinent, in Kharoshthi in the northwest, in Greek and Aramaic at Kandahar, are the first direct royal communication with subjects in Indian history. This is the empire that learned to teach.

The wheel — the dharma-chakra — appears on the Lion Capital at Sarnath, at the top of the abacus, a symbol not of military conquest but of the rolling-out of a moral order. The Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra, around 250 BCE, finalised the Tripitaka and sent Buddhist missions to Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Central Asia, and the Hellenistic west. It is through Ashoka, not through the Buddha alone, that Buddhism became a world religion.

The Mauryan cultural achievement is inseparable from its political reach. The polished Chunar sandstone pillars — the Sarnath Lion, the Rampurva Bull, the Sankissa Elephant — absorb Achaemenid bell-capital influences and transform them into something distinctively Indian. The Barabar Caves, carved from granite for Ajivika monks and given the characteristic Mauryan mirror-polish, are the earliest surviving rock-cut architecture in India; the chaitya-arch facade of Lomas Rishi is a prototype for everything Ajanta would later become. The Sanchi Stupa, originally built under Ashoka and expanded with elaborately carved toranas under the Shungas, and the Bharhut Stupa with its narrative reliefs, mark the transition from Mauryan imperial scale to a more organic popular Buddhist art. The empire fragmented after Ashoka's death in 232 BCE. The last Maurya, Brihadratha, was assassinated by his commander-in-chief Pushyamitra Shunga in 185 BCE, inaugurating the brahmanical resurgence that would colour the next four centuries. The apparatus the Mauryas had built was dismantled piece by piece. The wheel kept rolling, but no single hand directed it.

## The four-hundred-year plural

The period from 185 BCE to 320 CE — Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Kushans in the north; Satavahanas in the Deccan; Sangam Tamil states in the south — is conventionally treated as a story of separate powers in separate columns. I think this misses the deepest thing about the era: it was the most cosmopolitan period in ancient Indian history, and cosmopolitanism was its great intellectual engine.

The Indo-Greeks arrived first, pushing south from Bactria around 200 BCE. Their king Menander — Milinda, in the Buddhist texts — ruled from the Hindu Kush to at least the Yamuna and reportedly reached Mathura. What is remarkable is not the conquest but the cultural exchange at its edges. Menander converted to Buddhism, debated the monk Nagasena in a dialogue preserved in the *Milindapanho* as a Pali classic; his ashes were reportedly divided among cities like a Buddha's relics. A Greek ambassador named Heliodorus, sent to the Shunga king at Vidisha around 110 BCE, erected a Garuda pillar and called himself a Bhagavata — the earliest inscriptional evidence of a foreigner embracing Vaishnavism. The Indo-Greeks issued the first coins in India attributable to named kings and introduced theatrical vocabulary (yavanika — the stage curtain) and Hellenistic motifs into Indian art. The cultural absorption ran in all directions.

The Shakas displaced the Indo-Greeks in the first century BCE. The most interesting Shaka was Rudradaman I (reigned c. 130-150 CE), whose Junagadh Rock Inscription is the earliest substantial royal composition in chaste Sanskrit — marking the decisive shift from Prakrit to Sanskrit as the language of power. His inscription records repairing the Sudarshana Lake reservoir in Kathiawar, originally built under Chandragupta Maurya, on rock that already bore Ashokan edicts. Three empires, one rock: Maurya, Shaka, and soon Gupta would all leave their mark on that face of stone. Notice what is being said here: the Junagadh rock is a palimpsest, and so is Indian history.

The Kushans are the empire of the Silk Road. Kanishka — the greatest Kushan ruler, who began what became the Shaka Era in 78 CE (still the Government of India's official calendar) — held an empire from Bactria to Pataliputra, controlled the overland Silk Road from China through Central Asia into the Roman Mediterranean, and patronised Mahayana Buddhism's formative moment. The Fourth Buddhist Council at Kashmir, convened around 100 CE, produced Sanskrit commentaries on the Tripitaka and formalised the Mahayana-Hinayana split. The Mahayana — the Great Vehicle, the Buddhism of bodhisattvas who delay nirvana to help all beings — required images of the Buddha to focus devotion; and it got them. At Gandhara, in grey-blue schist, the Buddha appeared in Apollo-like form, his drapery folded in Roman style. At Mathura simultaneously, in red spotted sandstone, the indigenous school rendered the Buddha in fully Indian volumes, broad-shouldered, smiling. Both schools fed each other; both were products of the same cosmopolitan moment. The *Natyashastra* of Bharata Muni, codifying Sanskrit dramatic theory and the nine rasas, belongs to this same world of creative synthesis; so does the *Sushruta Samhita*, the foundational text of Indian surgery with its 300 surgical procedures.

In the Deccan, the Satavahanas held the centre for three centuries. Their greatest king, Gautamiputra Shri Satakarni (reigned c. 106-130 CE), defeated the Shaka Nahapana and re-struck 8,000 of his silver coins as a trophy. Their rock-cut patronage produced the Karle Chaitya — the grandest rock-cut prayer hall of ancient India, forty metres long, its vaulted interior a masterpiece of carved stone — and the Nasik caves, whose inscriptions by Gautamiputra's mother Gautami Balashri are among the key sources for the period. Their successors in the eastern Deccan, the Ikshvakus, made Nagarjunakonda on the Krishna — near which Nagarjuna, the great Madhyamaka philosopher of shunyata, had lived — one of the most architecturally dense Buddhist sites of the ancient world, its Mahabodhi-type stupa and two dozen monasteries now preserved on an island after the site was submerged by a dam in 1960.

The Sangam Tamil states — Pandya at Madurai, Chola along the Kaveri, Chera on the Malabar coast — were the most integrated part of this era's global economy. The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a Greek merchant manual of around 60 CE, describes the Chera port of Muziris with Roman trading colonies and a temple of Augustus. Some six thousand Roman gold and silver coins have been found across south India — evidence of a trade in which India sold pepper, cotton, ivory, and steel, and received gold. Pliny complained in 77 CE that Rome was being drained of wealth to pay for Indian spice. The Tamil literary tradition — the *Tolkappiyam* (the earliest surviving Tamil grammar and poetics), the Eight Anthologies, the *Silappadikaram* with its crossroads of sea-port and inland court — was being composed through this entire period. And from the same world, eventually, the *Tirukkural* of Tiruvalluvar would emerge: 1,330 couplets on virtue, wealth, and love, translated into over forty languages, a Tamil ethical canon owed nothing to the Sanskrit court world and everything to it. The wheel had reached the southernmost tip of the subcontinent.

## The classical moment

In 319-320 CE, Chandragupta I — a relatively obscure ruler of eastern UP and Bihar who married the Lichchhavi princess Kumaradevi and took the title Maharajadhiraja — declared a new era. His son Samudragupta (reigned c. 335-380 CE) earned the sobriquet "Napoleon of India" from V.A. Smith for what the *Allahabad Pillar Inscription* — composed by his court poet Harishena and engraved on the same Ashokan pillar at Prayag that bore the Mauryan edicts — records: nine Gangetic kings dispossessed, eighteen southern kings defeated and reinstated (the dakshinapatha digvijaya reaching the Pallava king at Kanchi), frontier kingdoms paying tribute. The same pillar had borne Ashoka's message of dhamma; Harishena's prashasti bore Samudragupta's military catalogue. The empire that had learned to teach was now learning to sing — in Sanskrit kavya, the court language of the new age.

Chandragupta II (reigned c. 380-412 CE) — Vikramaditya — defeated the Western Kshatrapa Shakas around 400 CE, absorbed their Gujarat and Malwa territories, and gave the empire direct access to western-coast trade. His daughter Prabhavati Gupta, married into the Vakataka dynasty of the Deccan, served as regent after her husband's early death, extending Gupta influence southward through matrimonial alliance. The Mehrauli Iron Pillar — a seven-metre wrought-iron column standing in Delhi, still rust-free after sixteen centuries — carries an inscription of a king "Chandra" whose exploits scholars identify with Chandragupta II. That pillar is a testimony to Gupta metallurgy; in its extraordinary durability, also a metaphor for what the Gupta age produced.

The word "classical" in "Gupta classical age" means something specific: later ages will quote this moment. A classical period, in this sense, is one whose canon becomes foundational for everything that follows. Kalidasa set the standard for Sanskrit literature — seven works, including the *Abhijnanashakuntalam* (translated by William Jones in 1789 as the first Sanskrit work into European languages, praised by Goethe), the *Meghaduta*, the *Raghuvamsha* — from which all later poets claimed descent. Aryabhata, born at Pataliputra in 476 CE, computed the circumference of the earth, correctly explained solar and lunar eclipses (not a demon Rahu but the earth's shadow), worked out sine tables, and gave a place-value decimal system that was the foundation on which the concept of zero was codified. Varahamihira synthesised five schools of Indian astronomy at Ujjain. Nalanda — founded by Kumaragupta I in the fifth century, supported by revenues from 100 villages, housing some ten thousand students from across Asia — produced the most sophisticated philosophical curriculum of the ancient world. The early Hindu temples crystallised here: the flat-roofed garbhagriha of Sanchi Temple 17, the first shikhara rising over the Dashavatara Temple at Deogarh (c. 500 CE), the colossal Varaha relief in the Udayagiri caves (401 CE) showing Vishnu-as-boar rescuing the earth goddess from the cosmic ocean — allegory of an empire rescuing order from chaos. The Mahabodhi Temple at Bodh Gaya, marking the spot of the Buddha's enlightenment with its stepped-pyramid shikhara, took its canonical Gupta-era form in the fifth and sixth centuries. The Ajanta paintings, in their second Mahayana phase under Vakataka patronage, rendered the Padmapani Bodhisattva in Cave 1 with a psychological depth that no earlier Indian art had approached. The *Bhagavad Gita* was consolidated into its canonical form as the defining text of Vaishnava devotion — karma yoga, jnana yoga, bhakti yoga, synthesised into a single philosophical framework. The Puranas were compiled. Sanskrit became the court language everywhere.

But the classical synthesis was already under pressure by the time it reached its finest expression. Skandagupta (reigned c. 455-467 CE) repelled the first great Huna invasion — the Hephthalite nomads pressing south through the Hindu Kush — and commemorated the victory in the Junagadh inscription, on the same rock that bore Ashokan and Rudradaman inscriptions. The empire that had learned to teach now had to re-learn to fight. The strain showed in Skandagupta's coinage: visibly debased gold, lighter than his father's. Under the Huna kings Toramana and the fanatical Mihirakula — who destroyed 1,600 monasteries per Xuanzang's account — north India was devastated. It was Yashodharman of Malwa who finally broke Mihirakula around 528-532 CE, setting up victory pillars that marked the end of Gupta paramountcy as definitively as the Guptas themselves had ended the post-Maurya interregnum. The centre could not hold.

## The last inheritor

The world Harshavardhana inherited in 606 CE was not the Gupta world. Pataliputra was a memory. The new capital was Kanauj — elevated, fortified, positioned on the doab between Ganges and Yamuna, suited to the feudal military logic of samantas and land-grants rather than the mercantile logic of riverside commerce. When Harsha's elder brother Rajyavardhana was murdered by Shashanka of Gauda (who had notoriously replaced the Buddha image at Bodh Gaya with a Shiva linga), Harsha took the throne of Thanesar at sixteen and spent the next four years in campaigns that reunited most of north India under his authority.

What is remarkable about Harsha's reign is how richly it is documented. Banabhatta's *Harshacharita* — the first Sanskrit historical biography, eight chapters of ornate gadya-kavya, a genre Banabhatta effectively invented — gives us Harsha's early life, his accession, and his character. Xuanzang, the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim who spent fifteen years in India (630-645 CE), studied at Nalanda under the great teacher Silabhadra and attended Harsha's grand assemblies at Kanauj and Prayag, left in his *Da Tang Xiyu Ji* the single most important primary source for seventh-century Indian society: great monasteries, bustling cities, generous royal patronage — and also caste structures growing more rigid, and the feudal land-grant system that Sharma would later call "Indian feudalism" beginning to crystallise. The land-grant system — pioneered by the Satavahanas and now become routine — transferred tax-revenue and judicial authority over peasant land to brahmana grantees and monasteries, slowly converting direct-revenue cultivators into dependent tenants. The seeds of the early medieval regional order were already germinating inside the last classical empire.

Harsha was stopped at the Narmada by the Chalukyan king Pulakesin II — whose *Aihole Inscription* (634 CE) compared him to the heroes Kalidasa had sung, and whose defeat of Harsha is the first decisive north-south military check in the subcontinent's history. Harsha himself wrote three Sanskrit plays and patronised a court whose sophistication Xuanzang marvelled at. He died around 647 CE without an heir. His empire — like the Mauryan, like the Gupta — had been held together by a single personality rather than institutionalised succession. After his death, Kanauj itself became the object of struggle among the Gurjara-Pratiharas, the Palas, and the Rashtrakutas. The wheel had completed its long revolution.

## What the era leaves us

This era built the grammar of Indian civilisation. Its first empire and the imperial idea that accompanied it. Two great heterodox religions — Buddhism and Jainism — and their transformations (Buddhism into a world religion through Ashoka and Kanishka; Jainism into a community of remarkable cultural persistence). The Sanskrit literary canon in its classical form; the mathematical zero; the temple form, from stupa to shikhara; the concept of the wheel — dharma-chakra — as a political-religious symbol that travels from Sarnath to Ashoka's edicts to Samudragupta's prashasti on the same pillar to Aryabhata's cosmology to the centre of India's national flag. Nalanda, which would survive for seven more centuries before Bakhtiyar Khilji's soldiers burned it. And the land-grant system, quietly converting the agrarian world beneath all this brilliance.

The world it passes to the next era is not the world it began in. The pan-Indian empire has failed twice — Mauryan and Gupta — and will not be reconstituted for nearly a thousand years. The early medieval world is the world of the regional: Pratiharas, Palas, Rashtrakutas in the north; Pallavas, Chalukyas, Cholas in the south. The Bhakti reconfiguration of religion — devotion to a personal god, open to all regardless of caste, singing in vernacular Tamil and eventually in Hindi and Bengali — which will displace the imperial Buddhist synthesis this era built, is already beginning in the Alvars and Nayanmars of the south. And from the northwest, the first Arab armies are pressing into Sindh.

The centre that this long classical era held with such effort will not be held again in the same way. When you open the timeline that follows, you will find Sarnath and the *Arthashastra* and Kanishka and Aryabhata and Harsha all spread across their years, waiting to be examined one by one. What I have tried to give you here is the thread that runs through them. The empire that began by teaching itself to conquer eventually learned to teach the world. That is what the cards are remembering.
