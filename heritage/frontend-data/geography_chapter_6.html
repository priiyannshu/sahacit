6 — What the rocks gave us

Everything that India has ever built out of metal was, at some point, dug out of a mine. The iron in the girders of a railway bridge, the copper in the wiring behind every light switch in every Indian city, the coal that heated the furnaces in which the steel was cast — all of it came out of rock, at a particular place, because a particular geological event concentrated it there, sometimes hundreds of millions of years ago. Before any of those things could exist, someone had to know where the rock had done that work. This chapter is about where, and why.

Start, briefly, with the three rock families. Igneous rocks form from cooling magma. Intrusive types like granite cool slowly underground and are coarse-grained, hard, light-coloured — the bedrock of continental interiors. Extrusive types like basalt cool quickly at the surface and are fine-grained, dark, dense — the Deccan Plateau is basalt at continental scale, hundreds of metres of lava poured out 66 million years ago and now the parent of the fertile black soils on which much of central India grows cotton. Sedimentary rocks form from material weathered off older rocks, deposited in layers by water or wind, and lithified over time. Sandstone, limestone, shale — the fossil-bearing, layered family. They carry fossils because organisms were buried in the sediment before it became rock. They also carry coal, and they host petroleum and natural gas, which is why all the oil companies drill into sedimentary basins. Metamorphic rocks are existing rocks transformed by heat and pressure without fully melting: marble from limestone, slate from shale, schist and gneiss from granites and other igneous rocks reorganised under deep mountain-building pressure. The rock cycle connects all three — igneous weathers to sediment that lithifies and may metamorphose and eventually melt to magma again. This has been running for billions of years. The chapter does not need to dwell on it longer than this, because the point now is what rocks yield — and what a rock yields depends entirely on its type, its age, and the specific geological events that affected it in its particular location.

A mineral is a naturally occurring inorganic substance with a definite chemical composition and characteristic physical properties. Over 3,000 minerals are known; only a handful form the bulk of common rocks, and only a smaller number still are concentrated into ores — deposits rich enough in a particular substance to be worth extracting. The geological processes that create ores are specific: volcanic heat driving mineral-rich fluids through fractures in rock and depositing metals as the fluids cool; ancient sea floors accumulating organic material that compresses under sediment into coal or cooks into petroleum; weathering concentrating residual heavy minerals in placer deposits on coastlines. Economic geography is, to a large extent, a map of where these processes happened. Minerals fall into three working categories: metallic (subdivided into ferrous — iron-based: iron ore, manganese, chromite — and non-ferrous: copper, lead, zinc, gold, bauxite, which is the ore of aluminium); non-metallic (limestone, mica, gypsum, dolomite, salt — not metals, but commercially essential); and energy minerals (coal, petroleum, natural gas, uranium, thorium).

The world mineral map is, in the first place, a map of where ancient geological events happened to make deposits accessible — not where modern economies needed them. Africa's Congo Basin holds most of the world's cobalt, now critical for battery technology; South Africa holds gold, platinum, chromite, and diamonds, inherited from specific crustal processes in one of Earth's oldest stable rock nuclei. Russia is a vast gas province and a major nickel producer. The Middle East — Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, the UAE — sits above roughly half the world's known conventional oil reserves, because the ancient Tethys Sea that once separated Gondwana from Laurasia deposited marine organic material in shallow basins that later became perfect pressure-cooking environments for petroleum. Australia holds immense iron-ore and coal deposits, plus bauxite. Chile dominates copper and is now central to lithium. China controls most of the world's rare-earth reserves. India inherits its own specific and uneven version of this planetary lottery.

India's mineral inheritance can be read as four belts, and what each belt holds is a function of the geological history of that piece of crust.

The North-eastern Plateau Belt — the Chotanagpur Plateau running across Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, and parts of West Bengal — is the densest mineral zone in India by almost any measure. It is a fragment of the Precambrian shield, the old Gondwana basement rock that has been sitting roughly in place for two billion years. In those billions of years, the slow geological processes that concentrate metals had enormous time to work. The result is a concentration that has no parallel elsewhere in India: most of the country's coal, major iron-ore deposits, manganese, mica, bauxite, chromite, and copper, all compressed into a relatively small area. Singhbhum in Jharkhand is the name to know for iron ore and copper. Hazaribagh and Kodarma are the mica heartland. The concentration is not accidental. It is the inheritance of an ancient shield where heat, pressure, and deep geological time produced ores that subsequent erosion exposed.

The South-western Plateau Belt — Karnataka and Goa — is a different face of the peninsula's geological inheritance. Iron ore at Kudremukh and the Bababudan Hills, at Bellary and Hospet; gold at Kolar — one of the world's deepest gold mines, now exhausted and closed since 2001, having produced ore for over a century — and still operational at Hutti in Karnataka; manganese spread across the plateau. Goa's iron ore has historically been exported through Mormugao port to steel mills in Japan and South Korea. The Bellary-Hospet belt became nationally notorious in the 2000s for a large-scale illegal-mining scandal, which eventually led to a Supreme Court-ordered moratorium. Geology produced the wealth; politics determined who captured it.

The North-western Belt runs along the Aravalli Range through Rajasthan into Gujarat. The Aravallis are among the oldest fold mountains on Earth — eroded remnants of ranges that predate the Himalayas by more than a billion years. This age is reflected in what the belt contains: not Gondwana-type heavy iron deposits, but non-ferrous metals formed by ancient hydrothermal processes. Copper at Khetri in Rajasthan, one of India's largest copper-mining complexes. Lead and zinc at Zawar — among the oldest continuously worked mines in the world, with evidence of ancient smelting — and at Rampura-Agucha, which is among the world's largest zinc-lead deposits. Gypsum, industrial minerals, and salt from Sambhar lake complete this belt. Gujarat adds the salt-pan complex of the Kutch coast, one of India's most important salt-producing zones.

A fourth zone — the Himalayan belt — is comparatively under-explored, partly because much of it lies in remote or border terrain, partly because the young Himalayas are still eroding, and the erosion that exposes deeper mineral deposits is in early stages. Copper, lead, zinc, cobalt, and tungsten have been identified in scattered pockets. The strategic future of this belt is not yet written.

Iron ore is where India is genuinely privileged by geology. India holds some of the world's largest haematite reserves and is among the top global producers. Singhbhum in Jharkhand; Bailadila in the Bastar hills of Chhattisgarh — a complex of fourteen deposits, much of it exported to Japan and South Korea under trade arrangements dating to the 1960s; Kudremukh in Karnataka; Odisha, which leads all states in production from deposits in the Keonjhar and Sundergarh districts. The two grades that matter are haematite (high iron content, found across the Gondwana-shield zones) and magnetite (harder to process, but found in Karnataka). Goa has historically been a significant exporter, though subject to moratoriums.

Coal is where India's Gondwana inheritance is most economically consequential. The forests that covered India when it was still part of Gondwana — roughly 250 million years ago, in the Permian period — were buried under accumulating sediment, compressed, and cooked by geothermal heat over vast stretches of time into the coal seams that now fuel the country. About 98% of India's coal reserves are Gondwana coal, sitting in the Damodar Valley (the great cluster: Raniganj in West Bengal, Jharia in Jharkhand — the largest coalfield in India by reserve, also notorious for underground fires burning for over a century — Bokaro and Giridih), and extending through the Mahanadi, Godavari, and Wardha basins. India is among the three largest coal producers in the world. And yet it imports millions of tonnes of coking coal every year, because Indian coal is overwhelmingly non-coking bituminous coal, and steelmaking requires coking coal that India's geology does not supply in adequate quantity. Tertiary coal — younger, formed 15–20 million years ago, lower in carbon content — occurs in Assam (Namchik-Namphuk and other deposits), Meghalaya, and Arunachal Pradesh. Every kilowatt of Indian thermal power — and thermal power produces roughly 55% of India's electricity — runs on a Permian forest, buried 250 million years ago. The deep-time register applies all the way into the power socket.

Mica deserves its own sentence. India was, for much of the twentieth century, the world's leading mica exporter. The Jharkhand-Bihar belt — centred on Hazaribagh and Kodarma — produces the highest-quality muscovite mica, used in the electrical and electronics industries for its insulating and heat-resistant properties. Nellore in Andhra Pradesh and Rajasthan (Jaipur–Bhilwara–Udaipur) are the other producing zones. Bauxite — the ore of aluminium — sits in laterite rocks, the iron-rich soils of intense tropical weathering. Odisha leads, with deposits in Kalahandi and Sambalpur; Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra, and Gujarat follow. Manganese — used in steel alloying and increasingly in battery technology — comes mainly from Odisha, Karnataka, and Madhya Pradesh.

Non-metallic minerals are less dramatic but no less load-bearing. Limestone — the raw material of cement and of the flux used in steelmaking — is found widely across Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, and Rajasthan. Its wide distribution matters because limestone is heavy and relatively cheap; you cannot transport it profitably over long distances, so having domestic sources close to cement plants and steel mills is economically significant. Gypsum, used in cement and fertiliser, comes mainly from Rajasthan. Salt is produced from two distinct sources: sea brine evaporation along the Gujarat and Tamil Nadu coasts (Gujarat's Kutch coast is the largest production zone), and lake brine from Sambhar lake in Rajasthan, the largest inland salt lake in India.

The Kerala and Tamil Nadu coastline carries a mineral inheritance that the examiner returns to insistently. The beach sands of this coastline contain a concentrated suite of heavy minerals: ilmenite and rutile (sources of titanium), zircon, sillimanite, and monazite. Monazite is the one to know. It contains thorium — and thorium is the long-term basis of India's nuclear energy strategy. Monazite also contains rare-earth elements that are becoming critical for modern technology. By law, monazite is a prescribed substance: only government bodies can process or export it. This coastline is not just a beach — it is a strategic resource held in trust.

India's petroleum story is, at its core, one of chronic scarcity relative to its needs. The first oil discovery was at Digboi in Assam in 1889 — one of the oldest continuously worked oilfields in the world. Assam remained India's oil province for most of the twentieth century, with Naharkatiya and Moran adding to the Digboi complex. Then, in 1974, the Mumbai High offshore field was discovered in the Arabian Sea, and it transformed the picture: Mumbai High became India's largest producing field, accounting for roughly 40% of domestic crude output at its peak, though its reserves have been declining for decades. The Krishna-Godavari basin — particularly the Reliance D6 block offshore Andhra Pradesh — emerged in the 2000s as a major natural gas province. Cambay basin in Gujarat, the Cauvery basin offshore Tamil Nadu, and the Barmer basin in Rajasthan round out the producing zones. Despite all of this, India imports roughly 85% of its crude oil. This is not a policy failure; it is the inheritance of a geology that is coal-rich and oil-poor. India simply did not receive the shallow warm Tethys basin sediments that the Middle East received. Natural gas follows largely the same geography — Mumbai High, the KG basin, Cambay, Tripura, Rajasthan — and GAIL has built a national pipeline network to move it from producing areas to industrial consumers.

The non-conventional energy picture is where India's geological inheritance shifts from deficit to advantage. India lies in a high-insolation belt — roughly 300 clear sunny days a year in its driest regions. Bhadla in Rajasthan, one of the world's largest solar parks, sits in the Thar Desert, where annual solar radiation is among the highest on Earth. Pavagada in Karnataka and Kurnool in Andhra Pradesh are the other headline parks. India co-founded the International Solar Alliance — headquartered at Gurugram — a grouping of tropical countries designed to accelerate solar deployment, a piece of energy diplomacy rooted in what India's geography makes possible. Wind energy is strongest along the southern peninsula coasts and the passes of the Western Ghats: Muppandal in Tamil Nadu is one of the largest wind farms in Asia; Jaisalmer in Rajasthan and the Kutch coast in Gujarat are the other major clusters. India has the fifth-largest installed wind capacity in the world.

Nuclear energy is where the thorium story comes full circle. India has uranium — at Jaduguda in Jharkhand (the main operational mine) and Tummalapalle in Andhra Pradesh — but not in very large quantities relative to the scale of programme it wants to run. India has therefore designed a three-stage nuclear programme specifically to eventually use thorium as fuel: the monazite sands of Kerala and Tamil Nadu hold one of the world's largest known thorium reserves, a long-term energy asset that current reactor designs cannot yet use but that a later stage of the programme is built around. This is, in the most literal sense, inherited geology being converted into national energy strategy across a multigenerational time horizon — a patience that only makes sense if you think in geological time. Geothermal energy surfaces at Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh and Puga Valley in Ladakh, but at scales that are not commercially significant yet. Tidal energy is theoretically feasible in the Gulf of Kutch and Gulf of Khambhat, where tidal ranges are large, but no commercial plant exists.

A structural fact that belongs here, even briefly. India's mineral wealth — the Gondwana coal, the Chotanagpur iron, the chromite and bauxite — is distributed almost entirely in the Scheduled V areas that the Constitution designates as tribal homelands. This is not coincidence. The Adivasi communities of central and eastern India have lived for centuries in forests growing on shallow soils above the ancient shield rock, precisely because those soils were too thin and nutrient-poor for intensive cultivation — which is itself because the hard basement rocks weathered without building deep soil profiles. The geological history that produced mineral wealth is the same geological history that made the land unattractive to settled agriculture and left it as forest and as the home of communities with customary forest rights. Mining these deposits therefore runs directly into questions about whose land it is and who benefits. The PESA Act 1996 and the Forest Rights Act 2006 are the legislature's attempts to manage this structural overlap — not resolving the underlying geometry, but drawing procedural lines around it. The environmental cost of extraction runs into the next-to-last chapter.

Minerals are what the rocks contain. Soils are what the rocks become when they weather — when centuries of rain, temperature change, biological action, and chemical work break the rock surface down into something that can hold water and support roots. The next chapter follows that slower transformation, and asks what the substrate, once weathered, permits to grow.
