1 — Where does the state's authority over us come from?

Begin, perhaps, with a fact so familiar that it has stopped being a fact at all. A state — call it India, call it any state of the modern type — claims the right to take your money in tax, to enlist your sons in its armies, to hold you in a cell if you disobey, and, ultimately, to end your life on its own scaffold. Most of you, reading this, have never met anyone who exercises this authority. You will never meet most of those over whom this authority is exercised alongside you. And yet the claim sticks. Tomorrow it will still stick. The interesting question, I think, is not what the claim says, but what is holding it up.

Let us, for a moment, set aside the conventional answer. The conventional answer is that the authority of the state is written down somewhere — in a constitution, in a statute, in some founding document — and so we obey because the document tells us we must. But this only displaces the problem. Why do we obey the document? What makes a document — paper and ink, or, increasingly, pixels — into a binding claim on the body of a stranger?

Three older answers have been given, in various combinations, across the long history of political life, and we may dismiss them quickly because none of them is, any longer, available to us.

There is the answer of force: the state rules because it has the means of violence and you do not. This is true as a description, but it is not an answer. To say that the strong rule because they can is not to justify their rule; it is only to report the geometry of the world.

There is the answer of the divine: God, or the gods, or the cosmic order, has installed these rulers, and to disobey them is to disobey something larger than them. This was, for centuries, the dominant answer in most of the inhabited world. But no constitution written in the twentieth century has tried this answer in earnest. The political theology has thinned to where it cannot bear weight.

And there is the answer of tradition: it has always been so, and what has always been so does not require justification. This answer is, I think, the most quietly powerful of the three — it does much of the work in our daily obedience without ever being articulated. But it cannot, by itself, constitute a state. It can only continue one. A people seeking to begin their authority over themselves cannot begin by appealing to the fact that they have already begun.

So we are left with the modern answer, the answer the twentieth century settled on — consent. We — the people — have agreed. We didn't, of course. You were not in the room. The room itself is, in most cases, an absurdly small room: in our case, three hundred or so persons sitting in New Delhi between 1946 and 1949, almost none of them directly elected by universal suffrage, several of them representing populations that had never voted for them in any meaningful sense. And yet from that room emerged the line that does the constitutive work of an entire nation:

We, the people of India, having solemnly resolved… do hereby… give to ourselves this Constitution.

Read this sentence slowly. Then read it again. Notice what it claims and what it cannot, in a literal sense, deliver. We, the people — but we were not consulted. Give to ourselves — but the giving was done by a few hundred. Solemnly resolved — but the resolution was the resolution of representatives whose representativeness was itself constituted by the very document they were producing.

This is not a flaw. This is the mechanism. The legitimacy of the modern state is a particular kind of fiction — a performative fiction, in the philosopher's sense. It does not describe a state of affairs that pre-exists it; it brings the state of affairs into being by declaring it. The line we the people makes a "we" out of populations that, until that line is uttered, are not yet a "we" at all. It produces what it claims to discover.

I think this is the deepest thing about the founding of any modern democracy, and it is especially astonishing in the Indian case, because the Indian case attempted this performance under conditions that the older theorists of consent had explicitly said were impossible. India in 1947 was overwhelmingly poor. It was overwhelmingly illiterate. It was deeply, structurally, hierarchically unequal. It was a society of plural peoples who had been, for two centuries, governed, in the modern sense — that is, treated as a population to be administered — by an outside power. To take a population that had been the object of governmentality, and ask it to become the subject of its own governmentality, in a single performative act, was a kind of philosophical audacity for which there was no precedent.

It is sometimes said, by Madhav Khosla among others, that India is "the most surprising democracy" because it was the first to begin in this condition and not collapse. I think this is correct, but the surprise is more specific. The surprise is that the performative held. The fiction did not, in the decades that followed, fall apart. It was challenged — in 1975 it was nearly extinguished, and we will return to that crisis — but it was not displaced. The line we the people continued, and continues, to do its work.

Now, here is the move that the chapter is asking you to hold in mind, because everything that follows depends on it. If the state's authority over you derives from your consent, then the state's authority is bounded — bounded, that is, by the terms of what was consented to. The lines drawn against the state by the founding document are not gifts that the state hands out, generously, to its subjects. They are the residue of the consent itself. Whatever you did not give up, you kept. This is the proper way to read the part of the document that draws those lines, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

There is a second thing to hold. If the authority of the state derives from a constituting act, then the right to re-constitute — to amend, to revise, to alter — is a distinct kind of authority, and one that the same document must somehow account for. Who, exactly, has the right to change what we the people once gave to ourselves? The original three hundred are dead; the people of 1950 are mostly dead. Does the present generation have the same constituting power as the founding one? This question is not posed openly until much later in the document — chapter nine, in our story — but the seed is here, and I want you to feel it.

There is a moment in the closing speech of the Constituent Assembly, delivered by B.R. Ambedkar on 25 November 1949, in which he said, in effect, that what the Constitution had achieved was political democracy — equality of vote, equality of participation, equality before the law — and that the more difficult task, the social and economic democracy that gives the political its substance, had only just begun. He warned the assembly, and through it the country, against hero-worship and against the assumption that the document on its own would do the work of a society. This warning is, I think, the most honest sentence delivered by any framer of any modern constitution, and it is worth keeping near you as you read what follows.

At midnight, on the 26th of January 1950, nothing visible changed. No army crossed a border. No flag was raised over a captured fort. And yet, by the operation of that single performative act — we, the people, give to ourselves — the apparatus of governmentality that had until that morning been the property of a foreign sovereign was, by the next, the property of a self-constituting people. Whether it remains so is, in some sense, the subject of every chapter that follows.

Hold the herd metaphor for a moment. A population, in the modern sense, is something a state governs — manages, counts, conducts. It is, in this respect, a flock; the state is, in this respect, a kind of shepherd. What India did at its founding was not abolish the relationship — no modern state has — but redraw the line of who, in the end, holds the staff. The shepherd, in a democracy, is supposed to belong to the flock. Whether that fiction can be sustained, whether the flock can in fact hold the shepherd accountable to itself, is the working problem of every chapter that follows.

So: where does the state's authority over us come from?

It comes from a sentence, written by three hundred people in a hall in New Delhi, and from the willingness of everyone since to act as though that sentence binds them.

This is a thinner foundation than it sounds. It is also the only foundation we have.
