6 — The Weight of the Question: Mughal Empire, 1526–1757

Begin with a question, not a date. Stand in the Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri in the year 1578 and imagine what you hear. On one side of the hall, a Sunni theologian is arguing at volume with a Shia theologian about the nature of the Imamate. On the other, a Jain monk from Kathiawar is waiting his turn to speak. A Portuguese Jesuit priest sits quietly in a corner — he has brought illustrated manuscripts of the Gospels as a gift, and the emperor is studying them with genuine curiosity. Akbar, son of Humayun, grandson of Babur, listens to all of it, asks his own questions, and comes to no public conclusion. His court historian Abu'l Fazl will later articulate what this listening amounts to as doctrine: sulh-i-kul — absolute peace, the right of all religions and schools of thought to exist and speak, provided only that none of them threatens the authority of the state.

This is not a concession. It is an answer to a question. The question the Mughal era poses — and it is a single question, asked in six different voices across 231 years — is this: what kind of state can govern a religiously plural population? Babur asks it by conquest. Humayun asks it through defeat. Akbar answers it with synthesis. Jahangir and Shah Jahan live inside the answer, clothing it in marble and light. Aurangzeb answers it with orthodoxy. The empire disintegrates because the second answer cannot hold what the first had built. The regional powers that fill the vacuum — the Marathas at Poona, the Nawabs at Murshidabad, the Nizams at Hyderabad — inherit its administrative bones and fight over its political soul.

That is the arc. Hold it in mind as we trace it.

The foothold and the exile

Babur arrives at Panipat on 21 April 1526 with perhaps twelve thousand soldiers and a piece of military technology that the Delhi Sultanate has never encountered in quite this form: field artillery combined with the tulughma — flanking divisions that curl around an enemy's wings and destroy it from behind. Ibrahim Lodi commands perhaps a hundred thousand men and five hundred elephants. He dies in the battle. The First Battle of Panipat is, among other things, the moment at which gunpowder warfare becomes decisive in north India. Babur then fights two more critical campaigns — Khanwa in 1527, where he defeats Rana Sanga of Mewar and takes the title Ghazi, and Chanderi in 1528 against Medini Rai. By his death in 1530, the Mughal foothold in Hindustan is real, though not yet secure.

Babur leaves behind the Baburnama — his memoir in Chaghtai Turkish — which describes Indian birds, fruit trees, and irrigation devices with the precise eye of a naturalist encountering an unfamiliar climate. He also introduces the chahar bagh, the four-quadrant formal garden with running water, which will become the defining spatial grammar of Mughal pleasure and mourning alike.

His son Humayun has the misfortune of facing an adversary of genius. Sher Shah Suri, an Afghan chieftain from Bihar who has studied the Mughals carefully, defeats Humayun twice — at Chausa in 1539, where Humayun barely escapes by swimming the Ganga on a water-carrier's inflated skin, and at Kanauj in 1540, where the rout is total. Humayun spends fifteen years in exile: first at the Safavid court of Shah Tahmasp in Iran, who provides an army in exchange for Shia allegiance, then rebuilding from Kabul. In those fifteen years, Sher Shah rules Hindustan with an administrative brilliance that Akbar will later study and copy: the standardised silver rupiya that makes cash revenue possible across the empire; horse-relay postal networks and sarais at regular intervals along a road from Bengal to the northwest — the spine that becomes the Grand Trunk Road; and a land-survey methodology that Todar Mal will systematise for Akbar a generation later. Sher Shah dies in 1545 from a gunpowder explosion; his Sur successors quarrel themselves into fragments; Humayun recaptures Delhi in 1555. He dies the following year, falling down a library staircase at Din Panah, leaving a thirteen-year-old on the throne of an empire barely secured.

The architecture of synthesis

The boy king is Akbar, crowned at Kalanaur in February 1556 while his father's body still lies at Delhi. His regent is Bairam Khan, a Turkoman veteran who fights the Second Battle of Panipat in November 1556 against Hemu — a Hindu general of remarkable ability who has declared himself Raja Vikramaditya and seized Delhi with fifty thousand cavalry and five hundred elephants. An arrow strikes Hemu in the eye during the battle; the leaderless army collapses. Akbar's real reign begins.

What Akbar builds over the next forty-nine years has no precise precedent: a state that institutionalises religious pluralism not as a concession but as its operating logic. The Mansabdari system — ranking every noble on a numerical scale of zat (personal rank) and sawar (cavalry obligation) — creates a composite nobility of Iranis, Turanis, Afghans, Indian Muslims, and Rajputs in which no single ethnic or religious group dominates. By design: Akbar's arithmetic is political. He builds Rajput alliances through marriage — Raja Bharmal Kachhwaha of Amber gives his daughter to Akbar in 1562, and she becomes the mother of Jahangir — and through military partnership: Raja Man Singh, reaching the highest Rajput mansab rank of 7,000, commands armies from Haldighati to Bengal to Kabul. The empire is divided into twelve subas (provinces), each with a subadar for political-military governance and an independent diwan for revenue — a deliberate check-and-balance that keeps no single provincial officer omnipotent.

Akbar's expansion is not gentle. At Chittor in 1568, he lays siege for six months and orders the killing of thirty thousand people when the fortress falls — including the defenders Jaimal Rathore and Patta Sisodiya, whose courage so impresses him that he commissions life-size stone statues of them, seated on elephants, at the gate of Agra Fort. Every court visitor would see them. Notice what the gesture says: even a conquered enemy, if he fights with honour, earns a place in the empire's visual memory. At Haldighati in 1576, Maharana Pratap of Mewar refuses to submit. Notice what this battle actually is: Man Singh, a Rajput, commands the Mughal side; Afghans fight in Pratap's van. It is not a Hindu-Muslim war. It is a contest over submission to one imperial order. Pratap switches to guerrilla warfare and is never fully defeated; his son Amar Singh submits only to Jahangir in 1615, and the settlement is honourable — no personal attendance at court required, Chittor returned as hereditary watan jagir. Mewar's long resistance and its final terms define the outer limit of what the Mughal synthesis could demand and still hold together.

Todar Mal's revenue survey — the zabt or dahsala bandobast of 1580, building on Sher Shah's earlier framework — measures crop yields, prices, and cultivated areas over ten years and fixes revenue in cash. It is one of the most sophisticated fiscal systems the pre-modern world produces. Abu'l Fazl's Ain-i-Akbari, the encyclopaedic third book of the Akbarnama completed in 1598, records the result: Agra produces thirty-nine crop varieties, Delhi forty-three, Bengal fifty varieties of rice alone. The Akbarnama itself — three volumes, thirteen years in the making — is the empire's self-portrait: a deliberately composed image of just, pluralistic sovereignty receiving divine light (farr-i-izadi) through the Timurid-Mughal lineage.

The ideological scaffolding for all this is sulh-i-kul. The Ibadat Khana, built at Fatehpur Sikri in 1575, gives it institutional form: open debates among theologians, Jains, Parsis, and Jesuit priests. When the debates produce more acrimony than illumination — the ulama are reportedly shouting at one another in the emperor's presence — Akbar closes them in 1582 and promulgates the Din-i-Ilahi: a Sufi-style personal order of initiates, bound to him through spiritual loyalty. Only eighteen formally join; Birbal is the only major Hindu nobleman among them. It is not a new religion. It is a political instrument for concentrating personal loyalty above sectarian faction. The Mahzar of 1579, drafted by Shaikh Mubarak and signed by leading ulama, gives Akbar power to arbitrate conflicting interpretations of Islamic law — not a claim to prophecy, as older historians sometimes put it, but a device for managing the sectarian conflicts that were tearing his administration apart.

The cultural dimension of this synthesis is inseparable from it. Akbar's court painter workshop commissions the Hamzanama — 1,400 illustrated folios produced over fifteen years — which fuses Persian miniature technique with Indian naturalism and is the foundational project of the Mughal painting tradition. Court musician Mian Tansen, summoned from Gwalior and one of the nine jewels (navaratna) of Akbar's court, creates the Senia gharana of Hindustani classical music; the ragas he develops — Darbari Kanada, Miyan ki Malhar — are still performed. The Maktab Khana translation bureau renders the Mahabharata, Ramayana, and Atharva Veda into Persian. Fatehpur Sikri, Akbar's capital built around the tomb of Shaikh Salim Chishti, borrows its architectural vocabulary from Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Bengal simultaneously — a building programme that enacts in stone what the Mansabdari system enacts in political sociology.

In 1604, one year before Akbar dies, Guru Arjan compiles the Guru Granth Sahib — a scripture containing compositions of Nanak and his successors alongside verses by the Muslim poet Shaikh Farid and the nirgun bhakti saints Kabir and Namdev. It is the document of an India that sulh-i-kul, at its best, makes possible: a single sacred text holding the devotions of the Punjab in multiple voices, without demanding that any of them renounce the others.

The visual zenith

Akbar dies in 1605 and Jahangir takes the throne. He is a weaker political figure than his father but a sharper observer. His Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri records natural history with the precision of a scientific journal; he personally directs his painters toward portraiture and wildlife study. His court painter Ustad Mansur — titled Nadir-ul-Asr, Wonder of the Age — produces studies of a Siberian crane and an African zebra that rank among the finest natural-history illustration in any tradition. The institution Jahangir builds around this is the Chain of Justice — a gold chain, thirty yards long, with sixty bells, strung from Agra Fort to the river bank, so that any subject denied redress by the courts could ring it and appeal directly to the emperor. The symbol matters as much as the mechanism: Mughal sovereignty presents itself as personally accountable.

The political apex of Jahangir's reign belongs to Nur Jahan, daughter of the Persian émigré Mirza Ghiyas Beg who had become the emperor's chief finance officer. She marries Jahangir in 1611 and within a decade issues farmans under her own seal and has silver coins struck in her name. Her father's marble tomb on the Yamuna — the Itmad-ud-Daulah, built by Nur Jahan between 1622 and 1628 — is the first Mughal building to use pietra dura inlay on white marble. It is, in every stylistic sense, the prototype of the Taj Mahal that Shah Jahan will build across the same river a generation later. Sir Thomas Roe, England's ambassador from 1615 to 1619, negotiates trading rights for the East India Company and finds, to his visible bewilderment, a court where a woman exercises effective authority.

But the reign of Jahangir also opens the first wound in the Sikh relationship with Mughal power. Guru Arjan, the fifth Sikh Guru, had built the Harmandir Sahib — the Golden Temple — at Amritsar in 1589 with a foundation stone reportedly laid by the Qadiri sufi Miyan Mir: an act of deliberate cross-tradition solidarity. In 1604 he compiled the Adi Granth at Amritsar, the scripture that will later become the Guru Granth Sahib. In 1606, he gives shelter to the rebel Prince Khusrau. Jahangir summons him, demands he expunge a hymn thought to reference the Prophet, and when Guru Arjan refuses, has him tortured and executed at Lahore. This is the first Sikh martyrdom. His son Guru Hargobind responds by introducing the doctrine of Miri-Piri — the Guru's temporal authority alongside his spiritual one — and begins the militarisation that will run through to Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa a century later. The wound opens here, in the middle of the visual zenith.

Shah Jahan takes the throne in 1628 and rules for thirty years as the empire's most extravagant patron. The Taj Mahal, commissioned in 1632 for his dead wife Mumtaz Mahal and completed in 1643, is the chahar bagh-and-mausoleum grammar that Babur introduced, carried to its absolute limit in white marble. The Peacock Throne, installed in the Diwan-i-Khas of his new Red Fort in Delhi in 1635 — solid gold, 1,150 kilograms, set with 230 kilograms of precious stones including the Koh-i-Noor — costs roughly twice the Taj Mahal. Shahjahanabad, Old Delhi, is completed in 1648, its Jama Masjid the largest mosque in India. The Badshah Nama of Abdul Hamid Lahori, modelled on the Akbarnama, records this reign in the same self-aggrandising register. In 1636, Shah Jahan achieves through diplomacy what Akbar's campaigns could not complete: a formal Deccan treaty subordinating Bijapur and Golconda as Mughal vassals while Ahmadnagar is absorbed directly. The Deccan is, notionally, fully imperial. Holding it will prove to be the era's fatal project.

The reversal

Shah Jahan's eldest son and designated heir is Dara Shukoh — a Sufi-influenced scholar who translates fifty-two Upanishads into Persian as the Sirr-i-Akbar and writes the Majma-ul-Bahrain (Confluence of Two Seas), a text arguing the compatibility of Sufi and Vedantic metaphysics. His death, charged with apostasy and executed in 1659 after the war of succession, forecloses the one alternative path the Mughal tradition had to Aurangzeb's orthodoxy. The body that Akbar built might have passed into the hands of a ruler who continued it. It does not.

Aurangzeb comes to power in 1658 by defeating his brothers at Samugarh and imprisoning Shah Jahan in Agra Fort — the old emperor will spend his last eight years reportedly watching the Taj Mahal across the river. Aurangzeb rules for forty-nine years — the longest Mughal reign — and leaves the empire territorially at its maximum and institutionally at its worst. Hold both facts together.

I want to be precise about what Aurangzeb is. He is not a personal pathology. He is a deliberate political theology — the mirror image of Akbar's. He believes the synthesis built on theological compromise has hollowed the empire's foundations, and that only orthodox Sunni governance can restore them. He reimmposes the jizya on non-Muslims in April 1679 — Delhi's Hindu merchants stage public sit-ins, blocking the imperial procession — and does not relent. He destroys the Vishwanath temple at Kashi in 1669 and the Keshav Deo temple at Mathura in 1670. He appoints muhtasibs — morality-enforcement officers — in each province to regulate public behaviour under the shariat. He commissions the Fatawa-i-Alamgiri, a thirty-volume Hanafi legal compendium, which becomes the subcontinent's most authoritative Islamic legal reference for generations.

The theological rival to the Chishti-inflected sulh-i-kul had been building since Akbar's own time. Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi — the Naqshbandi sufi who titles himself Mujaddid Alf-i-Thani, Renewer of the Second Millennium — had written for decades to Jahangir's nobles arguing that Wahdat-ul-Shuhud (unity of witnessing, in which God remains forever distinct from creation) must replace the Chishti Wahdat-ul-Wujud (unity of being, in which the divine pervades all things). Aurangzeb's religious policy is, among other things, a Naqshbandi victory over a Chishti synthesis — a doctrinal contest that had been running underground through the courts of Jahangir and Shah Jahan finally surfacing as state policy.

The costs are generational. Aurangzeb's execution of Guru Tegh Bahadur at Chandni Chowk in 1675 — tradition holds the Guru had intervened against forced conversions in Kashmir — radicalises the Sikh community in a way no prior pressure had achieved. Guru Gobind Singh responds by founding the Khalsa at Anandpur on Baisakhi 1699: five volunteers from diverse castes drink amrit from a single steel bowl, take the surname Singh, and become the nucleus of a Sikh political identity that is now militantly self-defensive. Two of Guru Gobind Singh's sons die fighting Mughal armies at Chamkaur in 1705; two younger sons, aged seven and nine, are bricked alive at Sirhind by the local governor Wazir Khan. By 1707, the Sikh community is no longer primarily a devotional movement. It is a political force with sovereign aspirations.

The Deccan campaigns become the empire's slow haemorrhage. Bijapur falls in 1685, Golconda in 1687, ending the Deccan Sultanates as political entities — but removing these buffer states releases the Marathas rather than containing them. Aurangzeb never finds a way to settle with Sambhaji, Shivaji's eldest son; he executes him in 1689, and Satish Chandra observes that this made Maratha resistance all-pervasive rather than killing it. Rajaram holds out at Jinji on the east coast for eight years. Aurangzeb is personally present in the Deccan from 1698 until his death, an emperor in his eighties directing sieges against invisible guerrilla fighters. He dies at Aurangabad in March 1707 having done what Satish Chandra enumerates as his most consequential misjudgments: refusing to befriend Shivaji when Mirza Raja Jai Singh urged it in 1666; executing Sambhaji when he might have negotiated; prolonging the Marwar-Mewar war for two decades; and executing Guru Tegh Bahadur in a way that ensured the Sikh community would arm. The empire is territorially the largest it has ever been. It is institutionally exhausted.

The body that only synthesis can hold

The Maratha story is the counter-history of this era, and it runs from beginning to end. Malik Ambar — an Ethiopian-born slave who rose through the Deccani nobility to become Peshwa of Ahmadnagar in 1601, holding the Mughals at bay for a quarter-century through large-scale Maratha cavalry partnerships — is the direct precursor of the guerrilla model Shivaji will perfect. Chand Bibi, regent of both Bijapur and Ahmadnagar at different moments, personally commands the defence of Ahmadnagar against Akbar's forces in 1595, extracting a negotiated withdrawal through sheer competence, before her own nobles murder her in 1600. These are not peripheral stories. The Deccan has its own synthesis question, running in parallel to the Mughal one: Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah of Golconda founds Hyderabad in 1591 and builds the Char Minar as its ceremonial centrepiece — a city of Dakhini Urdu poetry alongside Telugu and Persian, diamond wealth, and its own cultural confidence entirely independent of Delhi.

Shivaji builds on all of this. Born around 1627, raised at Poona under his mother Jijabai's fierce instruction in dharma and governance, he seizes hill forts in his teens, kills Bijapur's general Afzal Khan with a concealed bagh-nakh during a formal embrace at Pratapgarh in 1659, raids Surat in 1664, negotiates the Treaty of Purandar in 1665 (surrendering twenty-three forts to Mirza Raja Jai Singh), escapes Aurangzeb's court at Agra in 1666 after being publicly humiliated, sacks Surat again in 1670, and is crowned Chhatrapati at Raigarh on 6 June 1674. His Ashtapradhan — eight ministers managing separate governmental functions — is a rational bureaucratic system. His revenue base of chauth (a quarter of territorial land revenue) and sardeshmukhi (an additional tenth) is a systematic extraction mechanism, not mere raiding. He does not build a plunder operation; he builds a state.

When Shivaji dies in 1680, the question is whether the Marathas are a political entity or a raiding confederacy. Sambhaji answers it by fighting nine more years before capture and execution in 1689; Rajaram answers it by holding Jinji for eight years; his widow Tarabai answers it by continuing the war in her son's name after Rajaram dies in 1700. By the time Aurangzeb dies, the Marathas have outlasted him. Shahu — Sambhaji's son, raised at Aurangzeb's own court as a mansabdar — is released in 1707 and defeats Tarabai at the Battle of Khed in 1708. His Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath, appointed in 1713, begins building the Maratha Confederacy that Bajirao I will then expand dramatically: north of the Vindhyas into Malwa, Bundelkhand, and Gujarat, so that by 1737 Maratha cavalry raids Delhi itself. The two thrones of the era — Mughal at Delhi, Maratha at Poona — have by 1740 exchanged effective political weight. What had begun as the Mughal empire's body is now contested terrain.

What the era leaves us

Aurangzeb's eleven successors in the next fifty years are emperors in name. The structural cause of their weakness is the jagirdari crisis: the gap between assessed revenue and actual collection has been widening through the seventeenth century; the number of mansabdars has exploded from 2,000 under Akbar to over 11,000 under Aurangzeb; the jagirs to pay them have not grown to match. Khafi Khan, a contemporary historian, describes the situation as "one pomegranate among a hundred sick." Nadir Shah of Iran walks into this vacuum in 1739 and walks out with the Peacock Throne, the Koh-i-Noor, and the remains of Mughal prestige. He leaves behind a Delhi — gutted, its notables fled to the protection of Jat ruler Suraj Mal at Bharatpur — that will take a generation to recover. Ahmad Shah Abdali raids repeatedly through the 1750s.

"All the Gold and Silver which circulates throughout the World at last Centres here," wrote the Italian traveller Giovanni Careri in the 1690s, observing the flow of American silver through European and Ottoman trade networks into the Mughal exchange economy.

The observation is accurate and ironic in equal measure. The monetary system that Sher Shah's rupiya and Akbar's zabt revenue settlement made possible had, by Careri's time, made Mughal India one of the world's largest economies — the foremost producer of cotton textiles, its muslin a craze in England, its hundis honoured in Antwerp and Cairo. The institutional machinery that could distribute that wealth is collapsing precisely because it could not survive the question Aurangzeb answered wrongly.

The autonomous successor states are not catastrophes. They are transformations. Murshid Quli Khan's Bengal transfers jagirdars to Orissa and reassesses revenues with impressive discipline. Saadat Khan's Awadh controls the richest Ganga plain trade route and combines the offices of subadar, diwan, and faujdar under single authority for efficiency. Asaf Jah's Hyderabad founds a dynasty that will outlast the Mughals by nearly two centuries. Sawai Jai Singh II of Amber builds Jaipur — a planned city — and erects five astronomical observatories, the Jantar Mantars, at Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Mathura, and Varanasi. The Sikh jathas consolidate across Punjab into misls and begin striking sovereign coins in 1765. These are not merely fragments of a broken empire; they are new political forms growing in its soil.

But they do not cohere. They will be defeated one by one. The Battle of Plassey in 1757 — which closes the Mughal era and opens the era of Company rule — is won by an English trading company against the Nawab of Bengal: the moment when one of the empire's provincial successors falls to a power the empire itself had never fully reckoned with. The East India Company has been accumulating military capacity in the interstices of the Mughal order since its first factory at Surat in 1608. It begins at the eastern edge. It does not stop there.

When you open the timeline that follows, you will see all of this arranged as concept cards across 231 years and thirteen stages — the political dynasts, the battles, the administrative systems, the painters and musicians and sufis and Sikh Gurus and Deccan sultanas who fill the era's margins and its centre alike. What I have tried to give you here is the question they are all answering: what kind of state can govern a plural population? Akbar's body holds the synthesis together for a century. Aurangzeb breaks it from within, and the body scatters. The cards are the shards; this chapter was the vessel.
