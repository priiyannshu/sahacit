4 — The Age of Productive Fragmentation: Temple, Bhakti, and the Ghurid Horizon

Let me stand you inside the Brihadeshwara temple at Thanjavur in the year 1010 CE. The vimana above you is sixty metres tall — one of the tallest structures of its age anywhere on earth. The capstone alone weighs eighty tonnes; to lift it, Rajaraja I's engineers built an earthen ramp beginning four miles from the site, so gradual it could be mistaken for a natural hill, and hauled the stone to the top. Six hundred people maintained this temple: dancing women, musicians, accountants, tailors, goldsmiths, teachers. Revenue from villages across the Kaveri delta — and from Tamil-speaking settlements in Sri Lanka that the Chola army had recently taken — was assigned to its upkeep. Inside the sanctum, the inner ambulatory is lined with thirty representations of Shiva as Tripurantaka, Shiva the destroyer of three cities, each one a claim of Chola conquest over a defeated enemy encoded in stone theology.

This is the building that history textbooks call regional.

We are in the era that separates the classical period — the Guptas, Harsha's brief empire, the age of Kalidasa and Aryabhata — from the Delhi Sultanate. The standard framing runs something like this: after Harsha's death in 647 CE, India fragments. The imperial centre dissolves. What follows is five and a half centuries of regional kingdoms exhausting themselves in futile contests, until the Ghurid conquerors arrive in 1192 and impose a new order. In this framing the early medieval is an interregnum — neither the classical peak it follows nor the Sultanate achievement it precedes. A long, dark waiting room.

I want you to refuse this frame. The early medieval is not an interregnum. It is the era in which the classical centre — which had imposed a certain imperial uniformity across the subcontinent — fragments into regional formations, and each region develops a depth, a particularity, a civilisational ambition that the imperial centre had not made room for. The south builds the most powerful maritime empire in pre-modern Indian history. The Deccan produces the apex of Indian rock-cut architecture. The north carves Khajuraho. And across all of these, a new religious movement reconfigures Hindu devotion from the bottom up — not through ritual authority but through the force of personal love, sung in Tamil and Kannada and Bengali by women and low-caste men and converted Jains.

The frame I want you to hold is productive fragmentation. And the institution that holds this era together — better than any political power manages to — is the temple.

The fight for a city no one needed

For roughly two centuries, from the late 8th century to the early 10th, three dynasties fought an exhausting, inconclusive war for control of a single city: Kanauj, on the banks of the Ganga. The Gurjara-Pratiharas from Rajasthan and Gujarat, the Pala dynasty from Bengal, and the Rashtrakutas from the Deccan — each took Kanauj, lost it, and took it again, without any of them managing to hold it as a functioning imperial capital. The Arab geographer Sulaiman, visiting in the 9th century, described the Pratihara king Bhoja I as the greatest enemy of the Arabs and a ruler of extraordinary military power. Rashtrakuta armies from the south sacked the city more than once. Pala king Dharmapala (770-810) installed a puppet at Kanauj and held a grand durbar there — then was driven out. None of it produced an empire.

What were they fighting for? Kanauj was the symbolic seat of north Indian imperial legitimacy — where the Guptas had held court, where Harsha had ruled. To hold Kanauj was to claim to be the successor of that classical authority. But the authority was no longer real. Each of these three powers was, in its home region, building something more durable than what the city symbolised.

The Rashtrakutas, while contesting Kanauj, were producing one of the greatest acts of architectural ambition in Indian history. Krishna I, ruling from the Deccan in the late 8th century, commissioned the Kailashanatha temple at Ellora. The construction was unusual: instead of building up from a foundation, the craftsmen carved top-down from a volcanic basalt cliff, removing two hundred thousand tonnes of rock to reveal a temple that had, apparently, always been inside it. The result — Cave 16 at Ellora, at the heart of the thirty-four cave complex spanning Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain traditions — is a monolithic Shiva temple larger than the Parthenon: main shrine, Nandi pavilion, subsidiary shrines, and a stunning relief of Ravana shaking Mount Kailasha. The king who built it was simultaneously fighting for Kanauj. You would not know this from the temple. The temple is not about Kanauj. It is about what Deccan civilisation could do when it turned its ambition inward.

The Rashtrakuta king Amoghavarsha I (814-878), who established the new capital at Manyakheta, wrote the Kavirajamarga — the oldest extant work on Kannada poetics — establishing literary Kannada as a formal tradition. He was simultaneously conducting wars against the Pratiharas, the Palas, and the Eastern Chalukyas. A ruler as comfortable with a treatise on metre as with a cavalry campaign. This is the early medieval mode: the regional court as a site of intensive cultural production, not in spite of the political fragmentation but because of it. Each court needed to generate its own legitimacy, and legitimacy required literature, architecture, and the patronage of the sacred.

In 712 CE, meanwhile, the Umayyad general Muhammad bin Qasim had captured Sindh — the first major Islamic political intrusion into the subcontinent. He took Debal, defeated the Hindu king Dahar at the battle of Raor, and annexed Alor and Brahmanabad. Islam had arrived in India. But notice the limits: Sindh remained a precarious outpost. The Pratihara king Nagabhata I repelled Arab advances into Rajasthan in the 8th century, and for the next three centuries, the Arab presence in Sindh had no further consequence for the Ganga plains or the Deccan. The sea-change came later, with different conquerors.

The regional depth — North, East, West

In the north, the Chandella dynasty of Bundelkhand built Khajuraho. Twenty-five surviving temples, constructed between 950 and 1050 CE, their exteriors covered with horizontal bands of sculpture so dense that scholars have spent careers cataloguing them. The Kandariya Mahadeva, dedicated to Shiva, is the finest — a cluster of ascending shikharas producing, from a distance, the effect of a mountain range in stone. The erotic mithuna sculptures for which Khajuraho is famous abroad are not the point; they occupy only a portion of the exterior surface. The point is the Nagara architectural system developed here to the furthest limit of its own internal logic: the rekha-prasad shikhara with its multiple urushringas clustered around a central spire, every wall surface articulated into ratha projections and counter-projections, the entire building charged with a sculptural energy that has no equivalent in medieval architecture elsewhere in the world.

In the west, the Solanki dynasty of Gujarat built Modhera: a Surya temple completed in 1026-27, aligned so that the equinoctial sunrise strikes the deity directly. The surya-kund stepwell adjacent to the temple, with its hundred and eight small shrines, is itself a feat of geometric precision. Simultaneously, the Solanki minister Vimal Shah was building the Vimal Vasahi at Dilwara on Mount Abu — Jain marble temples of such intricate carving that every surface resolves, on examination, into a filigree of lotus flowers and celestial beings. The Paramara scholar-king Bhoja of Dhara (1010-1055), meanwhile, was writing treatises on architecture, yoga, poetics, and medicine — the most polymath ruler of the era, founding the Bhojshala at Dhara as a centre of learning. The Rashtrakuta-funded Jain pilgrimage site at Shravana Belagola in Karnataka had, in 983, produced the Gommateshvara statue: a 17.5-metre monolithic figure of the Jain tirthankar Bahubali, carved in a single granite outcrop, anointed in the mahamastakabhisheka ceremony every twelve years.

In the east, the Pala dynasty was the late champion of Indian Buddhism. Founded by Gopala — elected by the people, according to the Khalimpur copper plate, to rescue Bengal from the chaos of matsya-nyaya, the law of the fish — the Palas were consistent Buddhist patrons for three centuries. Dharmapala (770-810) held a durbar at Kanauj and founded two of the greatest Buddhist institutions of the medieval world: the Vikramashila monastic university at Antichak in Bihar, and the Somapuri Mahavihara at Paharpur in Bangladesh — the largest mahavihara south of the Himalayas. Nalanda, already ancient by the Pala period, continued its eight-hundred-year existence as the greatest centre of learning in Asia; Dharmapala and Devapala were among its patrons. Devapala (810-850) extended Pala power from the Himalayas to the Vindhyas. This was the milieu that produced the Pala school of painting: sinuous palm-leaf manuscript miniatures of Vajrayana Buddhist deities — sinuous lines, subdued tones, a deliberately meditative aesthetic — the work of artists named Dhimman and Vitapala, now preserved in Tibetan monasteries where the tradition they served survived the destruction of its Indian heartland.

Then, from 1000 CE, Mahmud of Ghazni began his raids. He came seventeen times, always plundering and retreating — his interest was treasure, not governance. His most famous target was the Somnath temple in Gujarat (1026). But his cultural afterlife in India is more interesting than his military one. Among the scholars he brought to Ghazni was Al-Biruni, seized from Khwarizm in 1017: a polymath who used his forced residence near the Punjab to learn Sanskrit, contact Brahmanical scholars, and produce the Kitab-ul-Hind — eighty chapters on Indian religion, philosophy, science, and society, written in Arabic, still the finest outside account of pre-modern India. The Ghaznavids absorbed the Punjab into their empire and wiped out the Pratiharas as a political force. The Ganga plains, the Deccan, and the south remained untouched. Mahmud was a raider, not a founder.

The southward turn

For the first half of this era, the civilisational centre of gravity is not in the north. It is in the Tamil south. And the dynasty that best embodies this southward turn is the Chola.

The Pallavas, ruling from Kanchipuram, had preceded them. Narasimhavarman I Mahamalla — the great wrestler — built at Mamallapuram the five Pancha Rathas: monolithic rock-cut temples named after the Pandavas, each a separate architectural experiment in plan and roof form, the Dharmaraja ratha with its terraced pyramidal tower, the Bhima ratha with its barrel-vaulted roof, the small Draupadi ratha with its hut-shaped curvilinear top. Alongside them, the Descent of the Ganga relief at Mamallapuram is the largest open-air carving in India, carved in living granite. His successor Narasimhavarman II Rajasimha built the Shore Temple on the Coromandel coast and the Kailashanatha at Kanchipuram — the first mature structural Dravida temples, moving the tradition from rock-cut into dressed stone. The Pallavas invented the Dravida grammar; the Cholas inherited it and extended it into the 11th-century achievement at Thanjavur.

The Chola rise begins in the mid-9th century when Vijayalaya captures Tanjavur from the Muttaraiyar chieftains. Aditya I ends Pallava political power in 893. What follows is three centuries of Chola ascendancy. Rajaraja I, who comes to power in 985, is the fulcrum. He destroys Anuradhapura in Sri Lanka and establishes a Chola province in the north of the island. He conquers the Maldives. He initiates, from 1011, the most exhaustive land survey any Indian dynasty had attempted — every field in the Kaveri delta measured, classified, assessed. And he builds the Brihadeshwara: a 60-metre vimana, six hundred employees, revenue from Sri Lanka assigned to its maintenance, the inner ambulatory lined with thirty Tripurantaka Shivas linking theology to conquest. The building we stood inside at the start of this chapter.

His son Rajendra I carries the empire further than any Indian ruler before or after into the maritime world. In 1025, he despatches a naval expedition to Sri Vijaya — the Shrivijaya kingdom controlling the Straits of Malacca — to ensure Chola access to the Indian Ocean trade routes. He builds a new capital at Gangaikondacholapuram with a second Brihadishvara, unfinished. Between 1015 and 1077, four Chola tribute missions travel to the Song emperors in China. This is the only Indian maritime imperial presence in the pre-modern Indian Ocean, and it is a thalassocracy driven not by ideology but by commerce: control of the sea-lanes that carried pepper, silk, sandalwood, and porcelain between the Persian Gulf and the South China Sea.

The Chola governance system deserves to stand alongside its military achievements. The ur (village assembly), the sabha (Brahmanical settlement council), the nagaram (merchant assembly), and the nadu (locality unit) formed a layered self-governing structure. The Uttaramerur inscription from the 10th century describes, in unusual detail, how the village assembly was constituted: eligibility criteria, a lottery using palm-leaf tickets, rotating membership, separate committees for irrigation, gardens, and temples. One of the earliest documented examples of representative local governance in the subcontinent. The Ayyavole merchant guild — the Five Hundred of Aihole — operated across South Asia and into Southeast Asia; their inscriptions have been found in Sumatra.

I want you to notice the temple at the centre of all of this. The Chola temple is not only a religious building. It is an economic node — endowed with land, employing hundreds, financing trade through accumulated wealth. It is a political statement — those Tripurantaka Shivas in the ambulatory are theology-as-conquest. And it is a cultural patronage site. The Chola bronzes — Shiva Nataraja, Vishnu, Uma, the great Nayanar saints cast in copper by the lost-wax method — are among the finest metalwork produced in any civilisation. They emerged from these temples. They processed through these streets.

Bhakti, Vedanta, and the grammars of the divine

The Alvars and Nayanars begin in the 6th century. Twelve Alvars — devotees immersed in Vishnu — travel Tamil Nadu singing hymns in the vernacular Tamil. Their four thousand compositions are eventually compiled as the Nalayira Divya Prabandham, which Sri Vaishnava tradition calls the Tamil Veda, chanted in temples alongside Sanskrit recitation. Among the Alvars is Andal, who saw herself as the bride of Vishnu; her verses are sung at Tamil Vaishnava weddings to this day. Nammalvar, chief of the Alvars, wrote the Tiruvaymoli — considered the Tamil equivalent of the Sama Veda — using the akam conventions of interior love poetry to express the intimacy of devotee and deity. Among the sixty-three Nayanars is Appar, who converted from Jainism to Shaivism and whose hymns insist that personal devotion matters more than caste, ritual, or ceremonial purity. The three great Nayanars — Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar — compose the Tevaram, which the Chola kings incorporate into temple worship, institutionalising bhakti as official religious culture.

I want you to notice what Bhakti is doing, structurally. It is not rejecting Hindu tradition; it is reconfiguring the point of access. The Vedic system required Sanskrit, priestly mediation, ritual precision, caste status. Bhakti says: the deity is reachable through personal love, expressed in the language you were born to speak. The theological implications unfold across three great philosopher-saints.

Shankaracharya, born in Kerala around 788 CE, systematises Advaita Vedanta: brahman, pure consciousness, without attributes, is the sole reality; the apparent plurality of the world arises from maya, ignorance born of avidya. The individual soul and brahman are identical. Liberation is realisation of that identity. He tours the subcontinent debating scholars, establishing four mathas at the cardinal points — Sringeri, Dwaraka, Puri, Joshimath. His critics call him a hidden Buddhist; the charge captures something true, since his negation of plurality echoes the Madhyamika school. But Shankara would insist the goal is different: not the extinguishing of the self in nirvana, but the recognition that the self was never separate from brahman to begin with.

Ramanuja, born around 1017 CE, refuses Shankara's nirguna brahman. His Vishishtadvaita — qualified non-dualism — argues that brahman has attributes, that individual souls and matter are real modes of brahman rather than illusions, and that bhakti to a personal deity is the highest path. The consequence is significant: Ramanuja opens Vaishnava devotion to all castes, arguing that the Nalayira Divya Prabandham belongs alongside the Sanskrit Vedas. He hagiographically opens temple entry to untouchable devotees. When his disciple Ramananda later carries the Sri Vaishnava message north, and when Kabir and Mirabai and Chaitanya arise from that lineage, the theological infrastructure is substantially Ramanuja's.

Madhvacharya, whose Dvaita Vedanta crystallises in the early 13th century at the era's close, takes the opposite position from both: God, souls, and matter are three eternally distinct categories. Shankara, Ramanuja, Madhva — three Karnataka-connected philosophers, three different resolutions of the same question about the relationship between the individual and the ultimate. The range of the question, and the seriousness with which this era pursued it, has no parallel in any other civilisation of the same period.

Basavanna (1106-68) takes the reconfiguration furthest in the social direction. A Brahmin minister in the Chalukya court of Bijjala, he founds the Lingayat or Virashaiva movement in Karnataka. The Lingayats wear a small linga around their necks and worship Shiva directly, without priests, temples, or caste mediation. They compose vachanas — short, fierce, direct poems in Kannada — that reject caste, ritualism, idol worship, and Brahmanical authority. They bury their dead rather than cremating them. They encourage widow remarriage. This is not devotion as an escape from the social order; it is devotion as a dismantling of it. Basavanna's anubhava mantapa at Kalyana — a gathering place for devotees of all castes — is one of the first experiments in what we might now call a community of equals organised around spiritual experience rather than birth.

The three temple grammars crystallise and mature across this same era. The Nagara style of north India: a curvilinear shikhara topped by an amalaka, fully developed by the 8th century, with three mature sub-schools — Odisha/Kalinga (the almost vertical rekhadeul shikhara of the Lingaraja at Bhubaneshwar), Khajuraho/Chandel (the clustered multiple-shikhara system of the Kandariya Mahadeva), and Solanki/Maru-Gurjara (the equinoctial alignment of Modhera). The Dravida style of the south: a pyramidal vimana of progressively smaller storeys, towering gopuram gateways, exemplified at Brihadeshwara. And the Vesara or Karnata-Dravida style of the Deccan: a hybrid that the Chalukyas began at Pattadakal — where, uniquely, you can find both a Dravida-style Virupaksha temple and a Nagara-style Papanatha temple side by side, the same dynasty experimenting with both grammars simultaneously — and that the Hoysalas bring to its zenith at Halebid and Belur: soapstone carved with a density and intricacy that would require a jeweller's patience, the star-shaped plinth of Somnathpur the final flowering.

Each grammar is a regional answer to the same question: how do you build a house for the divine? The productive fragmentation of the era's politics produced three different answers, each internally coherent, each beautiful, none of which needed an imperial centre to validate it.

The Ghurid horizon

Mahmud of Ghazni was a raider. Muhammad of Ghor came to govern.

Between 1175 and 1192, Muhammad Ghori — Ghurid sultan from the Afghan highlands — conducted a methodical conquest of north India. In 1191, at the First Battle of Tarain near Thanesar, Prithviraja III Chauhan defeated him and drove him back. Muhammad returned in 1192 with a larger cavalry force and different tactics. At the Second Battle of Tarain, the Rajput confederacy was broken. Prithviraja was captured and killed. The gateway to the Ganga plains was open.

What followed was swift and, in one respect, irreversible. Qutbuddin Aibak, Muhammad Ghori's slave-commander, established control over Delhi and the surrounding territories. His commander Bakhtiyar Khalji swept through Bihar and Bengal. Around 1193, Bakhtiyar Khalji reached Nalanda. What happened next is recorded by the Tibetan historian Dharmasvamin, who visited a generation later and found only ruins. The library burned for months — so large that the monks could not save it. The monks were killed or scattered. Vikramashila fell soon after. Eight centuries of Indian Buddhist monastic learning — the institutional home of the Vajrayana tradition that the Palas had nurtured and that Nalanda had housed for eight hundred years — ended in a single campaign season.

By 1206, Qutbuddin Aibak had established the Slave Dynasty at Delhi, the first of the Delhi Sultanates. The political era we have been describing is formally over.

But notice what survives. The bhakti movements continue — Namdev in Maharashtra, Ramananda in north India, and within another century Kabir and Mirabai and Chaitanya, each drawing on the Tamil and Karnataka foundations this era built. The temple-building culture of the south continues for another two centuries: the Eastern Gangas commission the Jagannatha temple at Puri and the sun temple at Konark; the Hoysalas complete Somnathpur in 1268. And Sufism arrives with the Ghurids. Muinuddin Chishti reaches Ajmer around 1192, and the Chishti order he founds will spend the next two centuries developing the most intimate dialogue between Islamic mysticism and Hindu devotion that South Asian religious history has seen. The khanqah and the bhakti matha are going to have a long conversation. That conversation is Era 5's great theme.

The surprise here is this: the Ghurid conquest, which looks from the outside like an ending, is also a beginning. The political rupture is total in north India. The cultural continuity, running through bhakti and temple and Sufi-bhakti exchange, is equally total. The era does not so much end as transform.

What the era leaves us

The Pala libraries are ash. Nalanda is rubble. The tripartite struggle for Kanauj is over because no one cares about Kanauj anymore. And yet what this era built is, in the deepest sense, the substrate on which subsequent Indian civilisation runs.

It built three complete architectural vocabularies — Nagara, Dravida, Vesara — each capable of producing masterworks, each expressing a different regional civilisation's understanding of the sacred. It built a canon of devotional literature in Tamil, Kannada, Bengali, and Apabhramsha held not in libraries but in the memory of singing communities — which is why Bakhtiyar Khalji could burn Nalanda and not extinguish it. It built the philosophical triangle of Shankara, Ramanuja, and Madhva whose vertices define the range of Vedantic argument. It built Chola maritime power and the south Indian merchant guilds that connected the Coromandel coast to the Malay peninsula and Song China. And it built Bhakti as a mode of religious participation outside ritual and caste — a mode that will, in Era 5 and beyond, become the most widely distributed form of Hindu practice.

Outside the subcontinent, the same two centuries saw Medieval Europe building Gothic cathedrals and launching the Crusades (1095-1291), and the Mongol steppe assembling toward the proclamation of Genghis Khan in 1206 — coinciding, almost to the year, with Qutbuddin Aibak's foundation of the Slave Dynasty at Delhi. The Mongol disruption of Central Asia drove waves of Turkic refugees westward; among their eventual descendants are the Mughals. Era 6's story begins in that disruption.

What the era leaves Era 5 is a north India under a Persianate Islamic Sultanate, and a south and Deccan that remain under their own rulers for another century. The temple-Bhakti civilisation this era built continues beneath the Sultanate, not abolished but pressured and transformed, entering into conversation with the Sufi tradition that arrives with the conquerors. The productive fragmentation is not ended by the conquest. It surfaces in new forms.

The temples still stand. The empire fell; the temples outlasted it. Open the timeline now, and what you will find are the nodes — the dynasties, the monuments, the philosophers, the saints, the battles. What the chapter was trying to give you is the single thing that holds them together: the understanding that fragmentation, in this era, was not the problem. It was the point.
