10 — What the land does for a living

Everything in the previous nine chapters — the old Gondwanan shield, the mountains still rising, the rivers draining those mountains into plains, the soils those rivers deposited, the monsoon that organises the whole agricultural year — has been accumulating toward one question: what do humans actually do with it? The physical geography we have been building is not background. It is the active constraint on every economic choice a population can make. Where people can farm, what they can grow, where factories can viably locate, where services cluster — all of it is downstream of the substrate. This chapter follows that translation from physical inheritance into economic life.

The cleanest way to classify what people do is the framework proposed by the British economist Colin Clark in the 1940s. Primary activities extract directly from nature — farming, fishing, forestry, mining, herding. Secondary activities transform raw materials into products — manufacturing, construction, processing. Tertiary activities are services that neither extract nor transform but facilitate — trade, transport, banking, education, healthcare. Quaternary activities are knowledge-intensive services: research, IT, consultancy, information processing. And quinary activities sit at the apex of decision-making — top corporate leadership, senior government, the bodies that set direction for everything below. Every economy has all five. What distinguishes a developed economy from a developing one is the mix — the share of workforce and output that each level accounts for.

India's mix is unusual in a way worth pausing on. About 45 percent of India's workforce is still in primary activities, overwhelmingly agriculture. Yet agriculture contributes only around 15 percent of GDP. The services sector — tertiary and quaternary combined — contributes more than half of GDP while employing only about 30 percent of the workforce. This inversion — most workers in the lowest-productivity sector, most output from the highest — is the structural fact running beneath everything in this chapter. It is not a recent policy failure; it is an inheritance from the particular way India industrialised, compounded by the Green Revolution's success at food security without equivalent success at labour absorption into more productive work.

Before looking at what India specifically does with its land, it is worth knowing what options any society with land has. Subsistence farming — growing just enough for the household — comes in two broad forms. Intensive subsistence is dominant in monsoon Asia: small holdings, very high labour input per hectare, very high yield per hectare, wet paddy as the archetype. The terraced rice paddies of eastern India, the river deltas of West Bengal, the lowlands of Southeast Asia — this is humanity's most labour-dense use of land, and it has fed more people per unit area than almost any other system in history. Extensive subsistence works the other way: large areas, low labour density, land cleared, farmed briefly, then abandoned. Shifting cultivation — called jhum in northeast India, milpa in Central America, ladang in Indonesia — cycles through forest land on a rotation of several years. It functions at low population density; as that density rises and cycle times compress, the system degrades.

Commercial farming spans an enormous range. The intensive, specialised mixed farming of northwestern Europe — crops and livestock together on moderate holdings, heavy capital investment, crop rotation — is one extreme. The vast, thinly-manned grain belts of the semi-arid mid-latitudes are another: wheat prairies of North America, the Argentine pampas, the Australian interior, producing enormous volumes with minimal labour because mechanisation has substituted for hands. Plantation agriculture is something else again — the colonial legacy of large single-crop estates organised around export and cheap labour: tea in Assam and Sri Lanka, rubber in Malaysia, coffee in Brazil. India inherited this plantation overlay in its hillier peripheries, and it is still visible in the tea estates of Darjeeling, the Nilgiris, and the cardamom hills of Kerala. Mediterranean agriculture — citrus, olives, grapes in a climate of dry summers and mild wet winters — appears around the Mediterranean itself, in California, in Chile, but not in India. Indian agriculture is mostly intensive subsistence, with commercial belts in the wheat northwest and plantation pockets in the highland south — none of this placement accidental.

India's farming calendar is organised almost entirely around the southwest monsoon, which should surprise nobody given Chapter 5. The kharif season opens when the monsoon arrives, around June or July, and crops are harvested in September and October: rice, jowar, bajra, maize, cotton, sugarcane, jute, soybean, groundnut. The rabi season uses the soil moisture left after the monsoon withdraws, sown in October and November for a March-April harvest: wheat, barley, gram, peas, mustard, linseed. Between them is zaid, the short irrigated summer from March to June — watermelon, cucumber, vegetables, fodder — marginal in output but important for farmers who can access irrigation. The three-season structure is the monsoon translated directly into agronomy.

Now for the actual geography of what India grows — one of the heaviest PYQ areas, and worth the time. The spatial pattern is not arbitrary, and the examiner tests the reason as much as the name.

Rice occupies roughly a quarter of India's cropped area. It needs either heavy rainfall or reliable irrigation, and it grows best on alluvial or clayey moisture-retaining soils. The consequence is predictable: West Bengal is the largest producing state, managing three crops a year in the Brahmaputra delta and Gangetic Bengal — a wet-zone advantage no other state matches. Uttar Pradesh produces large volumes. Punjab produces less but exports far more through the MSP-procurement system. Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, and Bihar also feature. The eastern and southern wet zones are rice territory; the northwest is not, except where canal irrigation carries water to an otherwise unsuitable climate.

Wheat is the rabi counterpart, dominant in the Indo-Gangetic plain from Punjab through Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, into Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh. It needs a cool growing season, bright sunshine at ripening, and well-drained loamy soils — conditions the Punjab-Haryana canal-irrigated belt provides almost perfectly. Uttar Pradesh is the largest producer by volume; Punjab and Haryana achieve the highest yields per hectare. When the Green Revolution arrived, it arrived here — and the reasons for that geographic concentration will matter shortly.

Pulses — gram, tur, urad, moong — are the protein layer of the Indian diet, and India is both the world's largest producer and (paradoxically) the world's largest importer, because demand consistently outstrips what can be grown in the rainfed marginal-soil zones where pulses thrive. Madhya Pradesh is the largest producer; Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, and Karnataka follow. The structural protein gap this represents is real and persistent.

Cotton sits in the Deccan black-soil belt, and if you recall Chapter 7, the reason is already in your hands: regur, the black cotton soil, is the product of weathered Deccan basalt — moisture-retaining, mineral-rich, exactly suited to cotton's needs. Maharashtra, Gujarat, Telangana, and Karnataka are the heart of this belt. The textile industry that grew around it was not placed arbitrarily either.

Sugarcane needs heat, moisture, and rich loamy soils. Uttar Pradesh dominates by mill count and total output; Maharashtra leads in yield per hectare and has been gaining share because its longer crushing season is agronomically more efficient. Together they account for roughly two-thirds of national production. Sugarcane is bulky and perishable — it must be crushed within 24 hours of cutting — which is a textbook Weberian constraint: sugar mills cannot be far from cane fields, and they are not.

Jute is concentrated in the Brahmaputra delta and West Bengal's Gangetic delta, moisture-loving and labour-intensive. Tea — plantation crop proper — is almost entirely in Assam (over half of national output), Darjeeling (smaller volume, extraordinary market premium), and the Nilgiris of Tamil Nadu and Kerala. Coffee is geographically the most concentrated major crop: nearly all of it comes from the southern Western Ghats — Karnataka (especially Coorg), Kerala, and Tamil Nadu — where hill-slope microclimates and laterite soils suit the Arabica variety. Kerala holds over 90 percent of India's natural rubber production: the combination of heavy rainfall, laterite soils, and a plantation tradition laid down by the colonial economy and maintained since. Horticulture adds a final spatial layer: India is the world's largest producer of mango and banana, and the second-largest of vegetables and fruit overall; the Deccan, Karnataka, and coastal south are the primary fruit zones.

Of India's total geographic area, roughly 46 percent is under net sown area. Forests cover about 22 percent by legal classification. Fallow, degraded land, and non-agricultural uses account for the rest — with the last category growing fastest as urbanisation consumes peri-urban farmland. Cropping intensity — the ratio of gross cropped area to net sown area — stands around 140, meaning a given piece of cultivated land is cropped about 1.4 times per year. India's figures here lag both China and Bangladesh; the main lever for raising it is irrigation expansion.

The structural problem underneath the crop geography is landholding size. The average Indian farm is about 1.08 hectares, and 86 percent of India's farmers are small and marginal — under 2 hectares. This is not a recent failure; it is accumulated inheritance from partition of land across generations under Hindu joint-family norms, combined with weak policy incentives for consolidation. Small farms can be productive. They cannot easily absorb mechanisation, access credit, or survive a bad monsoon year.

The Green Revolution is the most consequential agrarian intervention in independent India and deserves a precise account. In the mid-1960s, Norman Borlaug's work in Mexico had produced semi-dwarf high-yielding wheat varieties with a shorter growing cycle and better response to fertiliser. Similar work on rice was underway at the International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines. The Indian agronomist M.S. Swaminathan adapted these varieties to Indian conditions and coordinated their rollout alongside a full package: chemical fertilisers, assured irrigation, extension services, and procurement guarantees at minimum support prices backed by the Food Corporation of India, established in 1965. India went from near-famine in 1965 to wheat self-sufficiency by the early 1970s. But the geography matters enormously: the Green Revolution concentrated in Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh, not in Bihar, Odisha, or the eastern plains. The reason is the same canal-irrigated inheritance — the Punjab-Haryana belt had the water infrastructure to support the technology package; the east did not. This geographic concentration is why Punjab and Haryana, with a fraction of India's farmers, capture a disproportionate share of the MSP-procurement system's benefits. The costs of the concentration are now visible: the Punjab water table is in sustained decline from decades of over-extraction for paddy, soil salinity is rising, and fertiliser-subsidy distortions have proved politically difficult to unwind. The Green Revolution's geography is one of India's most load-bearing inheritances, in both its gifts and its liabilities.

The White Revolution — Operation Flood, organised under Verghese Kurien and the cooperative dairy model centred on Anand in Gujarat — did something structurally different: it spread benefits broadly, connecting milk producers in small towns and villages to urban markets through a chilling-and-processing network. India became the world's largest milk producer. The Blue Revolution extended similar logic to fisheries — coastal aquaculture and inland tank fisheries — making India the second-largest fish producer globally. The Yellow Revolution in oilseeds was less successful: India still imports more than 60 percent of its edible oil, a dependency that sits unresolved.

Industry — the secondary sector — is where geography and economics intersect most visibly as a location problem. The German economist Alfred Weber, writing in 1909, set out the logic that still broadly holds: a manufacturer locates where total cost is minimised, balancing transport to raw materials, transport to markets, labour costs, and agglomeration economies — the cluster benefits of shared infrastructure, labour pools, and supply chains. Weight-losing industries (those where raw materials are heavier than final products — iron ore and coking coal into steel, for example) locate near raw materials. Market-oriented industries locate near consumers. Footloose industries are not strongly pulled by either, so they cluster around skilled labour and connectivity.

The world's major industrial regions grew from the nineteenth-century coalfields: the NE USA and Great Lakes belt (Pittsburgh, Chicago), the Ruhr of Germany, Lombardy in northern Italy, the Moscow-Volga axis in Russia, the Pacific belt of Japan from Tokyo-Yokohama through Osaka-Kobe. Globalisation has reshuffled this map since the 1980s: the Yangtze delta, the Pearl River delta, and the Bohai Bay region in China now form the world's densest manufacturing zone; the old Ruhr and NE USA rustbelts have contracted. The geography of global manufacturing follows labour costs and policy arbitrage as much as raw materials.

India's industrial geography organises around six recognisable regions, each explicable. The Mumbai-Pune cluster is the oldest: a colonial-era deep-water port, proximity to the Deccan cotton belt, early capital accumulation through colonial trade, a tradition of skilled urban labour — the result was cotton textile mills from the 1850s, then petrochemicals, automobiles, and financial services. Jamnagar in Gujarat hosts the Reliance refinery, the world's largest single-site oil-refining complex. The Hugli industrial region around Kolkata grew around jute (recall that West Bengal produces nearly three-quarters of India's raw jute) and colonial port trade, adding engineering and leather; post-partition and post-1947 policy shifts drained investment and the region has not recovered its earlier share — a reminder that industrial geography is not permanent.

The Bangalore-Chennai-Coimbatore belt has three distinct nodes. Chennai has attracted automobile assembly — Hyundai, Nissan-Renault, BMW, Daimler — because of its port, engineering labour force, and position as a logistics hub; it is sometimes called the Detroit of India. Coimbatore has textile and precision engineering manufacturing going back to the colonial period. Bengaluru stands apart: its industrial inheritance begins with the post-1947 public sector — HAL, ISRO, Indian Telephone Industries, IISc — which built up a pool of technically educated workers. When software-export liberalisation arrived in the late 1990s, Bengaluru had the exact inputs a footloose knowledge industry needed. IT concentrated here not because of coal or iron ore but because of education clusters and international airports. It now leads India's IT exports, followed by Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Noida, and Gurgaon, with secondary hubs in Kochi, Coimbatore, and Bhubaneswar. I find the Bengaluru story the clearest illustration of how post-independence policy choices, compounded over decades, can become geographic inheritance.

The Gujarat corridor — Ahmedabad, Vadodara, Surat, Jamnagar — connects cotton textile (historically the anchor), chemicals, petrochemicals, and refining. Surat is also one of the world's dominant diamond-cutting and polishing centres. The Chotanagpur belt — Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, parts of West Bengal — is Weber's logic most purely expressed: iron ore from Odisha and Chhattisgarh, coking coal from Jharkhand, located close enough together to minimise transport cost. Jamshedpur holds TISCO (Tata Iron and Steel Company), Asia's first integrated private steel plant, founded in 1907. Public-sector plants at Bhilai (Soviet collaboration, 1955), Rourkela (German), Durgapur (British), and Bokaro (Soviet) were added in the Nehru-era industrialisation drive. Visakhapatnam and Salem serve the coast and the south. The sixth recognisable region — Visakhapatnam-Guntur — adds steel, petroleum refining, and port-led logistics on the eastern coast.

Below the large-scale industrial geography lies an enormous dispersed layer: Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises — some 63 million units, contributing roughly 30 percent of manufacturing GDP, employing over 110 million people. Their geography is diffuse, reaching almost every district. Some clusters are specialised and well-known: Tirupur for knitwear, Surat for synthetic textiles and diamonds, Moradabad for brassware, Firozabad for glass, Jaipur for gems and jewellery, Ludhiana for hosiery and bicycles, Aligarh for locks. These clusters demonstrate agglomeration at a smaller scale than heavy industry, built from craft inheritance and accumulated skill rather than mineral access. The Khadi and Village Industries Commission maintains a further layer of village-scale production — handloom, handicraft, food processing — sustained as much for rural employment as for economic efficiency.

Services now account for more than half of India's GDP, but their geography is harder to map cleanly because services, unlike steel, are not anchored to mineral outcrops. Trade and retail reach everywhere, density tracking population and purchasing power. The IT quaternary sector's geography has already been described. Banking and finance are disproportionately concentrated in Mumbai — the Reserve Bank, the Bombay Stock Exchange, the National Stock Exchange, and the headquarters of most major banks sit within a few kilometres of each other. The quinary layer of national governance concentrates in Delhi. The activities described in this chapter all generate flows — of crops, of steel, of software, of people moving for work. The next chapter follows those flows: how distance gets conquered.
