What you're standing on (and why it's here at all)

If you stand barefoot anywhere in peninsular India — at Kanyakumari with the three seas in front of you, or on the Deccan Plateau somewhere outside Pune, or on a granite outcrop in Karnataka — the rock under your feet is, in many places, between two and three billion years old. That is not a figure that quite goes into a normal sentence. The continental crust beneath the Indian peninsula is among the oldest exposed rock on the planet's surface. It was already old when the first multicellular life appeared. It was already old when the supercontinent of Pangaea assembled, broke up, and reassembled the world map several times over. The peninsula is, in a real sense, the oldest unit of land you will study in this whole syllabus.

Now turn your face north and walk, in your imagination, until you reach the Himalayan front — somewhere in Uttarakhand or Himachal Pradesh. The rock under your feet there is, by contrast, almost embarrassingly young. The Himalayas as a mountain range began rising only about 50 million years ago, which is — on the geological clock — yesterday afternoon. They are still rising, by something like 5 millimetres a year. Two pieces of land. Same country. One of them old enough that the figure stops meaning anything; the other so young that, if our clock measured in geological time, the paint would not yet be dry.

This is the first thing I want you to feel before any vocabulary lands: the country you are studying is geological assemblage, not single object. The peninsula was assembled at one set of times, on one set of physics; the Himalayas were assembled much later, on a quite different set of physics; the Indo-Gangetic plains between them were laid down by rivers cutting the Himalayas down and dropping the silt in between. Three pieces of inheritance from three different points of deep time. Everything else in the geography syllabus — the rivers, the soils, the climate, the vegetation, the population distribution, the agriculture, the industry — sits on top of that assemblage and is shaped by it. So we have to begin where the assemblage begins, which is with the planet itself.

The current best account of how the planet itself got here goes as follows. About 13.7 billion years ago, all the matter in the universe was concentrated into something so dense it cannot really be described in normal language; that something began to expand, and it has not stopped. (We know this because the universe is still measurably expanding now, and because the radiation left over from the early hot phase can still be detected as a faint warmth coming from every direction in space.) Within that expanding universe, clouds of gas and dust — nebulae — collected under their own gravity. About 4.6 billion years ago, one such nebula in our corner of the Milky Way began to contract. Most of its mass collapsed into the centre and became hot enough to ignite hydrogen fusion: that is the Sun. The leftover material flattened into a rotating disc around the Sun, and within that disc, particles stuck together, then chunks stuck together, then the chunks stuck together into worlds. The four innermost worlds — Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars — formed close enough to the Sun that the heat boiled off most of their lighter material; what was left was rock and metal. The four outer worlds — Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune — formed far enough out that the lighter gases stuck around; they grew into the giants. Earth is the third rock from the Sun, and it sits, almost embarrassingly precisely, in the narrow band of distance where water can stay liquid for a long time. That is the first inheritance.

The early Earth was hot and homogeneous — a roughly uniform ball of accreted rock and metal, kept molten by the heat of impacts and by radioactive decay. Inside that molten ball, density did its work. The heavy stuff — iron, nickel — sank inwards. The lighter silicates floated up. This is called differentiation, and it is responsible for the layered Earth you will spend the rest of this chapter learning the names of. Sometime around 4.5 billion years ago, while this differentiation was still underway, a Mars-sized body collided with the proto-Earth; the debris from that collision accreted into the Moon. (One thing this collision did was set Earth tilting at about 23.5 degrees on its axis, which is what gives us seasons; remember that in Chapter 5 when we get to climate.) Once Earth cooled enough, the gases that had been trapped inside the molten interior began escaping through volcanic vents — this is degassing — and they made the first proper atmosphere, mostly water vapour and carbon dioxide. When the planet cooled further, the water vapour condensed out and rained, for what we think was a very long time, and the oceans filled. About 3.8 billion years ago, in those oceans, life began. Two and a half billion years ago, photosynthetic microbes — cyanobacteria — started releasing oxygen, and the atmosphere we now breathe slowly assembled. Every step in that sequence is part of what India inherits.

Now, what is the layered structure that all of this differentiation produced? If you sliced Earth open like a fruit, you would find three concentric layers. Outermost is the crust — the thin solid skin you walk on. Under continents the crust averages 30 to 35 kilometres thick (under big mountain ranges, it bulges down to 70 kilometres); under oceans, it is only 5 to 7 kilometres thick. The continental crust is dominated by silica and aluminium — geologists call it sial — and is mostly granite-like. The oceanic crust is dominated by silica and magnesium — sima — and is mostly basalt-like. Sima is denser than sial; this matters more than it sounds. Below the crust, down to about 2,900 kilometres, is the mantle: silicate rock rich in iron and magnesium, mostly solid but partially molten in its upper reaches. Below the mantle, down to the centre at 6,371 kilometres, is the core: an outer core of liquid iron and nickel, then an inner core of solid iron and nickel. The inner core is solid not because it is cool — temperatures down there are around 5,000 to 6,000 degrees Celsius — but because the pressure is so colossal that the metal cannot melt. That swirling liquid outer core is also, incidentally, what generates Earth's magnetic field through a dynamo effect, and that magnetic field is what shields the surface from lethal solar radiation. We owe the breathable surface of the planet to the fact that there is liquid metal sloshing 3,000 kilometres beneath our feet.

A reasonable question is: how do we know any of this? Nobody has ever drilled deeper than about 12 kilometres. The answer is that we have a remote-sensing tool we did not build — we have earthquakes. When an earthquake happens, it sends two main kinds of waves through the planet: P-waves, which are compressional and can travel through any medium (solid, liquid, gas), and S-waves, which are shear waves and cannot travel through liquids at all. By recording these waves at seismograph stations around the world, we can map what they hit on the way through. There is a region on the far side of Earth from any earthquake — between about 104 and 140 degrees of arc from the source — where almost no waves arrive directly. This is called the shadow zone, and the fact that S-waves stop completely at one boundary inside the planet is how we know the outer core is liquid. The boundary between crust and mantle (called the Moho, after Mohorovicic, who found it in 1909), the boundary between mantle and core (the Gutenberg discontinuity), the boundary between liquid outer core and solid inner core (the Lehmann discontinuity) — all of them were discovered by reading seismic waves. The Earth's interior is, in a real sense, written in earthquakes.

There is one more conceptual layer to add before we can talk about India. Take the crust and the very top of the mantle together — the rigid skin and the rigid uppermost mantle, totalling about 100 kilometres thick — and call that the lithosphere. Below the lithosphere, in the upper mantle, lies a zone called the asthenosphere, somewhere between 100 and 410 kilometres deep. The asthenosphere is partially molten, weak, plastic — it can flow, very slowly, the way warm tar flows. Heat from the deep interior drives slow circulating convection currents in this layer: hot mantle material rises in some places, moves laterally beneath the lithosphere, cools, and sinks back down somewhere else. The lithosphere is broken into a small number of large pieces — tectonic plates — that float on top of the asthenosphere and are dragged around by these convection currents. There are seven major plates: Pacific (the largest), North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian, and Antarctic, plus a handful of smaller ones. Where two plates meet, you get geology. Where they pull apart (a divergent boundary), magma rises through the gap and makes new ocean floor; this is happening down the middle of the Atlantic right now along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and it is also tearing East Africa open along the East African Rift Valley. Where they push together (a convergent boundary), one of three things happens: an oceanic plate dives under another plate (this is subduction and gives you trenches and volcanic island arcs, as around the Pacific in the Ring of Fire); or two oceanic plates collide and crumple; or two continental plates collide and crumple upward into mountains. Where they slide past each other sideways (a transform boundary) you get earthquakes but no new landform — California's San Andreas Fault is the textbook example.

This whole picture — that the surface of Earth is a small number of rigid plates drifting on a slow-flowing mantle — is the theory of plate tectonics, and it is the single most important idea in geography. It was not the first attempt. In 1912, the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener noticed that the coastlines of South America and Africa look as though they were once joined, and that identical fossils of plants and animals appeared on continents now separated by oceans, and that geological formations matched across those same gaps. He proposed that all the present-day continents had once been joined as a single supercontinent, Pangaea ("all earth"), surrounded by a single ocean, Panthalassa, and that they had drifted apart. He was right. But he could not say what physical force could possibly drag a continent through ocean floor, and so for half a century mainstream geology rejected the theory. It took Arthur Holmes in the 1930s to suggest convection currents, and Harry Hess in the 1960s to discover that the floor of the Atlantic was actually being newly created down its central ridge and pushed outwards in symmetrical bands (this is called sea floor spreading, and it was confirmed by reading the magnetic stripes in the rocks on either side of the ridge), before continental drift was finally taken seriously and absorbed into the more general theory we now call plate tectonics. Wegener's intuition was vindicated thirty years after his death.

Now we can finally talk about how India arrived. About 250 million years ago, when Pangaea began breaking up, it first split into two: Laurasia in the north (which would become North America, Europe, and Asia) and Gondwana in the south (which would become South America, Africa, India, Australia, and Antarctica). Between them was a long warm shallow sea called the Tethys. India, at this point, was nestled against the eastern coast of Africa, somewhere down near where Antarctica is today. Around 140 million years ago, India began to detach from Gondwana and drift northward, alone, across what is now the Indian Ocean. It did this for roughly 90 million years, at unusually high speed for a continent — at times perhaps 15 centimetres a year. (Most plates move at the speed your fingernails grow. India was sprinting.) As it moved north, the Tethys Sea narrowed in front of it. The sediments that had been accumulating on the Tethys floor for hundreds of millions of years were caught between the advancing Indian plate and the stationary Eurasian plate to the north. About 50 million years ago, the two plates met. There was nowhere for the Tethys sediments to go but up. They were compressed, folded, and lifted into the air, and they are still being lifted today: that mass of folded sea-floor sediment is the Himalayas. If you find a marine fossil at 5,000 metres altitude in Ladakh, it is not a mistake; it is a piece of the floor of an ocean that no longer exists.

The Indian plate has not stopped. It is still pushing northward into Eurasia at about 5 centimetres a year, which is why the Himalayas are still gaining height, and why the Hindu Kush, the Karakoram, and the Pamir knot at the western end of the range continue to suffer earthquakes, and why northern India and Nepal sit in one of the most seismically active zones on Earth. Meanwhile, the southern part of the Indian plate — the bit that broke off Gondwana — is what we now call the peninsular plateau, and that part is some of the oldest continental crust on the planet, a fragment of Gondwanan shield rock that has been quietly riding north on the back of a moving plate for 140 million years. The Western Ghats and the Eastern Ghats are old, weathered uplands of this same Gondwana fragment. The Deccan Plateau, by contrast, is a younger feature laid on top: vast outpourings of basaltic lava that erupted about 66 million years ago from a series of fissures in what is now western India, just as the K-Pg extinction was wiping out the dinosaurs. (There is a serious scientific argument that those eruptions contributed to that extinction.) That basalt cooled into the dark fertile Deccan trap soils, on which much of central and western India farms cotton today. Geology is never not present.

This, then, is what India is: an old Gondwanan shield, riding on a moving plate, that ploughed into Asia and is still ploughing, throwing up the world's tallest mountains in front of it; with a younger basaltic plateau poured over its western half by deep volcanic activity; and with a long alluvial plain in between, still being filled by the rivers that drain the rising mountains down. Everything else — the climate that the mountains create by blocking winds, the rivers those mountains feed, the soils those rivers deposit, the crops those soils support, the populations those crops feed, the cities and industries those populations build — is downstream of this. Literally downstream, in the case of the Ganges. The geography of India is not just things that happen to be in India. It is the lawful consequence of what the planet did here, over deep time, and is still doing.
